# TM-035-01 — Procyclical leverage and value-at-risk: reasoning, not testable as a theory of its own

## The theory, and the forms its sources give it

TM-035 is Adrian and Shin's (2014) procyclical leverage: under value-at-risk constraints,
intermediaries take more leverage when measured volatility is low and must shrink their balance
sheets when it rises, often after an adverse move, selling into the fall; trend and momentum would
then be an emergent property of widely shared risk-management rules — volatility targets and
value-at-risk limits — applied with a delay that makes deleveraging and releveraging persist. Its
refutations: intermediaries' leverage that does not vary with measured volatility, or rises with it;
deleveraging after adverse moves that leaves no trace in subsequent price moves; changes in
value-at-risk or volatility-target constraints that do not affect institutional exposures. The bank
names stocks, equity indices, bonds, credit, commodities and currencies, and as data daily prices,
realised volatility and intermediary leverage.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library.** The library holds Adrian and Shin only in
others' reports and bibliographies.

- **Ilmanen** (*Expected Returns*, 2011, chapter 20, "Endogenous return and risk: Feedback effects
  on expected returns", §20.1, PDF pp. 585–586): among the mechanisms that boost procyclicality,
  leverage, loss and margin spirals, in which losses make intermediaries shrink their balance sheets
  and margin calls and redemptions make levered investors cut positions, and risk-control spirals,
  in which value-at-risk management, stop-losses, portfolio insurance and mark-to-market accounting
  cut risk when prices fall and measured volatility rises; higher volatility pushes haircuts up and
  funding down. Beside them he lists time-varying risk premia: falling wealth and rising volatility
  raise required returns, so that prices must fall now — a sign for higher expected returns after
  the fall, not for its continuation. Mechanisms, with no rule measured.
- **Ilmanen** (*Expected Returns*, 2011, section 8.6, PDF p. 237): the growth of broker-dealers'
  balance-sheet leverage has a correlation of −0.20 with the S&P 500's next-quarter excess return,
  −0.35 since the mid-1980s; after Adrian, Moench and Shin (2010), aggressive levering signals low
  prospective returns and deleveraging good subsequent returns — a sign on the theory's own
  variable, a reversal, on leverage the lab does not hold. **Campbell** (*Financial Decisions and
  Markets*, 2018, section 12.3, pp. 399–400) reports Adrian, Etula and Muir's broker-dealer leverage
  factor pricing equity and bond portfolios, on the same data.
- **Brunnermeier and Pedersen**, "Market Liquidity and Funding Liquidity", in Amihud, Mendelson and
  Pedersen's *Market Liquidity* (2012, chapter 6, summary p. 196, the article from p. 199), and held
  on its own in the library: margin and loss spirals in which a funding shock lowers market
  liquidity, which raises margins and tightens funding further; margins that escalated after Black
  Monday and in 2007 on the S&P futures; Adrian and Shin (2009) cited as evidence that the margin
  spiral forces traders to de-lever in downturns; volatility and capital as the state variables of
  risk premiums, high when capital is low. A model of funding and liquidity, with margins the lab
  does not hold.
- **Chevallier and Ielpo** (*The Economics of Commodity Markets*, 2013, chapter 2, §2.1.1, p. 78):
  reporting Daskalaki and others (2012), broker-dealers' leverage tested as a state variable for
  commodity futures (Adrian, Etula and Muir), in models none of which succeeded in pricing the
  cross-section of commodity futures.

## What the lab can read of it, and where each form already stands

The lab holds the daily closes and traded volumes of funds and of bitcoin, and the Treasury bill's
yield; it holds no intermediaries' balance sheets or leverage, no margins and no value-at-risk
limits. On those data:

- **Leverage moving with measured volatility, and constraints moving exposures**: intermediaries'
  leverage and exposures, which the lab does not hold; the margin and liquidity spirals are
  **FP-006**'s claim, forced sales **FP-005**'s, the risk-control selling of stop-losses, portfolio
  insurance and volatility overlays **FP-024**'s, all untouched. The library's sign on the theory's
  own variable, dealers' leverage, is a reversal (Ilmanen, §8.6), on data the lab does not hold.
- **Deleveraging after adverse moves leaving a trace in later prices**: on prices alone, a fall
  followed by rising volatility continuing into further falls. The trend itself is read by
  TM-017-01 (TM-017 in progress); the trend held in crises only, by TM-018-01, whose rule held after
  crisis months lost about 1.2% a year of alpha against TM-017-01's held always, refuted in that
  narrow form; momentum switched off when its group's market is down and its volatility high, by
  TM-024-01, not proven and not refuted. The library signs returns after a rise in volatility only
  weakly: single-digit correlations (Ilmanen, 2011, §8.6, PDF p. 239), the one component leaning
  toward continuation being single stocks' average variance, which the lab does not hold, while
  correlation among equities leans toward reversal (Pollet and Wilson, there); over days to weeks,
  short-term reversals stronger amid high volatility (Ilmanen, as TM-011-01 read him), **MR-001**'s
  claim; over longer horizons, Ilmanen's time-varying risk premium, higher returns after the fall.
  Greyserman and Kaminski (*Trend Following with Managed Futures*, 2014, chapter 4, table 4.1 and
  "Equity Market Crisis and Crisis Alpha") name losses, rising volatility and rising correlation as
  the triggers of institutional drawdown, leverage and value-at-risk limits, and persistent trends
  across markets as their result: crisis alpha, **TM-018**'s, read by TM-018-01.
- **Flight to quality in deleveraging**: liquid, high-quality assets outperforming risky ones while
  funding contracts, then the reverse once it eases, is **FP-006**'s claim, after Brunnermeier and
  Pedersen's Proposition 6(iv); bonds rallying as prolonged trends in crises is **TM-018**'s, read
  by TM-018-01, whose seven funds held IEF and TLT.
- **Commodity futures priced by dealers' leverage**: the leverage, which the lab does not hold, and
  models that failed in their source.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue a card of TM-035's own:**

- **A trend held only after volatility has risen on a fall**: the state is TM-024-01's panic state
  on its groups' markets and TM-018-01's crisis state on SPY, both published; a card on a close
  variant would be chosen from those results, and the library gives the continuation after a
  volatility rise no sign the lab can read.
- **Exposure cut as volatility rises, the volatility targeting TM-035 names as a cause**: asset by
  asset on TM-017-01's trend, it is TM-047-01's, not proven, whose verdict found the timing added
  almost nothing on these funds (an appraisal ratio of 0.02 against the same rule at fixed weights).
  Scaled as a whole portfolio by its own recent volatility and capped at full investment — Moreira
  and Muir's volatility-managed portfolio — the library signs it: Ang (*Asset Management*, 2014,
  chapter 8, pp. 267–269) finds equities and bills timed by the VIX, which the lab does not hold —
  its realised volatility is the proxy, volatility being forecast well from its own past (same
  section) — ending 1986–2011 at 3.06 against 2.14 for the static mix, and Ilmanen (*Investing Amid
  Low Expected Returns*, 2022, Box 13.1, pp. 216–217) finds volatility targeting may raise the
  Sharpe ratio, empirically "especially for the equity market premium". Both read the gain as
  volatility that can be forecast while expected returns do not rise with it, which needs no
  deleveraging that reinforces the fall and cannot tell TM-035 from its absence. The form is
  **TM-047**'s, whose prediction names risk-premium strategies scaled by volatility after Moreira
  and Muir, and which TM-047-01's verdict records as the form it left untested: claimed, and read by
  no verdict. TM-047 is reopened for it (TM-047-02), so that the form does not fall between a closed
  theory and one that cannot tell itself apart by it.
- **Returns after volatility spikes** (Ilmanen's risk premium): a reversal after the fall, which is
  not TM-035's claim; its reversal forms are the mean-reversion family's.
- **Other readings in the library**: Pedersen (*Efficiently Inefficient*, 2015) on the fire-sale
  path, a fall until the deleveraging ends then a rebound (chapter 5, p. 82), FP-005's and FP-006's,
  and on risk-management selling in down markets as a cause of trends (chapter 12, p. 212),
  TM-017's, read by TM-017-01; Ilmanen (2022, p. 158) counting some volatility targeters among
  extrapolative players, with no rule; risk parity (Ang, chapter 3), whose gain comes from the bond
  overweight TM-047-01 found too; and leverage constraints and low-risk assets (Frazzini and
  Pedersen), another theory than procyclical leverage.

What would make it testable as its own: intermediaries' leverage or exposures, or margins, frozen in

## Status

TM-035 is recorded `not-testable` as a theory of its own: its refutations read intermediaries'
leverage, exposures and constraints, which the lab does not hold, and their mechanisms are FP-005's,
FP-006's and FP-024's; the library's sign on dealers' leverage is a reversal, on data the lab does
not hold; on prices alone, its trend is TM-017-01's, its crisis and panic states TM-018-01's and
TM-024-01's, read; its trend scaled by volatility TM-047-01's, read, and its volatility-managed
portfolio TM-047's, claimed and not yet read, for which TM-047 is reopened; the library signs
returns after a rise in volatility only weakly, and its clearer signs are reversals, MR-001's over
weeks and the risk premium's beyond. No card is drawn, and no trial is spent.
