# AS-014-01 — Barriers to entry: reasoning, not testable as a theory of its own

## The theory, and the forms its sources give it

AS-014 holds that incumbents in industries structurally hard to enter show more durable profits,
more stable returns on capital and prices less sensitive to immediate competition than firms in open
industries, the differences lasting between firms and sub-universes as long as the barriers hold,
and vanishing when a technological or regulatory change removes them. Its refutations: protected
firms earning profits and returns on capital no more durable or stable than open industries';
protected incumbents' prices as sensitive to new competitors as unprotected firms'; incumbents'
profitability unchanged after a barrier falls. The bank names stocks and sectors; its references are
Greenwald and Kahn's *Competition Demystified* (2005) and Porter's *Competitive Strategy* (1980).

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library.**

- **Greenwald and Kahn** (*Competition Demystified*, 2005): barriers to entry and competitive
  advantages as the same thing, the only lasting source of returns on capital above its cost,
  industries without them seeing their profits competed away (chapter 1); the advantages of supply,
  of demand through captive customers, and of economies of scale combined with captivity, beside
  those granted by government — licences, tariffs and quotas, authorised monopolies, patents,
  subsidies and regulation (chapter 2); and two tests of whether an industry has them: incumbents'
  market shares stable, and their profitability exceptional over a long period (chapter 4). The
  falls of barriers the book dates — airline deregulation in 1978 (chapter 12) and the expiry of the
  lead-additive patent in 1948 (chapter 15) — come before the lab's years; the airlines' is signed
  on profits and on owners' aggregate returns, the additive industry's on its pricing; its account
  of Kodak's entry into instant photography (chapter 13) is of a barrier that held, Kodak losing
  Polaroid's patent suit in 1985 and leaving the business in 1986.
- **Porter** (*Competitive Strategy*, 1980, chapter 1, "The Structural Analysis of Industries"):
  economies of scale, product differentiation, capital requirements, switching costs, access to
  distribution, cost advantages independent of scale and government policy as the sources of
  barriers, which change as their conditions do — the expiry of Polaroid's basic patents lowering
  its barrier and letting Kodak enter; figure 1-2, "Barriers and Profitability", high entry barriers
  with low exit barriers giving high and stable returns, high exit barriers risky ones; and
  concentration rising with the height of the barriers (chapter 8, "Industry Evolution").
- **Koller and others** (*Valuation*, eighth edition, chapter 8): industries ranked by their median
  return on invested capital, 1995 to 2023 — pharmaceuticals and branded consumer goods, relying on
  patents and brands, high; oil and gas, mining and utilities low — the rankings changing little
  over time.
- **Hou and Robinson** (2006, reported in Ziemba, *Calendar Anomalies and Arbitrage*, section 1.12,
  "Industry Concentration"): from 1963 to 2001, firms in the least concentrated industries, by the
  Herfindahl index of three-digit SIC industries, earning about 4% a year more than firms in the
  most concentrated — a premium the authors read as the risk of innovation and distress. It is the
  one return sign in the library on the structure the barriers build, and its sign runs against the
  protected industries.

Greenwald and Kahn, Porter and Koller sign profits and returns on capital, not the returns of the
stocks or of the sectors that hold them; Hou and Robinson sign returns, on industries ranked by
their concentration.

## What the lab can read of it

The lab holds the daily bars and volumes of twenty-two funds and bitcoin — the funds from 2005 or
their later start, bitcoin from September 2014 — adjusted for dividends and splits, which encode the
funds' distributions, though no strategy reads them until a point-in-time reading with its own test
is built; the Treasury bill's yield from 2005 and two franc exchange rates; the funds' prices, never
their holdings' prices; and no single stock, profits, returns on capital, market shares,
concentration or entry event; its snapshot is closed, an asset or a series being added only before
the first card. Nothing was computed from the bars for this judgement. On those data:

- **Durable profits and stable returns on capital**, the claim and first refutation: accounting
  series of firms and industries, not held; a sector fund's price return is not its members' return
  on capital, and Koller's and Porter's rankings of industries by profitability sign no later
  return.
- **Prices less sensitive to new competitors**, the second: entry events, not held and not dated in
  the library for the lab's years.
- **A barrier's fall and incumbents' profitability**, the third: firms' profits around regulatory or
  technological changes, not held; every fall the library dates precedes 2005.
- **Concentration and returns** (Hou and Robinson): the form is this theory's own — no bank theory
  holds it — and it is unreadable: the lab holds no concentration of the funds' industries, the
  library gives none for the eleven sector funds or for 2005 to 2025, and eleven sectors are far
  coarser than three-digit industries. A ranking of the sectors fixed from outside the bars would
  change the holding only at the first holding and at the entries of the real-estate fund (September
  2016) and the communication fund (June 2018): at most three decisions against gate 1's thirty,
  certain to fail it whatever the prices, so no card is drawn for it (RUNBOOK step 9).
- **The return forms of durable advantage**: the quality and franchise value of single stocks are
  **EF-012**'s (quality minus junk) and **EF-004**'s (franchise value); the persistence and fade of
  profitability **EF-015**'s and **MR-029**'s — all on single stocks.
- **Bitcoin**: the crypto books in the library speak of barriers to entry as regulation, access
  costs, the low cost of launching a new coin or a protocol's liquidity moat, and sign no return on
  any of them.

**The bank's siblings.** AS-014 is the umbrella of the family: **AS-015**, switching costs;
**AS-016**, economies of scale; **AS-017**, network effects; **AS-018**, the experience curve;
**AS-019**, vertical integration — each one source of a barrier — and **AS-020**, two-sided
platforms; the concentration form is AS-014's alone, and its count above holds for any fixed ranking
of the sector funds a sibling might propose. Beside them, **EF-004**, franchise value; **EF-012**,
quality minus junk; **EF-015**, persistence of returns on capital; **MR-029**, profitability fade;
**CA-011**, sector rotation, and **CA-013**, business-cycle sector rotation, which read the sector
funds on time-varying signals; all untouched; **CA-012**, sector rotation on monetary conditions,
recorded not testable.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue a card:** the sector funds sorted by their
barriers — utilities regulated, health care with patents, technology with networks and scale — as a
static tilt: Koller puts utilities among the low earners and ranks profitability, not later returns;
the one return sign on the barriers' structure, Hou and Robinson's, needs a concentration series the
lab does not hold; O'Shaughnessy's ranking of the ten sectors by their compound returns from 1967 to
2009 (*What Works on Wall Street*, fourth edition, 2011, chapter 24) — consumer staples first, which
he explains after the fact by wide moats and monopoly or brand power, information technology last —
measures no barrier, and was found only after this judgement was first recorded; and any fixed
ranking makes at most three decisions, certain to fail gate 1.

What would make it testable: the concentration of the funds' industries over the lab's years, or
dated changes of barriers within them, with firms' or industries' profits — outside the lab's data.

## Status

AS-014 is recorded `not-testable`: its claim and every refutation read firms' and industries'
profits, returns on capital and entry events, which the lab does not hold; its sources' rankings of
industries sign profitability, not returns; its one return form, Hou and Robinson's concentration
premium, needs a concentration series the lab does not hold, and any fixed ranking of the sector
funds makes at most three decisions, certain to fail gate 1; the return forms of durable advantage
are EF-012's and EF-004's on single stocks. No card is drawn, and no trial is spent.
