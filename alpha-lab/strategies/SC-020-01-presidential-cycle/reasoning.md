# SC-020-01 — The presidential election cycle: reasoning, not testable

## The theory, and the form its sources give it

SC-020 holds that US equity returns vary in a recurring way over the four-year presidential term:
higher in its second half, the third and fourth years, than in its first two, through a political
business cycle — incumbents supporting activity and expectations before the next election — or a
public mood acting on the taste for risk; that returns differ by the party in power, small and
large capitalisations behaving differently under each; that they differ when Congress sits and when
it does not; and it names post-election effects. Its refutations: no difference between the term's
two halves; late-term strength unrelated to pre-election fiscal or monetary support; no difference
by party or by Congress's sessions; a cycle that does not recur out of sample.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library's books.**

- **The term's halves.** Huang (1985), on 1832 to 1979, higher returns in the last two years of
  terms than in the first two; Herbst and Slinkman (1984), a 48-month cycle peaking in November of
  election years, 1926 to 1977; Stovall (1992) and Hensel and Ziemba (1995, 2000), low returns in
  the first two years and higher ones in the last two. Hensel and Ziemba's table, from 1929 to 1997
  (which the text also calls 1929 to 1992): small stocks 24.65% a year in the last two years against
  15.90% in the first two under Democrats, 10.18% against −6.29% under Republicans; large stocks
  17.40% against 8.09% and 9.06% against 3.77%, both significantly higher in the second half
  (Dzhabarov and Ziemba, in Ziemba's *Calendar Anomalies and Arbitrage*, 2012, §1.9, and the Zacks
  *Handbook of Equity Market Anomalies*, 2011, chapter 9). Their update from 1998 to 2010, which
  overlaps the lab's first six years, reverses the halves under Democrats — large stocks 23.49% a
  year in the first two years against 5.97% in the last two, small stocks 17.16% against 9.12% —
  and keeps them under Republicans, −3.32% against 2.02% for large stocks. Ilmanen (*Expected
  Returns*, 2011, §25.3): the pattern "has partly broken down".
- **The months after an election.** Hensel and Ziemba, in Keim and Ziemba's *Security Market
  Imperfections in Worldwide Equity Markets* (2000, part II, chapter 10), on 1928 to 1997: over the
  election month and the next thirteen months, large and small stocks earned more than their
  average in November of the election year and in March, April, May and July of the next, and "lower
  than average or average" in the other months; over the fourteen months, 2.0 points a year below
  average for large stocks and 5.5 for small, the latter "not statistically significant"; from 1998
  to 2010, 1.1 points below for large stocks and 4.0 above for small. Bonds earned less than their
  average over the same fourteen months, cash more.
- **The cycle's second year.** Foerster and Schmitz (1995), in Athanassakos and Foerster's chapter
  of the same volume, with the cycle's years running from 1 November of the election year: US
  capital gains of 4.3%, −2.8%, 21.6% and 8.8% a year in the cycle's four years from 1957 to 1994,
  the same shape in Canada, where a second-year dummy stays significant in a regression on Canadian
  returns beside the usual predictors, and a switching rule out of Canadian equities in the second
  year earned 11.4% a year against 7.3% from 1961 to 1994.
- **Wong and McAleer** (2008), as the Zacks handbook reports them, on 1965 to 2003: a low in the
  second year and a peak in the third or fourth, stronger under Republicans, absent from the second
  terms of Reagan and Clinton, who faced no re-election.
- **The Almanac**, as Ziemba reports it: the cycle's "sweet spot" from the fourth quarter of the
  midterm year to the first quarter of the pre-election year; no falling third year since 1939; the
  best years under a Democratic president with a Republican Congress.
- **The party.** Hensel and Ziemba: small stocks 20.15% a year under Democrats against 1.94% under
  Republicans from 1929 to 1997, the difference no longer significant once 1929 to 1936 are
  removed; large stocks statistically the same under both; small stocks' advantage under Democrats
  spread over the months from April to December, month by month in their table; a strategy of small
  caps under Democrats and large caps under Republicans, before costs. Riley and Luksetich, and
  Hobbs and Riley, as the books report them: short-term gains after Republican victories and losses
  after Democratic ones, 1900 to 1980.
- **Congress.** Ferguson and Witte (2006), as the books report them: a difference of 4 to 11 basis
  points a day between sessions and recesses, 3 to 6 after controls for other calendar effects, 3.3
  to 6.5% a year, most capital gains made out of session, tied to the public's opinion of Congress;
  Ziemba's text first puts the higher returns in session, then out of it, and the Zacks handbook out
  of it.

Santa-Clara and Valkanov (2003), Kräussl, Lucas and Siegmann (2014) and Allvine and O'Neill (1980),
which the bank names, are not in the library.

## Why the lab cannot test it

**Every form read on the calendar of the term fails gate 1 by construction.** Gate 1 needs at least
thirty clustered decisions, changes of the targets, over the in-sample years (the lab's framing,
before any card); the lab's in-sample years, 2005 to 2022, hold four and a half terms. A rule whose
holdings follow the calendar of the term changes them on dates fixed in advance, so its count of
decisions is known before any price is read:

- the term's halves, out in the first two years and in the last two: eight decisions, 2005 being a
  first year, nine when the weak years hold something other than cash;
- the cycle's second year alone, on Foerster and Schmitz's November-to-October years: eleven;
- the Almanac's sweet spot, two quarters a term: about as many as the halves;
- the months after an election, the funds held in November and in March, April, May and July of the
  next year, cash in the fourteen months' other months, and the funds held in the term's other
  months, the finest form with a sign in the library: twenty-nine, counted on the calendar, and
  twenty-eight combined with the halves.

None reaches thirty, whatever the funds did. Herbst and Slinkman's 48-month cycle is given only by
its November peak; no other month-by-month profile of the term is in the library.

**Why no card is drawn, when SC-019-01 was.** SC-019-01's barometer was expected to fail gate 1, not
certain to: its count of decisions depended on how many of the funds' Januarys fell, and the card
put a chance of 1 to 31% on reaching thirty, so the battery could still have judged it. Here the
count is fixed by the calendar and falls short with certainty, as SC-011-01's one-day rule failed
gate 6's check of a day's delay by construction. A card certain to fail a gate buys only its clause,
a comparison of eighteen years over four and a half terms, and adds a trial to gate 4's count for
every card that follows; the lab does not spend a trial on it. The claim's unit is also the term:
eight strong years against ten weak ones in four and a half terms, two of them second terms (2005–08
and 2013–16) in which, by Wong and McAleer, the incumbent had no election to court.

**The party.** Which party holds the presidency is not in the lab's data and follows no rule the
code could compute; written into a card or a strategy by hand, it would be data with no hash, which
the rule of a frozen snapshot exists to prevent (SC-011-01). Its count is not the wall: read year
by year it changes hands three times in-sample (2009, 2017, 2021), four decisions, but a monthly
rotation between IWM and SPY on Hensel and Ziemba's month-by-month table by party would reach far
more. The same wall holds for incumbency, which Wong and McAleer's reading needs.

**Congress's sessions.** The one form that would reach thirty decisions easily, recesses within each
year; but the dates Congress sits are not in the snapshot and follow no calendar rule — the same
wall as the party's — and a stand-in built on fixed calendar months would read the month of the
year, not Congress, as SC-011-01 found for announcements on fixed days. Ferguson and Witte's reading
through public opinion also needs polls the lab does not hold.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue the test:**

- **Election dates**, which do follow a rule — the Tuesday after the first Monday of November every
  fourth year, the inauguration on 20 January since the Twentieth Amendment: they place the years
  and months of the term without data, but every form read on them stays below thirty decisions.
- **Several funds, to add decisions**: the five equity funds, or the sector funds, would all switch
  on the same dates; the battery counts decisions across the portfolio, clustered in time.
- **Bonds after an election**, below their average over the fourteen months in Hensel and Ziemba:
  read on the lab's SHY, IEF and TLT, the same calendar and the same count.
- **Canada**, where Foerster and Schmitz find the cycle and its switching rule: the lab holds no
  Canadian fund, and its dates are the same.
- **The second refutation, pre-election monetary support**: the bill's yield, ^IRX, is in the data
  and could be read beside a card; it does not change the count.
- **The January barometer in the first year of the term**, Sturm (2009), as the books report him:
  SC-019-01 read the barometer on every year, not on the five first years in-sample, too few to
  grade; no card stated that reading.
- **Sell in May in election years**, less reliable by the books: SC-018's claim, with four
  election years in-sample.

What would make it testable: a history long enough to hold many terms — decades of index returns,
which the lab's snapshot from 2005 does not have — or a gate 1 floor set for claims whose unit is a
year or a term, a decision for the lab's framing, not for a card; for the party, incumbency and
Congress, those facts as dated, hashed data beside the prices, of which Congress's sessions would
give the one form with decisions enough.

## Status

SC-020 is recorded `not-testable`: every form of the cycle read on the calendar of the term changes
its holdings on dates known in advance, at most twenty-nine times over the lab's eighteen in-sample
years, and fails gate 1's thirty decisions by construction; the party in office, incumbency and
Congress's sessions are not in the lab's data and follow no rule, and written by hand they would be
data with no hash. No card is drawn, and no trial is spent.
