# AS-019-01 — Vertical integration and asset specificity: reasoning, not testable as a theory of its own

## The theory, and the forms its sources give it

AS-019 holds that where assets, suppliers, channels or skills are specific to a relationship, firms
are exposed to hold-up, opportunistic renegotiation and desynchronisation, and that vertical
integration can protect them — bearing on their margins, capital intensity, flexibility, supplier
dependence and resilience against non-integrated rivals — while excessive integration becomes a
handicap once the industry matures and outside suppliers grow efficient. Its refutations: firms
dependent on specific assets bearing no hold-up or renegotiation costs, integrated or not;
integrated firms showing no difference in margins, resilience or supplier dependence where assets
are specific and modularity low; integrated firms doing no worse in mature, modular industries with
efficient suppliers. The bank names stocks and sectors, over years, and measures of vertical
integration, margins, capital intensity and supplier concentration; its references are Greenwald and
Kahn's *Competition Demystified* (2005), Porter's *Competitive Strategy* (1980), held in the 1998
edition, and Caton's *The Economics of Blockchain and Cryptocurrency* (2022).

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library.**

- **Porter** (*Competitive Strategy*, 1980, chapter 14, "The Strategic Analysis of Vertical
  Integration"): the strategic benefits and costs of integrating — economies of combined operations,
  of coordination and of stable relationships, assured supply and demand, access to technology,
  higher entry and mobility barriers, against higher operating leverage, less flexibility to change
  partners, higher exit barriers, capital needs and dulled incentives — and the illusions that lead
  firms to integrate; economies of integration as a barrier to entry, and buyers' threat of backward
  integration as bargaining power, in chapter 1 — signed on costs, risk and competitive position.
- **Greenwald and Kahn** (*Competition Demystified*, 2005, held in a 2007 printing): Coors
  integrated to an unusual degree, from its own barley onward, as it entered the beer wars, an
  integration that gave it no lasting cost advantage (chapter 5); Ford's River Rouge, a model of
  integration, turning into a liability once the car industry was mature and its outside suppliers
  had become reliable and efficient, and Compaq hurt by doing too much in-house (chapter 6) — the
  theory's handicap of excessive integration, signed on costs and the firms' fortunes.
- **Caton, ed.** (*The Economics of Blockchain and Cryptocurrency*, 2022): Williamson's transaction
  costs — uncertainty and asset specificity making some transactions cheaper inside the firm than in
  the market — and his four kinds of asset specificity, in the blockchain space mostly human capital
  and dedicated assets; governance, not returns.
- **Others in the library**: hold-up after relationship-specific investments in commodity supply
  contracts (Kleindorfer, chapter 3 in Geman, ed., *Risk Management in Commodity Markets*);
  vertically related markets, foreclosure and hold-up, with Hortaçsu and Syverson's study of cement
  and ready-mix concrete, 1963 to 1997, in which integration itself explains little once plants'
  size and productivity are accounted for — the second refutation's kind of evidence, signed on
  prices, shares and productivity, before the lab's years (Belleflamme and Peitz, *Industrial
  Organization*, 2010, chapter 17); outsourcing raising returns on capital without necessarily
  creating value, Apple, HP and Nike its examples (Koller and others, *Valuation*, eighth edition,
  chapter 24); technology making value chains disintegrate and letting specialists threaten
  integrated firms (Mauboussin and Rappaport, *Expectations Investing*, revised edition, 2021); the
  vertically integrated electricity utility and its unbundling into separate generation,
  transmission and distribution under restructuring (Pirrong, *Commodity Price Dynamics*, 2011;
  Geman, *Commodities and Commodity Derivatives*), signed on power prices and market design;
  vertical integration as one way of providing a common input to several users (Tirole, *The Theory
  of Corporate Finance*); vertical integration among the strategic choices that drive a firm's
  value, and AMD's separation of its chip design from its fabrication in 2008, the plants becoming
  GlobalFoundries, as rising plant costs made integration too heavy for the smaller rival — signed
  on its capital spending, which fell by 85% from 2006 to 2011 (Mauboussin and Rappaport,
  *Expectations Investing*, revised edition, 2021).

**Return signs near the theory.**

- **Cumming, Dai and Johan** (*Hedge Fund Structure, Regulation, and Performance around the World*,
  chapter 7): hedge funds' ties to their key service providers as asset-specific, and funds in
  jurisdictions that restrict where those providers may sit showing lower alphas and lower average
  monthly returns — a measured return sign, on hedge funds across countries, which the lab does not
  hold.
- **Ang** (*Asset Management*, 2014, chapter 16): mutual funds whose management is outsourced to a
  subadviser underperforming funds run inside the family (Chen, Hong and Kubik, 2010) — a sign on
  fund managers' boundaries, on mutual funds the lab does not hold.
- **Fisher** (*Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits and Other Writings*, "Is the Market Efficient?"):
  Raychem, whose principle was to conceive, make and sell its products itself, trading at a high
  price-earnings ratio in the early 1970s, and falling to about a third of its high in 1976 after
  two blows: Stilan, a proprietary polymer and the first product for which it was to make its own
  base chemicals rather than buy them, proved a commercial failure, and a reorganisation cut its
  earnings for some quarters — one firm, a failed backward integration among two causes, before the
  lab's years.
- **Tirole** (*The Theory of Corporate Finance*, section 4.3.1, "Redeployability"): specialised
  assets fetching low resale prices, so that a Silicon Valley firm borrows at high spreads over
  Treasuries while a gas pipeline borrows cheaply — a credit-spread sign on asset specificity, as an
  illustration, on corporate bonds the lab does not hold.
- **Ilmanen** (*Expected Returns*, 2011, chapter 14): a lead-lag between the returns of a company
  and its customers or suppliers (Cohen and Frazzini, 2008) — a form held by **LL-010** for firms
  and **LL-011** for industries, not integration within a firm.

Each is a sign on hedge funds, mutual funds, one firm, corporate bonds or another theory's lead-lag;
none reads the integration of the firms the lab's funds hold.

## What the lab can read of it

The lab holds the daily bars and volumes of twenty-two funds and bitcoin — the funds from 2005 or
their later start, bitcoin from September 2014 — adjusted for dividends and splits, which encode the
funds' distributions, though no strategy reads them until a point-in-time reading with its own test
is built; the Treasury bill's yield from 2005 and two franc exchange rates; the funds' prices, never
their holdings' prices; and no single stock, measure of integration, margin, capital intensity,
supplier concentration or contract; its snapshot is closed, an asset or a series being added only
before the first card. Nothing was computed from the bars for this judgement. On those data:

- **Hold-up and renegotiation costs**, the first refutation: firms' contracts and costs, not held.
- **Integrated against non-integrated firms' margins and resilience**, the second: firms'
  integration and accounts, not held; each sector fund holds integrated and non-integrated firms
  together.
- **Integration as a handicap in mature, modular industries**, the third: dated changes of an
  industry's modularity with firms' accounts, not held; the library's cases — River Rouge, Compaq,
  cement and concrete, electricity's unbundling, AMD's separation of its plants in 2008 — are single
  firms' or industries' and precede the lab's years but for AMD's, the one dated event inside them,
  far fewer than gate 1's thirty decisions, and none is signed on returns; a firm's separation as a
  listed event is **AS-007**'s form (spin-offs), recorded not testable.
- **The return forms of durable advantage**: **EF-012**'s and **EF-004**'s, and the persistence and
  fade of profitability **EF-015**'s and **MR-029**'s, all on single stocks.
- **Bitcoin**: Caton's transaction-cost reading of blockchains, mining pools' specific investments
  in hardware and cheap power, and hold-up among miners are on governance, with no return sign.

**The bank's siblings, and how AS-019 differs.** **AS-014**, barriers to entry, the family's
umbrella, of which integration is one source; **AS-015**, switching costs, customers' lock-in where
AS-019's is between firms along the supply chain; **AS-016**, economies of scale, **AS-017**,
network effects, and **AS-018**, the experience curve; all five recorded not testable, AS-014's
concentration form and the count for any fixed ranking of the sector funds holding here too;
**AS-020**, two-sided platforms; **AS-007**, spin-offs, recorded not testable; **LL-010**,
customer-supplier information diffusion, the nearest to AS-019's supplier dependence, which reads a
customer's news reaching its suppliers' prices, not the protection integration gives; **LL-021**,
complicated firms, which reads the slow pricing of firms spanning several industries; **LL-011**,
vertical industry links and cross-predictability, which reads the returns of supplier and customer
industries as a channel of news between them, not integration within a firm, and which is its own
theory; **EF-004**, **EF-012**, **EF-015** and **MR-029**, which read single firms' returns on
capital; **CA-011** and **CA-013**, sector rotation, which read the sector funds on time-varying
signals; all untouched but AS-007 and AS-014 to AS-018.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue a card:** the sectors where assets are most
specific — energy and utilities for their plants and networks, industrials for complex supply chains
— against the others: no source measures a sector's integration or asset specificity or signs a
return on it; O'Shaughnessy's ranking of the ten sectors by their compound returns from 1967 to 2009
(*What Works on Wall Street*, fourth edition, 2011, chapter 24) is of past returns, explained by
other causes; and any fixed ranking of the sector funds makes at most three decisions — the first
holding and the entries of the real-estate fund (September 2016) and the communication fund (June
2018) — certain to fail gate 1's thirty whatever the prices, so no card is drawn for it (RUNBOOK
step 9).

What would make it testable: firms' integration, contracts, margins and supplier concentration, with
dated changes of an industry's modularity within the lab's years — outside the lab's data.

## Status

AS-019 is recorded `not-testable`: its claim and every refutation read firms' integration,
contracts, margins and supplier dependence, which the lab does not hold; its sources sign costs,
governance and firms' fortunes; its nearby return signs are on hedge funds, mutual funds, one firm
before 2005 or corporate bonds the lab does not hold; the one dated de-integration inside the lab's
years is a single firm's; the returns of vertically linked industries are LL-011's own form; and any
fixed ranking of the sector funds makes at most three decisions, certain to fail gate 1. No card is
drawn, and no trial is spent.
