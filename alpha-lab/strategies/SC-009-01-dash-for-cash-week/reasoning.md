# SC-009-01 — The equity funds out of the market over the month-end dash for cash: reasoning

## Why this theory now

SC-009, the payday hypothesis and the dash for cash, explains the end- and start-of-month effects
by the monthly payment cycle. Salaries, pensions, dividends, coupons and contributions concentrate
at the turn of the month: in Ogden's form, investors receive that cash and invest it at the turn,
lifting prices; in the dash-for-cash form, institutions need cash for those payments before the
month ends, sell to raise it, and move prices and the cost of capital before the pressure is
absorbed. The bank holds the cycle structural, following the payment calendar rather than a belief.

**The sources.** The lab read them in the library's books.

- Ogden (1990), on the CRSP value- and equally weighted indices from 1969 to 1986: the standardised
  payment dates of the United States — about 70% of corporate and 90% of municipal debt payments,
  and 45% of the S&P 500's common dividends, due on a month's first or last business day — induce a
  surge of returns on the four turn-of-month sessions Lakonishok and Smidt found: 0.167%, 0.047%,
  0.127% and 0.172% a day on the value-weighted index, the other sessions of the month
  insignificant. The surge is larger in months of easy money, when investors' liquid profits are
  larger, measured by the spread of the Fed funds rate over the bill rate below 2%: the turn's
  sessions averaged 0.179% a day in easy months and −0.061% in stringent ones on the value-weighted
  index, a difference of 0.240% (significant at 1%), the other sessions alike in both; in January,
  on the equally weighted index, the difference was 0.54% a day, not significant, and some of the
  January results, he writes, appear inconsistent with the hypothesis.
- Lakonishok and Smidt (1988), on the Dow Jones Industrials from 1897 to 1986: the index rose 0.473%
  over the four sessions from −1 to +3, more than its average month's 0.349%, so that it fell over
  the rest of the month: −0.032% a day over the sessions from −9 to −5, against −0.001% over +5 to
  +9.
- Hensel, Sick and Ziemba, in Keim and Ziemba's *Security Market Imperfections in Worldwide Equity
  Markets* (2000), on the S&P 500 from 1928 to 1993: all of the month's gain came from −1 to +9, the
  sessions from +10 to −2 negligible, and session −5 the low-return day, consistently negative; all
  of the sessions from −9 to −3 below the average of −9 to +9, −8 and −5 significantly negative (as
  the logic audit read their table).
- Ziemba (*Calendar Anomalies and Arbitrage*, 2012, §1.10 and its Table A.8), on S&P 500 and Russell
  2000 futures from 1993 to 2011 and from 2004 to 2011, by session of the month: sessions −10 to −6
  averaged about −0.065% and −0.06% a day from 1993 to 2011 (−6 −0.16% for the S&P 500, t −1.89),
  against about +0.04% and +0.02% on the sessions from +6 to +11 and −11 to −12, gaps of about
  −0.10% and −0.075% a day; from 2004 to 2011, years that overlap the lab's 2005 to 2011, −0.07% and
  −0.04%, gaps of about −0.08% and −0.05%; sessions −4 and −3 positive in every panel, −5 only from
  1993 to 2011. He holds the turn's causes largely cash flows and institutions: much US money is
  paid on session −1; in Japan, where most salaries were paid from the 20th to the 25th and funds
  invested them with a lag, the turn ran from −5 to +2. Hensel, Sick and Ziemba (1994), as he
  reports them, found about two thirds of the S&P 500's and the Value Line index's monthly gain on
  −1 to +4 from 1982 to 1992 and the second half of the month "at best noise".
- McConnell and Xu (2008), on US stocks from 1926 to 2005 and 35 countries: no reward for bearing
  market risk outside the turn, +0.01% a day on the value-weighted index from 1926 to 2005 (t 0.87)
  and −0.001% from 1987 to 2005; their figure by day of the month, read from its chart by the logic
  audit, puts sessions −10 to −6 at about −0.06% a day from 1987 to 2005 and about nil over the
  whole period. They tested the payday hypothesis on NYSE volume and on the daily flows of equity
  mutual funds from 1998 to 2005: "neither of our tests could rule out" a payday effect, nor
  supported one. The 90-day bill's realised return was lower at the turn than on the other days (t
  −2.54), their bond results "at best, ambiguous"; the turn was not related to a rise in the bill
  rate or other interest rates.
- Ilmanen (*Investing Amid Low Expected Returns*, 2022, box 5.2) states the dash-for-cash pattern:
  equity markets "underperform about a week before the month end, and outperform at the turn of the
  month", which Etula, Rinne, Suominen and Vaittinen (2020) explain by payment cycles that imply
  selling pressure before the month end and buying pressure once the new cash is received. His
  earlier book (*Expected Returns*, 2011, chapter 25) found returns higher at the turn, "both
  during the week before and after", and mutual fund inflows without the regularity the flow
  explanation needs.

Etula and his co-authors' paper is not in the library: its window and its sizes are known to the lab
only through Ilmanen's sentence.

**The window, and how it was chosen.** Ilmanen's two books hold together if the underperformance
lies before the week that precedes the turn: the week before the turn rose (2011), and the market
fell "about a week before the month end" (2022). The card's first reading was the five sessions
centred on a week before the month end, −8 to −4; the base was moved to −10 to −6, the week before
the month's last five sessions, on those two sentences, while SC-008-01's published means by session
(−4 +0.229%) were known. The library's figures by session, found by the logic audit, then placed the
weakness there in the futures from 1993 to 2011 and in McConnell and Xu's recent years, and at −9 to
−5, the only interval Lakonishok and Smidt measured, and at −8 and −5 in Hensel, Sick and Ziemba's.
The first reading is a neighbour of gate 6, −9 to −5 the one-day-late rule's, and both are reported.

**The bank's neighbours.** **SC-008**, the turn of the month, whose explanation this theory is;
**SC-010**, the semi-month effect; **SC-012** and **SC-013**, institutions' rebalancing windows and
infrequent rebalancing; **FP-018**, window dressing, which predicts buying before quarter ends, the
opposite sign in those months; **FP-023**, the mandated rebalancing of balanced and target-date
funds, another month-end flow. This card reads the pressure before the turn, not the turn.

## From the claim to a signal

- **The market**: the lab's five equity funds, SPY, QQQ, IWM, EFA and EEM, in equal parts, as
  SC-008-01 took them. The clause grades the three US funds' average, the market whose payment
  calendar and institutions the sources describe; the five funds' average is reported, and the
  battery holds the five. EFA and EEM are listed and priced in New York, as SC-014-01 found; a US
  institution's sale of them is a New York sale.
- **The calendar**: SC-008-01's calendar of scheduled sessions, derived from the New York Stock
  Exchange's holiday rules alone; the last scheduled session of a month is −1, the one before it −2,
  and so on back, the first scheduled session of a month +1, the next +2, and so on. A session's
  return is taken from the previous session's close.
- **The timing**: payments due on session −1 need cash settled by then; under T+3 settlement, to
  September 2017, a sale on −4 settles on −1, under T+2, a sale on −3. The mechanism places the
  selling before those sessions, not at a given session; the window is the sources'.
- **The rule**: the five funds in equal parts on every session but the window's, and the bill over
  the window's sessions, from −10 to −6: out at the close of −11, back in at the close of −6.
- **The parameters for gate 6**: the window's last session counted back from the month's last,
  `end` 6, with its neighbours 4 and 8 at ±25% and 3 and 9 at ±50%; its length, `length` 5, with 4
  and 6, then 2 and 8. All change the targets.

The sessions were counted before the card by a scratch script outside the repository (`pace.py`, in
the session's scratch folder) that reads the calendar and the market's dates alone, and the logic
audit confirmed them: 1,080 window returns in-sample, five in each of 216 months, and 432 switches;
1,295 mid-month returns, four to eight a month, those whose scheduled session lies from the month's
sixth to its eleventh-last; every neighbour keeps 432 switches. The first return out of the market
is 2005-01-18's. No in-sample return spans both groups; the closures of 2007-01-02, 2012-10-29 and
30, and 2018-12-05 fall on +1, −3 and −2, and +3.

## What the battery judges, and what to expect

**The size predicted — a judgment, from the sources.** From the sources' figures by session: the
gap about −0.03% a day over the long record and −0.05 to −0.10% on index futures to 2011, which
overlap 2005 to 2011. The card judges the three US funds' sessions from −10 to −6 at about −0.02 to
−0.07% a day below the mid-month sessions from 2005 to 2022, shrunk for noise and for publication,
and the five funds' at about the same.

**The rule.** Out of the market over 60 sessions a year, the rule's alpha over the five funds held
always is about 0.238 of the funds' premium, since the hedge charges it only its 192 sessions' share
of a premium earned at the turn, less 60 times the window's mean. At an assumed premium of 6% and a
window at the mid-month's mean, the sources' pattern gives about 1.4% a year before costs, and half
that with half the premium at the turn. At the funds' published premium of 8.7% a year and the
pooled mean above, the judged gap gives about 2.4 to 4.2% a year before costs of about 1.2% a year
(24 switches of the whole portfolio at 5 basis points a side), an appraisal ratio of about 0.13 to
0.32 after costs, the hedged returns' volatility about 9% a year, and a Sharpe ratio of about 0.41
to 0.50, the rule's volatility about 19% a year, against the benchmark's published 0.39 to 0.40.
Gate 2 is then expected to pass from a five-funds gap near −0.02% a day, and gate 3 or gate 4 to
fail — gate 3's probabilistic Sharpe ratio near its 0.95 at a Sharpe ratio of 0.41, gate 4 on an
appraisal ratio of 0.35 or less against about 0.65 expected from the lab's trials by luck.

**The clause.** Judged before costs: over the in-sample sessions from the base's first held, the
three US funds' (SPY, QQQ, IWM) average daily return less the bill's rate, each from the market's
previous close; the mean over the sessions whose return spans a scheduled session from −10 to −6 of
its month, less the mean over the sessions whose return spans a scheduled session outside −10 to +5,
from the month's sixth scheduled session to its eleventh-last, with the standard error of that
difference, the square root of the sum of each group's variance over its count, and its t
statistic. The theory is refuted in this form if that t statistic is +0.35 or above: the window
stronger than the middle of the month by 0.35 standard errors or more. Below it, short of passing
gates 1 to 7, it is not proven, not refuted; a base that passes gates 1 to 7 while the clause
refutes leaves the theory refuted in this form.

Of SC-009's refutation criteria, the second, no move in prices at the end of the month when
institutions need cash, or one not absorbed afterwards, is graded through the window's weakness; its
absorption is reported only, as the summed gaps of the sessions from −10 to +3 against the
mid-month, and the cost of capital through the bill's yield. The fourth, the effect no stronger
where investors are bound by accounting calendars, is reported through a proxy, the months that end
a quarter against the others, within one market. The first, the clustering of payments, and the
third, the turn as large where payment calendars are spread, need payment data the lab does not
have; Ogden's monetary test needs the Fed funds rate, which the lab's data do not carry.

**The test's power.** At a daily standard deviation of about 1.33% for the three US funds' average
over all in-sample sessions, and 1,080 window sessions against about 1,295 mid-month ones, the
standard error of the difference is about 0.055% a day. The clause then refutes about 36% of the
time with no effect, about 24% at −0.02% a day, 10% at −0.05% and 5% at −0.07%; with the sessions
before holidays placed as they are, about 42%, 28%, 13% and 7%. At the low end of the judgment it
cannot tell the effect from none.

**Measures stated before the run**, reported, not graded, each gap against the mid-month sessions
with the standard error from the two groups' variances, for the three US funds' average unless
stated:

- the same gap for each of the five funds, for the five funds' average, and for IWM less SPY;
- the gap of each session from −10 to +3, for the US funds' and the five funds' averages; the summed
  gaps from −10 to +3, the pressure and its absorption, its standard error the square root of the
  sum of each session's variance over its count plus 13² times the mid-month variance over its
  count; and the gap of the turn from −1 to +3;
- the gap of −8 to −4, the first reading, and of −9 to −5, the long records' place;
- the window's gap for the Treasury funds SHY, IEF and TLT; and the change of the bill's yield,
  ^IRX's close on the session less its close on the session before, in basis points, on each session
  from −10 to +3 against the mid-month sessions;
- the window's gap in the months that end a quarter, against those months' mid-month sessions, and
  in the other months, against theirs;
- the gap over 2005–2011, inside Ziemba's 2004–2011 panels, and 2012–2022, outside every source; and
  without 2008;
- the rule's alpha before and after costs, and the neighbours' and the one-day-late rule's alphas
  and appraisal ratios.

**Risks named before the run.**

- **Gate 2**: the rule sits out a twelfth of the year's sessions, whatever they earn; its alpha
  rests on the turn's concentration, which SC-008-01 found in these funds.
- **Gate 4**: the appraisal ratio the judgment allows is about half of what the lab's trials reach
  by luck.
- **Costs**: 24 switches a year, about 1.2% a year.
- **Gate 6**: the neighbours move the window by two to three sessions; the reading −8 to −4 takes in
  session −4, which rose most in SC-008-01's figures; a weakness confined to the card's week may
  fail gate 6 on them. The five funds are held most of the time, each fund's share of the profit its
  own gain outside the window: rules held most of the time have come near 30% — QQQ 33% in
  SC-017-01, which failed on it, 23.8% in SC-001-01.
- **2008**: the window takes in the crash's autumn falls, which may carry much of any gap.

## Choices, and the options rejected

- **Out over the window rather than in over the turn**: in over the turn is SC-008-01's rule; the
  dash for cash adds the pressure before it, which only a rule out of the market over that week
  reads.
- **The comparison with the middle of the month rather than with all other sessions**: the turn and
  the week before it rise in every source; against all other sessions, the window would look weak
  for the turn's strength alone.
- **The Treasury funds as the market**: the dash for cash moves them too, in the bank's words, but
  McConnell and Xu's bond results are ambiguous and no source gives a sign for the funds; they are
  reported.
- **Ogden's monetary split**: the Fed funds rate is not in the lab's data.

## Implementation

Long only, the five equity funds, each with a European (UCITS) fund that tracks it, or cash; 24
orders a year on sessions known ahead. A weekly portfolio holds it only if it places the two orders
on those sessions; a monthly one cannot: a calendar effect enters a portfolio only if it passes the
battery. The targets are filled at the close of the session on which they are set; gate 6 adds a
day's delay.
