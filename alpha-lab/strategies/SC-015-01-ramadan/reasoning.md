# SC-015-01 — The Ramadan effect: reasoning, not testable

## The theory, and the form its sources give it

SC-015 holds that the month of Ramadan changes sentiment, attention, the hours of activity and
possibly risk aversion among the investors who observe it, so that the equity markets of
majority-Muslim countries earn more during Ramadan than in the rest of the year, sometimes with
lower volatility. Ramadan follows the lunar Hijri calendar and moves through the solar year by about
eleven days a year, so the effect moves with it. The bank's refutations: no difference in returns or
volatility during Ramadan in the markets of majority-Muslim countries; an effect that stays in a
fixed solar month; an effect as strong in markets whose investors are not exposed to Ramadan as in
those whose investors are; no change in trading activity or sentiment.

**Its sources, as the lab read them.** Białkowski, Etebari and Wisniewski's working paper of 2009,
as Dzhabarov and Ziemba report it in the same text in two books, the Zacks *Handbook of Equity
Market Anomalies* (2011, chapter 9) and Ziemba's *Calendar Anomalies and Arbitrage* (2012, §1.5.2):
one secondary reading printed twice; the bank cites a 2012 publication of the study, *Fast Profits*,
which is not in the library. On fourteen predominantly Muslim countries from 1989 to 2007: returns
during Ramadan almost nine times those of the rest of the year, 38.1% against 4.3%, as the books
give them, with no unit (they read as annualised means), after controls for the January, Halloween
and turn-of-the-week effects; higher in eleven of the fourteen countries, not in Bahrain, Saudi
Arabia and Indonesia, which the authors put down to few observations in the first two and to the
Asian crisis of 1997–98 in the third; a cumulative abnormal return of 2.5 to 3.1% over the month,
against a constant mean and against a market model on 23 industrialised countries without Muslim
majorities; volatility lower during Ramadan in every country but Turkey; the effect kept through
tests for illiquidity, exchange rates and the other calendar effects; and no evidence of higher
trading volume during Ramadan. They conclude that an investor could buy at Ramadan's start and sell
at its end, but neither model that strategy nor estimate its costs. The bank's other references,
Hassan, Rabbani and Rashid (2019) on returns and volume, and Lopez-Martín (2022) on
cryptocurrencies, where the bank reports the effect varying from coin to coin, are not in the
library.

## Why the lab cannot test it

The claim is about the equity markets of majority-Muslim countries, and the lab holds none. Its
universe holds the US sector funds, SPY, QQQ, IWM, EFA, EEM, gold, silver, a commodity basket,
three Treasury funds and bitcoin.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue the test:**

- **EEM**, the emerging-markets fund, alone or against EFA or SPY: the majority-Muslim markets —
  Malaysia, Indonesia, Turkey, Egypt, Pakistan and Morocco early on, the Gulf markets from 2014
  (Saudi Arabia from 2019) — are a minority of its index's weight, on the order of a tenth, rising
  over the years; the lab holds no record of the weights. At a cumulative abnormal return of about
  3% over the month in those markets, EEM's Ramadan month would move by about 0.2 to 0.4%. Over
  2005–2022, EEM's returns over 21 sessions had a standard deviation of 6.9%, EEM's less EFA's 3.3%
  and less SPY's 4.3%, all months together: over eighteen Ramadans a standard error of about 1.6%
  for EEM alone and 0.8% for its difference from EFA, a t statistic of about 0.1 to 0.5 at the
  source's size. No run could tell such an effect from none, and a refutation on EEM would say
  nothing about the markets the theory names.
- **Volatility rather than returns**: the source's lower volatility during Ramadan is a measure, not
  a position, and in EEM it would be diluted as the return is.
- **Bitcoin**, after Lopez-Martín: no source in the library gives the sign of a Ramadan effect in
  cryptocurrencies, and the bank reports it varying from coin to coin, which leaves a card no sign
  to predict; bitcoin's prices start on 2014-09-17, leaving eight Ramadans in-sample; and the lab
  holds one cryptocurrency.
- **The second refutation**, an effect fixed in a solar month: in-sample Ramadan moves back from
  October (2005) to April (2022), and from about 2006 to 2019 falls between May and October, the
  half of the year the Halloween effect calls weak, which SC-017-01 and SC-018-01 read. Like the
  third, it reads only beside an effect in the exposed markets, and on EEM it would be confounded
  with the season.
- **The third refutation, on the lab's other funds**: that the US and developed-market funds show no
  Ramadan effect is a control, which reads only beside an effect in the exposed markets; alone it
  grades nothing of the theory. It would also be a rule timing funds with no stated reason to move.
- **The fourth refutation, trading activity**: the source found no evidence of higher volume during
  Ramadan, which does not bear out the bank's change in trading activity as a rise, and the books
  say nothing of a fall; the snapshot's files hold the funds' volumes, but the lab passes a strategy
  its closes only, and a US-listed fund's volume is not the trading in its home markets.
- **Cultural holidays in Asian markets**: Keim and Ziemba's volume reports Chan and others (1996),
  who found the pre-holiday effect only on cultural holidays, Aidilfitri, the end of Ramadan, and
  other Islamic holidays among them, in India, Malaysia and Singapore; one session before each, in
  markets the lab does not hold, and SC-014's and SC-016's claims rather than this one's.
- **SC-016**, the religious and cultural calendar effects, names the same outperformance during
  Ramadan in Muslim markets, with Białkowski and his co-authors among its references: that part of
  SC-016 is this claim and meets the same wall; its Rosh Hashanah and Golden Week parts are its own.
- **The dates**: Ramadan's start follows the sighting of the new moon in some countries and a
  calculation in others, and varies by one or two days between them; a tabular rule places it
  within about as much, which a month-long window would tolerate, and a table of dates written in
  the strategy's code would read nothing outside its market. The dates are not what stops the test.

What would make it testable: price data for the equity markets of majority-Muslim countries —
US-listed country funds, of which the Malaysian one alone covers 2005 and the Turkish, Indonesian,
Egyptian and Gulf ones start from 2008 to 2015, their liquidity to be checked — added to the lab's
universe: a decision to reopen the lab's framing, which fixes the universe before the first card.

## Status

SC-015 is recorded `not-testable`: its claim is about the equity markets of majority-Muslim
countries, which the lab does not hold; EEM holds them only as a minority, about a tenth of its
weight, where an effect of the source's size would lie within a fraction of a standard error even
against EFA; the lab's one cryptocurrency has no sourced sign, and the second and third refutations
are controls that read only beside an effect in the exposed markets. No card is drawn, and no trial
is spent.
