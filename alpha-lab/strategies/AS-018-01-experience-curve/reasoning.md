# AS-018-01 — Experience curve: reasoning, not testable as a theory of its own

## The theory, and the forms its sources give it

AS-018 holds that a firm's unit costs fall with its cumulative output — it learns, refines its
processes, cuts waste — so that in industries where that learning is at least partly proprietary,
the firms with the largest cumulative output keep a lasting cost advantage over their followers,
which collapses when the knowledge spreads quickly or the technology is abruptly renewed. Its
refutations: unit costs not falling with cumulative output; leaders in cumulative output holding no
cost advantage over followers where learning is proprietary; the leaders' advantage persisting
unchanged after the knowledge spreads or the technology is renewed. The bank names stocks and
sectors, over years, and cumulative production, unit costs, margins and market shares; its reference
is Porter's *Competitive Strategy* (1980), which the library holds in the 1998 edition.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library.**

- **Porter** (*Competitive Strategy*, 1980, chapter 1, "Barriers to Entry" and "Experience and Scale
  as Entry Barriers"): unit costs declining with cumulative experience as workers, equipment,
  designs and controls improve, most in labour-intensive, intricate assembly such as aircraft and
  shipbuilding and in a product's early years; a barrier only if the experience stays proprietary,
  and a more elusive one than scale; nullified by a new technology that starts a new curve on which
  entrants overtake the leaders, and nearly fatal when more than one firm builds its strategy on it;
  chapter 8, "Accumulation of Experience": a lead from experience lasting only while followers
  cannot catch up by copying the leaders' methods or buying newer machinery; and elsewhere cost
  leadership (chapter 2), leaders against followers (chapter 7), pre-emptive capacity (chapter 15)
  and the growth-share matrix — all signed on costs, entry, strategy and industry structure.
- **Greenwald and Kahn** (*Competition Demystified*, 2005, held in a 2007 printing): learning
  raising yields in chemical and semiconductor processes, its cost advantages shorter-lived where
  technology changes fast — semiconductors, their equipment, biotechnology (chapter 2); Philips's
  head start down the learning curve in compact discs, worked through as a cost advantage that would
  vanish within less than two years once an entrant's cumulative output caught up, sustained only by
  a slowly growing market (chapter 7, "Production Advantages Lost") — the third refutation's
  collapse, told on costs.
- **Others in the library, on single firms' costs and margins**: a doubling of cumulative output
  cutting unit costs by about 20% for the median firm, raising operating margins (Mauboussin and
  Rappaport, *Expectations Investing*, revised edition, 2021); a study by Levin and others (1987)
  finding that moving quickly down the learning curve protects better than patents, with Intel
  keeping its lead by moving first to each new generation of chips (Damodaran, *Investment
  Valuation*, fourth edition, 2025, "Measuring the Moat"); learning economies and cost leadership in
  industry analysis (Palepu, Healy and Bernard, *Business Analysis and Valuation*, third edition,
  2003).

**Return signs near the theory**, all on the third clause — knowledge spreading or technology
renewed:

- **Lev and Gu** (*The End of Accounting*, 2016): Dell's share price halving from mid-2005 once
  competitors copied its build-to-order model, and BlackBerry's patents undone by the iPhone, a loss
  of the intangibles' value rather than a price.
- **Mauboussin and Rappaport** (*Expectations Investing*, revised edition, 2021, "Model of
  Disruptive Innovation"): disruptive technologies leading investors to lower their expectations of
  incumbents, Netflix's streaming against Blockbuster, bankrupt in 2010.
- **Fisher** (*Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits and Other Writings*): makers of electronic tubes
  seeing their price-earnings ratios shrink as semiconductors took their market, and makers of
  magnetic memory later — undated, before the lab's years.
- **Tvede** (*Business Cycles*, third edition, 2006, chapter 18, "Dot Bomb"): Moore's law among the
  drivers of the 1990s capital-spending boom that ended in the 2000 crash — one episode before the
  lab's years.
- **Ziemba** (*Calendar Anomalies and Arbitrage*): the Hirsches' forecast of a super boom from 2017,
  the Dow at 38,820 in 2025, carried by the end of war, inflation and enabling technologies — one
  dated call on the whole market, with new technologies one of three drivers, which a fixed stance
  reads as one decision.
- **O'Shaughnessy** (*What Works on Wall Street*, fourth edition, 2011, chapter 24): information
  technology the lowest compound return of the ten sectors from 1967 to 2009, which he blames on the
  sector's relentless innovation and change — AS-018's renewal, told as a ranking of past returns in
  portfolios that weight their stocks equally.

Each is a single firm, one episode or one dated call on the market, read after the fact; the
library's anomaly and factor books carry no return sign on the experience curve or learning by
doing.

## What the lab can read of it

The lab holds the daily bars and volumes of twenty-two funds and bitcoin — the funds from 2005 or
their later start, bitcoin from September 2014 — adjusted for dividends and splits, which encode the
funds' distributions, though no strategy reads them until a point-in-time reading with its own test
is built; the Treasury bill's yield from 2005 and two franc exchange rates; the funds' prices, never
their holdings' prices; and no single stock, cumulative output, unit cost, margin or market share;
its snapshot is closed, an asset or a series being added only before the first card. Nothing was
computed from the bars for this judgement. On those data:

- **Unit costs falling with cumulative output**, the claim and first refutation: products' output
  and costs, not held.
- **Leaders' cost advantage where learning is proprietary**, the second: firms' costs and shares,
  not held.
- **The advantage's collapse when knowledge spreads or technology is renewed**, the third: dated
  diffusions and renewals with firms' costs, not held. The library dates about four inside the lab's
  years — Dell's model copied from mid-2005, Netflix's streaming from 2007 and Blockbuster's
  bankruptcy in 2010, the iPhone of 2007, the HD DVD war's end in February 2008 — each one firm's or
  one product's, none on costs or cumulative output, none signed on a fund, and far fewer than gate
  1's thirty decisions.
- **The return forms of durable advantage**: **EF-012**'s (quality minus junk) and **EF-004**'s
  (franchise value), and the persistence and fade of profitability **EF-015**'s and **MR-029**'s,
  all on single stocks.
- **Bitcoin**: the crypto books treat mining hardware's costs as a matter of concentration, and tie
  no learning curve to a return.

**The bank's siblings, and how AS-018 differs.** **AS-014**, barriers to entry, the family's
umbrella, of which experience is one source; **AS-016**, economies of scale, cost falling with size
at a moment rather than with cumulative output; **AS-015**, switching costs, and **AS-017**, network
effects, advantages on the demand side; all four recorded not testable, AS-014's concentration form
and the count for any fixed ranking of the sector funds holding here too; **AS-019**, vertical
integration; **AS-020**, two-sided platforms; **LL-013**, technological links, which reads the
returns of firms sharing technologies, semiconductors among them, as a channel of news; **EF-032**,
research and development capital, a single-stock form on spending rather than cumulative output;
**EF-004**, **EF-012**, **EF-015** and **MR-029**, which read single firms' returns on capital;
**CA-011** and **CA-013**, sector rotation, which read the sector funds on time-varying signals; all
untouched but AS-014 to AS-017.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue a card:** the sectors where learning matters most
— industrials for aircraft and complex assembly, technology for chips, where Greenwald and Kahn find
learning advantages shorter-lived — against the others: no source measures a sector's learning;
O'Shaughnessy's ranking, which reads the technology sector's renewal as a drag, is of past returns;
a fixed tilt to the Nasdaq-100 fund or the technology fund makes one decision; and any fixed ranking
of the sector funds makes at most three — the first holding and the entries of the real-estate fund
(September 2016) and the communication fund (June 2018) — certain to fail gate 1's thirty whatever
the prices, so no card is drawn for it (RUNBOOK step 9).

What would make it testable: products' cumulative output and unit costs, firms' shares, and dated
diffusions or renewals of technology within the lab's years, many more than thirty — outside the
lab's data.

## Status

AS-018 is recorded `not-testable`: its claim and every refutation read cumulative output, unit costs
and market shares, which the lab does not hold; its sources sign costs, margins and entry; its
nearby return signs are single firms, one episode or one dated call on the market, read after the
fact; the dated renewals inside the lab's years are about four, each a single firm's; and a fixed
tilt makes one decision and any fixed ranking of the sector funds at most three, certain to fail
gate 1. No card is drawn, and no trial is spent.
