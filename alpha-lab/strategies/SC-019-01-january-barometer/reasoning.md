# SC-019-01 — Each equity fund after its own positive January, cash after a negative one: reasoning

## Why this theory now

SC-019, the January barometer, is a predictive claim rather than a window's return: the sign of the
market's January return indicates the regime of the following eleven months, January revealing a
latent state — risk appetite, flows at the start of the year, confidence, the macro-financial
environment. The books present it as asymmetric: a positive January is more informative than a
negative one. Its refutations: no relation between January's sign and the rest of the year; a
negative January as informative as a positive one; January returns unrelated to measures of risk
appetite, confidence, flows and macro-financial conditions.

**The sources, as the lab read them in the library's books.**

- **Hensel and Ziemba**, reprinted in Ziemba's *Calendar Anomalies and Arbitrage* (2012), on the
  S&P 500's total returns from 1926 to 1993 and on MSCI indices from 1970 to 1993, the rest of the
  year taken from February to December: from 1940 to 1993, the rest of the year rose in 91.2% of
  the 34 years with a positive January and in 50.0% of the 20 with a negative one; the gains after a
  positive January were larger (17.2% against 10.2% when the rest of the year rose after a negative
  one); a negative January "has provided no information"; from 1926 to 1939 the barometer did worse
  than chance. Abroad, positive Januarys were followed by a rising rest of the year in 74.6% of the
  eight other countries' years on average and in 82.4% of EAFE's; negative ones carried little
  signal — a falling rest of the year followed them in 30% of the US's years from 1970 to 1993 and
  22.2% of Canada's — Europe's index an exception on five negative Januarys.
- **Dzhabarov and Ziemba**, in the same book (§1.3) and in the Zacks *Handbook of Equity Market
  Anomalies* (2011, chapter 9), updating them to 2010 and 2011 on the S&P 500's price index: over 72
  years from 1940 the rest of the year rose in 84.4% of the years after a positive January, against
  72.2% of all years, and fell in 48.1% of the 27 after a negative one, "noise"; 8 of 11 from 1994
  to 2011. Their regression table gives the rest of the year on January over 1940–2010 a slope of
  0.80 (t 2.22) and an R² of 6.7%, no significant slope within the positive or the negative Januarys
  alone; the text calls the 6% plus 80% of January Bronson's regression and their own "a minuscule
  0.5% R²", 12.6% less 0.27 times January — the book contradicts its own table. They report Cooper,
  McConnell and Ovtchinnikov (2006): a spread of 7.76% between the eleven months after positive and
  after negative Januarys over 147 years of NYSE data, robust to the business cycle, sentiment and
  the presidential cycle, in small and large, value and growth stocks, and a rest of the year of
  5.71% on average even after negative Januarys; Stivers, Sun and Sun (2009): weaker since its
  publication in the early 1970s, still useful from 1975 to 2006; Bronson: 12 of 19 failures since
  1978; Bohl and Salm (2010): it works in the United States, Norway and Switzerland, not in the 16
  other countries they study, where "in some cases, the signal is actually going the wrong way";
  Easton and Pinder (2007), cited for international markets under the title "A refutation of the
  existence of the other January effect"; and Hirsch's account of the Lame Duck (20th) Amendment of
  1933, which moved the sessions of Congress and the inauguration into January — a US mechanism
  which the Almanac, as the books report, offers as the barometer's cause, and which would fit its
  failure before 1933. On trading it, the long-in-January, T-bills-after-a-negative-January rule is
  the best of the three tried: 12.79% a year against 11.94% held always from 1940 to 2006 for Cooper
  and others, 13.09% against 12.68% for Marshall and others from 1940 to 2007, who judge the
  difference of no use once tested properly; both before costs; after a negative January, the books
  say, "average 11-month T-bill returns are only marginally larger than average 11-month equity
  returns".
- **Ilmanen** (*Expected Returns*, 2011, chapter 25): the barometer "surprisingly consistent across
  risky assets" from 1927 to 2009 (his Figure 25.5, whose bars the library's text does not hold),
  read with the January effect and the Halloween effect as a seasonally high risk appetite at the
  start of the year; "very well in 2008 but poorly in 2009".
- **Kaufman** (*Trading Systems and Methods*), after Kaeppel (1990), on the S&P 500 from 1938 to
  1989: the first five days' direction, confirmed or reversed by January's end, a guide to February
  to December whose expectation is January's own sign in every one of its six patterns; and his
  update on the Dow to 2010: 7 profitable years of 12, a net of 14 points, a confirmation "very
  weak"; McGinley: January sets a bullish tone, but says nothing of a bear market. Dzhabarov and
  Ziemba report Hirsch's first five days: from 1950 to 2010, 37 positive starts followed by a rising
  year 32 times, 23 negative ones by 12 rising years and 11 falling.

None of the bank's other references is in the library beyond these retellings. The sources' own
samples end between 1993 and 2011, and they name three of the lab's years: January 2008 negative
and the year "devastating", 2010 negative with a rising rest of the year, 2009 a failure; their 8
positive Januarys of 11 from 1994 to 2011 leave 7 negative ones in those eighteen years, some of
them the lab's. 2012 to 2022 lie outside all of them.

**What the lab already knows, and is not blind to.** SC-017-01 published the five funds' mean by
calendar month from 2005 to 2022, January among them (−0.016% a day), and their September and
October returns in 2008 (−28.5%), 2018 (−9.4%) and 2022 (−5.3%); SC-018-01 the halves from November
to April and May to October; SC-007-01 the five funds' excess return year by year over a window that
includes January's first two sessions; SC-016-01 and others their hedged returns by year; SC-002-01
IWM less SPY by year over its window; SC-017-01 and SC-018-01 their yearly differences in their
extreme years. None gives a January's sign or a year's return from February to December. The writer
knows the market's history in broad lines, as any reader of it does; the card takes its expectation
from the sources, not from that memory.

**The bank's neighbours.** **SC-002**, the turn-of-the-year effect, and **SC-018**, the Halloween
effect, which Ilmanen reads with this one as one risk appetite; **SC-020**, the presidential cycle,
in whose first year Sturm (2009), as the books report him, finds the barometer strongest;
**SC-017**, the month of the year.

## From the claim to a signal

- **The market**: the lab's five equity funds, SPY, QQQ, IWM, EFA and EEM, each read on its own
  January: EFA in dollars as Hensel and Ziemba read EAFE; SPY as the S&P 500; QQQ and IWM on their
  own Januarys, the lab's choice, Cooper and others finding the barometer in size and style
  portfolios without the books saying on whose January; and EEM, which no source measures and which
  Bohl and Salm's sixteen failures and the Lame Duck mechanism, American, warn about. The clause
  grades the three US funds' average, the market the sources measured over the longest span; the
  five are reported.
- **January's return**: each fund's return from its last close of December to its last close of
  January, on adjusted closes, which carry dividends as Hensel and Ziemba's total returns did (the
  later updates read the S&P 500's price); for January 2005, the lab's first, from its first close,
  3 January. Its sign, positive or not, is the signal, as in the sources.
- **The rule**: the long-in-January, bills-after-a-negative-January rule, the best of the three the
  books report: each fund holds a fifth of the portfolio over January; from the second scheduled
  session of February to December's last, a fund whose January return was positive keeps its fifth,
  and one whose January return was zero or negative leaves its fifth in cash, the bill's rate. The
  decision is set at the close of February's first session, the first that can read January's last
  close; the funds are bought back at the close of the last session whose next scheduled session
  falls in the next year, read from the calendar. A target is set on both sessions every year, and
  on the first, so that no weight drifts for more than a year.
- **A parameter for gate 6**: `months`, the calendar months from the year's start whose return is
  read, 1 for January; its neighbour 2 at both steps, January and February read and March to
  December decided, the same claim read over a longer signal. No variant: the first five days
  predict, in Kaeppel's table, what January's own sign does, and are reported.
- **Memory**: 45 sessions, covering two months' return read back from the session before the
  decision.

## What the battery judges, and what to expect

**The size predicted — a judgment, from the sources.** Cooper and others' spread of 7.76% over the
eleven months, over 147 years, is about +0.034% a day between the rest of the year after a positive
January and after a negative one; the sources find the spread weaker since the barometer's
publication and in most countries outside the United States, and none measures it after 2011. The
card judges the three US funds' gap at about 0 to +0.034% a day from 2005 to 2022, EFA's and EEM's
at about 0 to +0.02%, the five funds' at about 0 to +0.028%. The rule is out of a fund over the rest
of the year after about three Januarys in eight (27 of 72 in the sources), about 34% of the time.
Its alpha over the five funds held always, at a share out p, is about p(1 − p) times the gap, 0 to
about +1.6% a year before costs of about 0.04% a year (a fund's switch out and back after each
negative January, at 5 basis points a side); out 34% of the time at about 21.9% a year, a tracking
error of about 10% a year, an appraisal ratio of about 0 to 0.16; a Sharpe ratio of about 0.32 (no
effect: the benchmark's 0.40 scaled by the square root of the time held, at equal volatility) to
0.42.

**Gate 1 will probably fail.** The battery counts a decision where the targets change, clustered: a
year adds two — out in February, back at December's last session — only when at least one fund's
January is zero or negative; in a year when all five rise, the February target is December's and
adds none. The count is then 1 plus twice the years with a negative January somewhere among the
five, and 30, gate 1's floor, needs 15 of the 18. At the sources' rate, 27 negative Januarys in 72,
and five funds that move together, a year has about a 55 to 75% chance of one, about 21 to 28
decisions expected, and a chance of 1 to 31% of reaching 30. The card expects the base to fail gate
1 on its count of decisions, and, if it passes, gate 2, or gate 3 or 4, and gate 6 on one fund's
share of the profit, QQQ's as in SC-016-01 and SC-017-01. It is locked all the same: the clause
reads the theory whatever the battery finds, a barometer read on a year's sign cannot decide more
often than once a year, and one trial is the price of reading it.

**The clause.** Judged before costs, on the years, the claim's unit: for each year from 2005 to
2022, the three US funds' (SPY, QQQ, IWM) average daily return less the bill's rate, each from the
market's previous close, averaged over the year's sessions from February to December; its January
return, the mean of the three funds' returns from their last close of December to their last close
of January (from their first close, 3 January, in 2005). The mean of the years' February–December
means over the years whose January return was positive, less that over the years whose January
return was zero or negative, with the standard error of that difference, the square root of the sum
of each group's sample variance of the years' means (over n − 1) divided by its number of years, and
its t statistic, computed apart from the battery. The theory is refuted in this form if that t
statistic is −0.35 or below. Above it, short of passing gates 1 to 7, it is not proven, not refuted;
a base that passes gates 1 to 7 while the clause refutes leaves the theory refuted in this form. The
same gap on the daily returns pooled over the sessions of each group of years, with each group's
variance of daily returns over its number of sessions, is reported beside it.

Of SC-019's refutation criteria, the first is graded; the second, a negative January as informative
as a positive one, is reported through the rest of the year after each sign against all years; the
third needs measures of risk appetite, flows and macro-financial conditions the lab does not hold.

**The test's power.** With about 7 negative Januarys in 18 (the sources' frequency) and about 231
sessions a year at a daily standard deviation of about 1.33% for the three US funds' average, a
year's February–December mean has a standard error of about 0.088% a day if the days are
independent, and the difference between the two groups of years about 0.042%; with 5 negative
Januarys, about 0.046%. A year's volatility regime moves all its sessions together, and the sources
place 2008 and 2010, volatile years, among the negative Januarys, and name 2009 a failure: the
years' own spread, which the clause uses, will likely be wider than the daily errors say. On about
ten degrees of freedom the clause refutes about 37% of the time with no effect, about 23% at +0.017%
a day and about 13% at +0.034%, the sources' size. It cannot tell an effect of the predicted size
from none.

**Measures stated before the run**, reported, not graded, for the three US funds' average unless
stated:

- the same gap on daily returns pooled over each group's sessions;
- the same gap for each of the five funds on its own January, for the five funds' average on its
  January, and for EFA and EEM each;
- the asymmetry: the rest of the year's mean after positive and after negative Januarys, each
  against the mean over all years; the count of years whose rest of the year rose after each sign,
  the sources' table; the regression of the rest of the year's return on January's;
- the first five sessions' sign: the same gap with the years grouped by the sign of the year's first
  five sessions, Hirsch's reading;
- the gap over 2005–2011 and 2012–2022, without 2008, and without 2005, whose January starts on 3
  January;
- each year's January return and rest-of-year return, for the US funds' average;
- the battery's count of decisions, and the years with a negative January among the five;
- the base's alpha before and after costs, and at a beta equal to its share of sessions held; the
  neighbour's and the one-day-late rule's alphas and appraisal ratios.

**Risks named before the run.**

- **Eighteen Januarys**, a handful of them negative, 2008 and 2010 among them by the sources, 2009 a
  failure: one year, one bear market or one recovery after a negative January, can decide the
  clause.
- **The rule's exposure and its beta**: out a third of the time, its Sharpe ratio sits below the
  benchmark's with no effect; the alpha, not the Sharpe ratio, carries the claim. If the years out
  are volatile ones, the regression beta falls below the share held and the alpha gains the
  difference times the funds' premium, a mechanical gain that is not the barometer's, and the
  Sharpe ratio gains from timing volatility alone; the alpha at a beta equal to the share held
  guards against it.
- **Gate 3's placebos** shift the weights in time by a year or more: with about seven to ten blocks
  of eleven months out per fund, the thousand placebos are few distinct alignments, and the rank
  turns on whether the real blocks out cover late 2008 better than shifted ones, which cover it
  about a third of the time.
- **Gate 6**: one neighbour only, `months` at 2; QQQ's share of the profit, as in the lab's other
  calendar cards held most of the time.

## Choices, and the options rejected

- **Short after a negative January**: the books report it losing to the market, the rest of the
  year rising on average even then; the lab is long only.
- **Out in January, waiting for its sign**: the books' standard rule, which misses the January
  effect and loses to the market in both studies they report.
- **SPY's January for every fund**: the sources read each market on its own January, and Hensel and
  Ziemba's regional results are EAFE's own.
- **A threshold other than zero, or the size of January's return**: the sources' claim is the sign;
  the regression's slope is reported.
- **Excess over the bill for the sign**: the sources use the return itself.
- **A wider universe to reach gate 1's count**: the US sector funds, each on its own January, would
  decide in nearly every year; no source reads the barometer on sectors, and a universe chosen to
  pass a gate is not the claim's.
- **Graded on pooled daily returns**: the claim's unit is the year, and a year's regime moves its
  days together; the pooled gap is reported.

## Implementation

Long only, the five equity funds, each with a European (UCITS) fund that tracks it, or cash; at most
two orders a year for each fund, on dates known ahead. The targets are filled at the close of the
session on which they are set; gate 6 adds a day's delay, which decides at the close of February's
second session and buys back at the close of January's first.
