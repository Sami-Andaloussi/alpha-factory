# SC-026-01 — Out of the US sector funds on the session after each change of the clocks: reasoning

## Why this theory now

SC-026, the daylight-saving anomaly, links returns around the changes of clock time to the
disruption of sleep and attention: a small shock to the body's clock affects investors' mood,
cognition and risk aversion, and moves prices just after the change. Its prediction: returns are
abnormal immediately after each change of clock time, studies finding a negative effect, especially
in spring or depending on the country, which re-examinations over more markets and years do not
always confirm. Its refutations: returns on the trading days just after the changes no different
from those of comparable days; no effect in larger samples over more markets and years; no
measurable change in investors' sleep, attention or risk-taking around the changes.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library's books.** Kamstra, Kramer and Levi (2000),
Gregory-Allen, Jacobsen and Marquering (2010) and Hirshleifer, Jiang and Meng DiGiovanni (2020) are
not in the library beyond its reference lists; it holds no size, no autumn-specific finding and no
contrary finding.

- **Chincarini and Kim** (*Quantitative Equity Portfolio Management*, and its second edition, 2022):
  the daylight-saving effect is that "the market tends to drop slightly on the first trading day
  after the beginning of daylight savings, supposedly because the disruption of traders' circadian
  rhythms increases their aversion to risk"; they cite Kamstra, Kramer and Levi (2000).
- **Singal** (*Beyond the Random Walk*, 2003) lists the daylight-saving changes, "for two weekends a
  year", among the proposed explanations of the weekend's negative return, which together account
  for some portion of it; he gives them no figure. Both weekends are thus signed negative.
- **Harris** (*Trading and Exchanges*, 2002, "Why October?"), in a SAD-type account of October's
  weakness: "the change from daylight savings time to standard time may also be important".

The library thus signs the spring change negative, on the first trading day after it, and the autumn
change negative with less weight; the bank signs both, "especially in spring". Keeping the autumn is
sourced, and it is also what keeps the rule above gate 1's count (below).

**What is known before the run, and the lab is not blind to.**

- **2020-03-09**, the Monday after the change of 8 March 2020, is one of the thirty-six sessions:
  from general market history, not the lab's data, the US market fell about 7.6% that day, with a
  trading halt, the oil-price war and the pandemic's spread, and the energy sector far more. That
  one session moves the thirty-six sessions' mean by about −0.2% a day, nearly the whole predicted
  range, widens their standard deviation, and alone is worth about +0.4% a year of alpha to a rule
  out of the market on it. No source ties that day to sleep: the clause is tilted toward "not
  refuted" by it, and the gap and the alpha are reported without it.
- **Election eves**: from 2007 the autumn change's Monday is the eve of a US federal election in
  seven of the eight even years, 2008, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018, 2020 and 2022, every presidential
  election among them (and 2024 in the holdout); the one-day-late rule and the neighbour `sessions`
  2 sit out Election Day itself. The autumn gap is reported without the election eves.
- **SC-001-01** published the week's first session as weaker than the other sessions in the five
  equity funds from 2005 to 2022 (SPY −0.049% a day, QQQ −0.031, IWM −0.066, EFA −0.101); each
  change of the clocks falls on a weekend, so every session after one is a week's first session.
  **SC-025-01**'s window, 22 September to 20 December, held the autumn change's Monday in every
  year, and its published October and November gaps (+0.006%, +0.046% a day) dilute it among forty
  sessions and say nothing of it. No verdict has published the sessions after the changes
  themselves.

**The bank's neighbours.** **SC-001**, the day of the week, whose Mondays these sessions are;
**SC-025**, the season's light; **SC-028**, sunshine.

## From the claim to a signal

- **The dates**: the United States' changes of the clocks, by the rules in force — from 2007 the
  second Sunday of March and the first Sunday of November, before it the first Sunday of April and
  the last Sunday of October — derived from the rule alone, read from no price. The session after a
  change is the first scheduled session after its Sunday: a Monday in every year of the sample, none
  of them a holiday or an unscheduled closure.
- **The markets**: the eleven US sector funds, XLB, XLC, XLE, XLF, XLI, XLK, XLP, XLRE, XLU, XLV and
  XLY, in equal parts among those that trade: US companies, priced at New York's close by a market
  that changes its clocks on these dates. The broad funds are reported apart: SPY, QQQ and IWM on
  the same clock; EFA, whose markets change on their own dates, Europe's about two weeks later in
  March and a week earlier in November from 2007; EEM, whose markets mostly keep no daylight-saving
  time. The sector funds also spread the profit: no one of eleven is expected to carry 30% of it,
  the largest share in a sector rule near its benchmark having been XLK's 14.7% (SC-006-01), where
  QQQ carried 44% among four funds held nearly always (SC-025-01).
- **The rule**: the sector funds in equal parts on every session but the `sessions` first scheduled
  sessions after each change, cash over those: the long-only trade of a claim that those sessions
  are weak. `sessions` is 1, the first session, where the library places the effect; its neighbour
  is 2, at both steps, as the runbook asks of a base of 1.
- **Decisions**: an exit and a return a session apart count as one decision; two changes a year over
  eighteen years and the first session's entry make 37, and 39 with the entries of XLRE and XLC,
  which change the equal parts when they start trading, above gate 1's 30. A rule on the spring
  change alone, the library's firmest sign, would make 21, short of 30 whatever the prices, and gets
  no card (the runbook's step 9).
- **A day's delay**: gate 6's delayed rule sits out the Tuesday instead; out of the market two
  sessions a year, it keeps nearly the base's Sharpe ratio whatever the prices, so the delay does
  not fail it by construction, unlike a rule that holds only on the days the claim names.

## What the battery judges, and what to expect

**The size predicted — a judgment.** No source in the library gives the effect a size; Chincarini
and Kim call the drop slight. The card judges the sector funds' return on the first session after a
spring change at about 0 to −0.3% below the comparable sessions, and after an autumn change at about
0 to −0.1%: over the thirty-six, about 0 to −0.2% a day, leaving 2020-03-09's pull aside, which adds
about −0.2% a day more; the gap without that session is the one read against the range. Out of the
market two sessions a year, the rule's alpha over the sector funds held always gains those sessions'
gap to the comparable ones and, as an estimate from SC-001-01's published figures for the broad
funds, the week's first session's own weakness, about 0.05% a day: about +0.1 to +0.5% a year before
costs of about 0.2% a year, −0.1 to +0.3% after; and about +0.4% a year more from 2020-03-09 alone.
On a hedged return that moves only on those sessions, an appraisal ratio of about −0.1 to +0.3. The
card expects gate 4 to fail, which asks about 0.64; gate 2 may pass, carried by 2020-03-09, the
rule's Sharpe ratio a few hundredths from the benchmark's; gate 3 is uncertain, its probabilistic
Sharpe ratio near the benchmark's and its placebo rank raised by the crash day; gate 5 likely fails,
2020, the best year if the crash day carries the alpha, leaving it at or below zero without it.

**The clause.** Judged before costs, over the in-sample sessions from 4 January 2005, each placed by
the market session whose return spans it: the sector funds' equal-weighted average daily return,
over those that trade, less the bill's rate, over the thirty-six sessions whose return spans the
Sunday of a change of the clocks, from a Friday's close to a Monday's close, less the same over the
other sessions whose return spans a Saturday and a Sunday alone, from a Friday's close to the next
Monday's; the standard error of that difference from the two groups' daily variances, and its t
statistic, computed apart from the battery. The theory is refuted in this form if that t statistic
is +0.35 or above: the sessions after the changes no weaker than comparable sessions, the bank's
first refutation. Below it, short of passing gates 1 to 7, it is not proven, not refuted. The
comparison is with the other Friday-to-Monday sessions, so that the weekend's own weakness,
SC-001-01's, is not counted for the clocks.

**The test's power.** Thirty-six sessions against about 800, at a daily volatility of about 1.4% on
a Monday: a standard error of about 0.24% a day, about 0.32% with 2020-03-09's session among the
thirty-six. The clause refutes about 36% of the time with no effect, and about 12 to 16% at −0.2% a
day, the top of the range. It can tell only an effect near the top of the range from none.

**SC-026's refutations, and what the card can grade.**

1. *Returns after the changes no different from comparable days*: graded, against the other
   Friday-to-Monday sessions.
2. *No effect in larger samples over more markets and years*: the lab's funds from 2005 to 2022 are
   a new sample, after Kamstra, Kramer and Levi's; EFA on its own markets' dates and EEM are
   reported.
3. *No change in investors' sleep, attention or risk-taking*: out of reach; the lab holds prices.

**Measures stated before the run**, reported, not graded:

- the gap by sector fund, and for SPY, QQQ, IWM, EFA and EEM;
- the spring changes and the autumn changes apart; the autumn without the election eves;
- without 2020-03-09, and without 2020;
- the same gap against all other sessions;
- the second session after each change, the neighbour's;
- EFA on the New York session whose return spans the Sunday of its European markets' change, the
  last Sundays of March and October, against the other Friday-to-Monday sessions; in 2005 and 2006
  the autumn dates are the United States' own, and the three European springs whose change fell on
  Easter Sunday, 2005, 2013 and 2016, when New York's session spans Thursday to Monday and Europe is
  closed on the Monday, are left out;
- 2007–2022, the rule's present calendar; 2005–2006, four sessions, too few to inform;
- the same gap over the holdout, 2023 to 2025;
- the alpha before and after costs, with and without 2020-03-09, and the neighbour's and the
  one-day-late rule's alphas and appraisal ratios.

## Choices, and the options rejected

- **The spring change alone**, the library's firmest sign: 19 decisions, a failure of gate 1
  whatever the prices; reported.
- **Holding only on the sessions after the changes**, or shorting them: a one-day holding fails gate
  6's delay by construction (the runbook's step 9), and the lab does not short.
- **The four northern broad funds**, as SC-025-01 held: EFA's markets change on other dates, and
  QQQ's share of the profit would fail gate 6 whatever the clocks; they are reported.
- **Each fund on its own markets' dates**: the sector funds' markets are the United States'; EFA's
  European dates are reported.

## Implementation

Long only, the sector funds in equal parts or cash, with European (UCITS) funds that track them; two
exits and two returns a year, on dates known ahead.
