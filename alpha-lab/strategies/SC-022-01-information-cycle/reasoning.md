# SC-022-01 — The information-cycle explanation of return seasonality: reasoning, not testable

## The theory, and the form its sources give it

SC-022 reads the same-calendar-month seasonality of stock returns, SC-021's, as the trace of a
calendar of firm-specific information: firms that release important information — guidance,
seasonal sales, budgets, signals about cash flows — at the same times each year have months
structurally richer in valuation news, so that part of the same-month effect comes from the
repetition of the same windows of revaluation, stronger for firms, industries or periods whose
information arrives at an uneven pace. Its refutations: seasonality as strong in months without
recurring disclosures as in months with them; seasonality no stronger for firms and industries whose
disclosures recur in a few months of the year; seasonal returns that stay in the same calendar month
when a firm changes its fiscal year or its disclosure dates.

**Its sources.** The bank names Heston and Sadka (2008), SC-021's, and Li and Loh (2021), *The
Information Cycle and Return Seasonality*, which is not in the library. What the library holds on
the link between the same-month effect and announcements:

- **For the mechanism**: Frazzini and Lamont's earnings-announcement premium, as Campbell
  (*Financial Decisions and Markets*) and Tulchinsky and others (*Finding Alphas*, 2020) report it:
  stocks tend to rise in the months in which they announce earnings, and a strategy long the firms
  expected to announce in the next month and short the others earned 7 to 18% a year in testing,
  strongest in large caps; Ilmanen (*Investing Amid Low Expected Returns*, 2022, box 5.2) adds that
  single stocks earn more on their announcement days. Reporting recurs in the same months each year,
  so a premium in the announcing month would show as a same-month recurrence. And Ilmanen (*Expected
  Returns*, 2011, box 14.1) reports the stocks' periodicity at quarterly as well as yearly cycles —
  lags of 6 and 9 months beside 12 — a pattern consistent with quarterly reporting.
- **Against it as a full account**: the same box reports the periodicity not concentrated in the
  months of earnings announcements, and §25.3 not necessarily coinciding with them; Ilmanen (2022)
  calls the extent to which information events, seasonal dividends or varying risk tolerance explain
  it debated, and reports Keloharju, Linnainmaa and Nyberg finding the same-month recurrence "even
  stronger in systematic factors", portfolios of stocks, and present in other asset classes, which
  have no firm's calendar of disclosure.

The library measures a firm-level premium in the announcing month, consistent with the explanation,
but not how much of the same-month effect it accounts for, which is SC-022's claim. SC-021-01
records the rest of what the library holds on the same-month effect.

## Why the lab cannot test it

Every refutation the bank names reads firms or industries against each other by their own calendars
of disclosure: months with and without a firm's recurring releases, firms and industries whose
releases cluster against those whose releases spread, a firm's seasonality before and after a change
of its fiscal year. The lab's data holds neither firms nor disclosure dates: the snapshot and its
addition hold daily prices of twenty-two funds and bitcoin, the bill rate and two exchange rates of
the Swiss franc. And the effect it explains, the same-month recurrence across stocks, is itself out
of the lab's reach (SC-021-01).

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue the test:**

- **Frazzini and Lamont's premium**: it ranks firms by their expected month of announcement, and
  needs single stocks and their dates; on funds it becomes the earnings-season calendar below.
- **The earnings seasons**, the weeks after each quarter's end in which most US firms report, read
  on SPY or the sector funds against the other weeks: a calendar rule on fixed weeks or months,
  which at a monthly pace reads the month of the year (SC-017), and, as SC-011-01 found for
  macroeconomic releases on fixed days, a calendar fixed in advance cannot tell an announcement from
  the date. Its claim would be an index-level announcement premium, which no source in the library
  signs for an index — Frazzini and Lamont's premium is relative, announcers against the others —
  and which no theory of the bank claims; nor is it SC-022's claim, about firms whose calendars
  differ from the others'.
- **Industries whose news is more seasonal than others'**, the bank's retail, tourism, energy,
  software and agriculture, read on the sector funds (XLY and XLE among them; no agriculture fund):
  the second refutation needs a measure of how each industry's disclosures cluster in the year,
  which no source in the library gives and the lab's data does not hold; eleven funds, two of them
  only from 2015 and 2018, are few to compare industries; and the sector funds' own same-month
  recurrence is SC-023's measure, across asset classes, to which SC-021-01 left it.

What would make it testable: a stock universe, which the lab's framing provides for the theories
that need one, with each firm's disclosure dates and fiscal-year ends over time, a new kind of data
it does not provide — on which SC-021's same-month effect would first have to be found.

## Status

SC-022 is recorded `not-testable`: its refutations compare firms and industries by their own
calendars of disclosure, which the lab's data does not hold, and the effect it explains is itself
SC-021's, out of the lab's reach; its fund-level readings fall into a fixed calendar that cannot
tell an announcement from the date, lack a sign in the library, or are SC-023's measure. No card is
drawn, and no trial is spent.
