# CA-012-01 — Sector rotation on monetary conditions: reasoning, not testable

## The theory, and the form its sources give it

CA-012 holds that monetary policy shifts leadership between sectors: when monetary conditions ease,
cyclical sectors — sensitive to the cost of capital, to the duration of their cash flows or to
credit — are favoured; when they tighten, defensive sectors, less dependent on leverage and on
discretionary demand. Its source, Conover, Jensen, Johnson and Mercer (2008), reads monetary
conditions from the direction of the Federal Reserve's last change of its discount rate: a regime is
expansive from the first cut after a series of increases until the first increase after a series of
cuts, and restrictive the other way round. They choose that signal because such shifts are rare,
widely publicised, binary and known on the day they happen, so that a rotation on them trades
little and reads nothing of the future. Over 33 years, holding cyclical industries in expansive
regimes and defensive ones in restrictive regimes earned consistent excess returns, most of them in
restrictive periods and in poor markets.

The lab could build that rotation: the sector funds split, for example, into cyclical (XLB, XLF,
XLI, XLK, XLY) and defensive (XLP, XLU, XLV) — accounts of the source differ on where energy and
health care belong — and the Treasury bill's yield, which the lab holds as its cash rate,
follows the policy rate closely enough to tell a series of increases from a series of cuts.

## Why the lab cannot test it

The signal changes too rarely in the lab's years for any test to tell an edge from luck. From 2005
to 2022, the in-sample years, the Federal Reserve's policy changed direction four times: the first
cut after the increases of 2004–06, in August 2007 for the discount rate and September for the
federal funds target; the first increase after the cuts, in February 2010 for the discount rate — a
technical widening of its spread over the federal funds rate — or in December 2015 for the policy
rate itself; the first cut after that series, in July 2019; the first increase after it, in March
2022. Read either way, the in-sample years hold five regimes — the first of them begun before the
data — and the rotation would change its holdings four times. The holdout, 2023 to 2025, adds one
shift, the first cut in September 2024.

Gate 1 requires 30 independent decisions in-sample before any figure is believed; this rule would
change its holdings four times — five decisions counting its first, seven at most if the two sector
funds that start late, XLRE and XLC, joined a group — whatever its edge. Its Sharpe ratio, its placebos and its alpha by block would all be
decided by which of five regimes happened to favour which sectors: five draws. The sources' 33
years hold several times as many shifts, and even there the evidence is a handful of regimes per
decade. Running the rule would spend a trial — which counts in gate 4 against every later card — on
a failure of gate 1 known before the run.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue the test:**

- **A signal that changes more often** — the bill's yield over the past three or six months,
  updated monthly — would count more decisions, but most of its changes would be noise around the
  same five turning points: more decisions, no more independent evidence, and a signal the sources
  do not use. It would be a different theory's form, forced to fit the gate.
- **The same authors' later three-state measure** — expansive, restrictive or indeterminate, each in
  place about a third of the time since 1966 (Johnson, Jensen and Garcia-Feijoo, *Invest with the
  Fed*, 2015) — adds, by the accounts available, the direction of the federal funds rate to the
  discount rate's. Since 2003 the discount rate has been set at a fixed spread over the federal funds
  target, so that the two turn together, and from 2009 to 2015 the federal funds rate sat near zero,
  where its changes are basis points of noise; the lab holds neither rate, only the bill's yield. The
  measure would add states, not independent turning points.
- **A tilt toward cyclicals or defensives instead of a switch**, as the source's "overweight" may
  mean: the same few changes of holdings.
- **More sectors, or other markets.** More sector funds do not add policy shifts; the lab has no
  other country's policy rate.
- **A longer history.** The lab's snapshot starts in 2005, and a frozen snapshot does not change.

## Status

CA-012 is recorded `not-testable`: its signal, the direction of US monetary policy, changed four
times in the lab's in-sample years, far too few independent decisions for the battery — or any test
— to judge a rotation on it. No card is drawn, and no trial is spent. The theory would become
testable on a history of several decades of sector returns and policy shifts, which the lab does
not hold.
