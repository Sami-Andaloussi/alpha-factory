# AS-009-01 — Market segmentation: reasoning, not testable as a theory of its own

## The theory, and the forms its sources give it

AS-009 holds that share classes or listings of the same, or economically similar, firms that are
open to different sets of investors can trade at persistently different prices while the
segmentation lasts, and that prices move when it is suddenly relaxed — documented in emerging
markets, dual-class structures and access restrictions. Its refutations: classes or listings that
differ only by who may hold them trading at one price; gaps closed by arbitrage while the barriers
remain; a relaxation, an opening to foreign investors or a non-tradable block becoming tradable,
leaving prices unchanged. The bank names stocks and crypto, days and years, and share class prices,
foreign ownership restrictions, tradability changes and daily prices. Its references, Stulz and
Wasserfallen (1995), Claessens and Rhee (1994) and Fernald and Rogers (2002), are not in the
library.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library.**

- **Foucault, Pagano and Röell** (*Market Liquidity*, 2013, chapter 10): stock market
  liberalisations, relaxing limits on foreigners' purchases, go with more liquidity, a jump in
  prices and a lower cost of equity (Henry, 2000); cross-listed firms earn positive abnormal
  returns, more so when their shareholder base widens (Foerster and Karolyi, 1999).
- **Keim and Ziemba, eds.** (*Security Market Imperfections in Worldwide Equity Markets*, 2000):
  country closed-end funds trade at a premium possibly because of limits on direct foreign
  investment; Bonser-Neal, Brauer, Neal and Wheatley (1990) find four of five country funds'
  price-to-NAV ratios falling at announcements that loosened them, weekly, 1981 to 1989.
- **Pedersen** (*Efficiently Inefficient*, 2015, chapter 9): Siamese twins, two companies each
  listed at home and run as one firm through an equalisation agreement, such as Unilever NV and PLC,
  deviate from parity, each tracking its own market in part; share classes of one firm differ in
  price by their voting rights and liquidity, a gap from the rights attached rather than from who
  may hold them. **Froot and Dabora** (in Thaler, ed., *Advances in Behavioral Finance II*, 2005,
  chapter 3): the twins' relative price moves with the relative indices of the markets where each
  trades.
- **Baker and Kiymaz, eds.** (*Market Microstructure in Emerging and Developed Markets*, 2013):
  chapter 16, on single stocks — Chinese ADRs and home shares not cointegrated under capital
  controls (Chen, Li and Wu, 2010), B-shares leading the A-shares closed to foreigners (Chui and
  Kwok, 1998), A- and H-shares tied more closely as the market developed (Ma, Swan and Song, 2010);
  chapter 21, emerging markets' gradual opening to global investors, with no dated event.
- **Singal** (*Beyond the Random Walk*, 2003): ADRs; country closed-end funds, the Korea Fund's
  capital locked in from 1984; Merton's (1987) investor recognition, a stock's price rising as more
  investors know it, which he finds most consistent with the effects of S&P 500 changes.
- **Burniske and Tatar** (*Cryptoassets*, 2017), on access to bitcoin within the lab's years: GBTC
  trading publicly on OTCQX from May 2015; the People's Bank of China's restrictions on its
  exchanges, which carried over 90% of bitcoin's volume, in January 2017, after which the price fell
  steeply and recovered within a month; the SEC's denial of the Winklevoss ETF on 10 March 2017,
  after which bitcoin fell from $1,250 to below $1,000 within minutes and rallied back toward
  $1,100.
- **Ang** (*Asset Management*, 2014) and **Ilmanen** (*Expected Returns*, 2011): segmentation as one
  reason the Fed's purchases could move one part of the yield curve; Ilmanen reports estimates that
  the 2009 purchases lowered ten-year yields by 40 to 80 basis points.

## What the lab can read of it

The lab holds the daily bars and volumes of twenty-two funds and bitcoin — the funds from 2005 or
their later start, bitcoin from September 2014 — adjusted for dividends and splits, which encode the
funds' distributions; the Treasury bill's yield from 2005 and two franc exchange rates; the funds'
prices, never their holdings' prices or net asset values. Its snapshot is closed, an asset or a
series being added only before the first card. On those data:

- **Twin listings and share classes at different prices**, the claim and its first two refutations:
  single stocks, whose prices the lab does not hold; the twins' form is **MR-037**'s, temporary
  deviations from arbitrage prices, and cross-listed stocks' home-market lead **LL-031**'s.
- **A liberalisation moving prices**, the third refutation: the library signs it (Henry; Foerster
  and Karolyi), but every liberalisation it dates falls before the lab's years, and none within them
  — no inclusion of a market's shares in an index a fund tracks, which would be **FP-011**'s.
- **A static tilt, EEM against EFA as segmented against integrated markets**: the library gives a
  gradual opening with no dated event; a rule holding the tilt would make about one decision, short
  of gate 1's thirty whatever the prices, as AS-007-01's IWM against SPY; country allocation and
  home bias are **CA-016**'s.
- **Country funds' premiums to their holdings at announcements**: net asset values are not held;
  closed-end discounts, and wrappers such as GBTC with no hard conversion, are **MR-026**'s.
- **Bitcoin's access events**: the library dates two or three within the lab's bitcoin years, the
  PBoC's restrictions and the ETF denial with a sign; on one asset, where the lab requires four,
  with two or three decisions, short of gate 1's thirty, and moves that reversed within days to a
  month, against gate 6's delay and bitcoin's day of lag: certain to fail a gate whatever the prices
  (RUNBOOK step 9), no card is drawn. The launch of bitcoin futures appears in the library only in
  reference titles, spot funds not at all; derivatives and funds as institutional access are
  **FP-032**'s and **LL-040**'s, capital flight into crypto **FP-033**'s, listing shocks
  **AS-031**'s and **AS-032**'s. Crypto's segmentation across exchanges is **MR-038**'s; the lab
  holds one price of bitcoin.
- **The Fed's purchases moving one part of the curve**, readable in shape on SHY, IEF and TLT: the
  library dates one programme, a handful of decisions short of gate 1; the supply form is
  **FP-016**'s, inelastic markets, and **MR-015**'s.
- **Investor recognition** on index changes: **FP-011**'s.

**The bank's siblings.** **MR-037**, deviations from arbitrage prices; **LL-031**, home-market
dominance; **CA-016**, country allocation; **MR-025**, limits to arbitrage; **MR-026**, closed-end
fund discounts; **MR-038**, crypto cross-exchange spreads; **FP-011**, index inclusion; **FP-016**,
inelastic markets; **MR-015**, downward-sloping demand; **FP-032**, **LL-040** and **FP-033**,
crypto access and flight; **AS-031** and **AS-032**, exchange listings and clienteles; all
untouched; **AS-008**, clientele effects, recorded not testable.

What would make it testable: the prices of twin listings or share classes, or net asset values, with
the dates of their relaxations; or a calendar of access events dated in the library, numerous enough
for gate 1 and across four assets.

## Status

AS-009 is recorded `not-testable`: its claim and its first two refutations compare share classes or
listings of single firms, whose prices the lab does not hold; the relaxations the library signs are
all dated before the lab's years; a static emerging-against-developed tilt, the bitcoin access
events and the Fed's one programme are each certain to fail gate 1 by their count; country funds'
premiums need net asset values, MR-026's; the twins are MR-037's, crypto's cross-exchange gaps
MR-038's. No card is drawn, and no trial is spent.
