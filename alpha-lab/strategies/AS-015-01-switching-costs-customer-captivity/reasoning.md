# AS-015-01 — Switching costs and customer captivity: reasoning, not testable as a theory of its own

## The theory, and the forms its sources give it

AS-015 holds that firms whose customers face high switching costs — in time, money, learning,
compatibility or the risk of migrating — keep an economic power over them that firms with
free-switching customers lack, in software, brokerage, financial services, industrial inputs, some
recurring consumer goods and networks built on standards, and that open standards or strong
interoperability can remove it abruptly. Its refutations: captive customers leaving as often, and
accepting price rises no more readily, than free ones; firms with captive customers showing margins
and power no more durable than comparable firms; open standards or interoperability leaving
incumbents' shares and margins unchanged. The bank names stocks and sectors, over years, and
customer retention, profit margins, market shares and daily prices; its references are Greenwald and
Kahn's *Competition Demystified* (2005), Porter's *Competitive Strategy* (1980) and Belleflamme and
Peitz's *Industrial Organization*, whose first edition (2010) is the one the library holds, not the
second the bank names.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library.**

- **Greenwald and Kahn** (*Competition Demystified*, 2005, chapter 2, "Demand Advantages: Customer
  Captivity" and "Switching Costs"): captivity — habit, switching costs, search costs — as the
  demand advantage, which a brand alone does not give; software and the retraining of its users,
  reinforced by network effects, as the clearest switching costs; standardised products as their
  antidote, the IBM mainframe's captivity loosening as others sold compatible machines, and credit
  cards' balance transfers ending a cost of switching — neither dated, and both signed on
  competition; captivity lasting when joined to economies of scale (chapter 3); IBM's open PC
  standard of 1981, on which Compaq built, and its later, failed attempt to close it with the PS/2
  (chapter 6) — signed on the firms' fortunes.
- **Porter** (*Competitive Strategy*, 1980, chapter 1): switching costs as a barrier to entry and as
  a source of suppliers' power over buyers, signed on industry profitability.
- **Belleflamme and Peitz** (*Industrial Organization*, 2010, section 7.2): switching costs giving
  firms market power over locked-in customers, for which they compete fiercely beforehand, so that
  whether a market with switching costs is more or less competitive is not clear a priori — their
  two-period models give both signs; ways of estimating switching costs, with the warning that the
  level of switching says little by itself, low switching coming with low switching costs when
  prices adjust to keep customers, and high switching with high ones; a case on mobile telephony in
  Korea, where number portability lowered switching costs without removing them (Lee and others,
  2006); switching costs as an entry deterrent (section 16.3.3), their tie to network effects and
  standards (chapters 20 and 21), and changes of compatibility dated for single firms and products —
  Apple's FairPlay challenged in 2004, LEGO losing its trademark case in November 2005, Microsoft
  and Yahoo! joining their messaging services in 2006, Toshiba ending HD DVD in February 2008 — all
  on customers' choices, prices and market power.
- **Others in the library, on single firms' profitability and value**: customer lock-in as a source
  of a price premium and of returns on capital, Sonos and medical devices its examples (Koller and
  others, *Valuation*, eighth edition, chapter 8); switching costs as a strategic factor in how long
  abnormal earnings persist (Palepu and others, *Business Analysis and Valuation*, third edition,
  2003); Excel overcoming Lotus's switching cost, and social media's switching costs too low to
  protect high returns on capital (Damodaran, *Investment Valuation*, fourth edition, 2025);
  platforms creating switching costs for their users (Mauboussin and Rappaport, *Expectations
  Investing*, revised edition, 2021); an incumbent monitor's information as a switching cost for the
  firm it finances (Tirole, *The Theory of Corporate Finance*); captive customers in Greenwald and
  others' *Value Investing*; lock-in and recurring revenue in the library's summary of *Quality
  Investing*; a financial system built to sustain high switching costs, interoperability its undated
  cure (Harvey and others, *DeFi and the Future of Finance*).

**Two return signs near the theory.**

- **Lev and Gu** (*The End of Accounting*, 2016, chapter 12): for subscription firms — telecoms,
  cable, internet, software — share prices and returns moving with churn, new subscribers and
  revenue per subscriber, most strongly with lifetime customer value (Bonacchi, Kolev and Lev,
  2015). It is a same-period association on single stocks, on the first refutation's own variable,
  customer retention; not a rule.
- **O'Shaughnessy** (*What Works on Wall Street*, fourth edition, 2011, chapter 24, "Sector
  Analysis"): from 1967 to 2009, consumer staples had the highest compound return of the ten
  sectors, financials the second and information technology the lowest; he explains the staples'
  lead after the fact by necessity, wide moats, brands and monopoly or brand power. It ranks sectors
  by their past returns, with no measure of captivity, and puts technology — the theory's software —
  last.

Every other source signs customers' behaviour, prices, margins, returns on capital or value, not the
returns of the stocks or sectors that hold such firms; other than O'Shaughnessy's ranking above, the
library's anomaly and factor books carry no return sign on switching costs, lock-in, customer
loyalty or satisfaction.

## What the lab can read of it

The lab holds the daily bars and volumes of twenty-two funds and bitcoin — the funds from 2005 or
their later start, bitcoin from September 2014 — adjusted for dividends and splits, which encode the
funds' distributions, though no strategy reads them until a point-in-time reading with its own test
is built; the Treasury bill's yield from 2005 and two franc exchange rates; the funds' prices, never
their holdings' prices; and no single stock, customer, retention rate, margin, market share or
standard's adoption; its snapshot is closed, an asset or a series being added only before the first
card. Nothing was computed from the bars for this judgement. On those data:

- **Customers' churn and acceptance of price rises**, the first refutation: customer data, not held
  — and, as Belleflamme and Peitz warn, the level of switching alone would not measure switching
  costs. Lev and Gu's association reads single subscription firms' churn, not held either.
- **Durable margins and power**, the second: firms' accounts, not held; the return forms of durable
  advantage are **EF-012**'s (quality minus junk) and **EF-004**'s (franchise value), and the
  persistence and fade of profitability **EF-015**'s and **MR-029**'s, all on single stocks.
- **Open standards removing incumbents' power**, the third: market shares and margins around dated
  changes of standards or interoperability, not held; the changes the library dates are single
  firms' and products', some inside the lab's years, none signed on returns and none readable in a
  fund.
- **Bitcoin**: Glasner (chapter 3 in Caton, ed., *The Economics of Blockchain and Cryptocurrency*,
  2022) ties a money's value and its stability to users' lock-in through network effects, with a
  risk of tipping; it signs no return and gives no measure over time, and its network form belongs
  to **AS-017** and **AS-021**.

**The bank's siblings, and how AS-015 differs.** **AS-014**, barriers to entry, the family's
umbrella, recorded not testable, of which captivity is one source; **AS-016**, economies of scale,
with which captivity lasts; **AS-017**, network effects, which reinforce switching costs;
**AS-018**, the experience curve, and **AS-019**, vertical integration, whose asset specificity is a
lock-in between firms rather than of customers; **AS-020**, two-sided platforms; **AS-021**, native
network tokens; **EF-004**, franchise value, which names captivity and switching costs as the first
driver of lasting returns on capital, on single stocks — AS-015 claims the power and margins
themselves; **LL-010**, customer-supplier information diffusion, which reads the links between firms
and their customers as a channel of news, not captivity; **EF-012**, **EF-015** and **MR-029**; all
untouched but AS-014.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue a card:** the sectors the theory names —
technology for software, financials for brokers and banks — or O'Shaughnessy's moat-rich staples,
against the others: no source measures a sector's captivity, O'Shaughnessy's ranking is of past
returns explained after the fact and puts technology last, Koller's rankings of industries sign
returns on capital, not later returns, and any fixed ranking of the sector funds makes at most three
decisions — the first holding and the entries of the real-estate fund (September 2016) and the
communication fund (June 2018) — certain to fail gate 1's thirty whatever the prices, so no card is
drawn for it (RUNBOOK step 9).

What would make it testable: firms' customer, retention, share and margin data around dated changes
of standards or interoperability — outside the lab's data — or a measure of sectors' captivity that
changes over the lab's years, with a return sign for it in the library.

## Status

AS-015 is recorded `not-testable`: its claim and every refutation read customers, margins and market
shares, which the lab does not hold; its sources sign customers' behaviour, prices and returns on
capital, not returns; its two nearby return signs are a same-period association on single
subscription firms' churn and a ranking of sectors by past returns with no measure of captivity; and
any fixed ranking of the sector funds makes at most three decisions, certain to fail gate 1. No card
is drawn, and no trial is spent.
