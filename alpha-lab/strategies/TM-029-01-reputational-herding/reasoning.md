# TM-029-01 — Reputational herding: reasoning, not testable as a theory of its own

## The theory, and the forms its sources give it

TM-029 is Scharfstein and Stein's (1990) reputational herding: managers with career concerns trade
in the same direction as their peers, since "it is better to fail conventionally than to succeed
unconventionally", and where their positions are adjusted with inertia their correlated demand
persists, so that the securities they herd into trend in its direction over intermediate horizons.
Its refutations: managers whose trades are no more correlated with their peers' than their private
information implies, even with strong career concerns; herding that disappears as managers gain
experience, the incentives unchanged; correlated institutional demand not followed by price trends
in the securities concerned, even where positions are adjusted with inertia. The bank names stocks,
equity indices, bonds, credit, commodities and currencies, and as data institutional holdings, fund
flows and daily prices.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library.** The library holds Scharfstein and Stein in
others' reports.

- **Hirshleifer and Teoh**, "Thought and behavior contagion in capital markets", chapter 1 of the
  *Handbook of Financial Markets: Dynamics and Evolution* (Hens and Schenk-Hoppé, eds., 2009): the
  model — a second manager imitates the first whatever her own signal, since observers who see the
  two agree infer that both are probably able, even when the outcome is poor; Zwiebel's (1995)
  relative-performance herding and antiherding; analysts' reputational pressure (Stickel; Mikhail,
  Walther and Willis), newsletters herding on the Value Line survey (Graham, 1999); younger fund
  managers choosing more conventional portfolios, with lower nonsystematic risk (Chevalier and
  Ellison, 1999). On prices, Dasgupta, Prat and Verardo find that buying by career-driven traders
  predicts *underperformance* and selling overperformance — the opposite of a trend — while
  institutions are a source of positive serial correlation in portfolio returns (Sias and Starks,
  1997) and emerging markets' autocorrelation rose as institutions entered them (Aitken, 1998).
- **Shleifer** (*Inefficient Markets*, 2000, chapter 1) and **Ang** (*Asset Management*, 2014,
  chapter 15): professional managers herd and hug their benchmarks to avoid falling behind; Ang
  reads herding from career or reputational concerns as one channel by which agency affects prices,
  index inclusions among its measured effects, stocks "widely held by institutions" earning lower
  returns, short-term persistence and long-term reversal, and momentum in the asset classes
  institutions dominate against its absence in over-the-counter stocks. Index inclusion is
  **FP-011**'s, untouched, and needs single stocks and inclusion dates; institutional ownership
  needs holdings; the reversal is **MR-021**'s, untouched; the momentum is read by TM-017-01,
  CA-001-01, TM-003-01 and TM-001-01; the contrast between institutions and individuals TM-015-01
  left to **TM-041**, large caps against IWM, and found no sign for against bitcoin, one asset
  (TM-005-01). **Shleifer and Vishny** ("The limits of arbitrage") and **Hong and Stein**, in
  *Advances in Behavioral Finance, Volume II* (Thaler, ed., 2005, chapters 2 and 14), cite the paper
  among the models of delegated management and of herding.
- **Tirole** (*The Theory of Corporate Finance*, 2005, §7.2.2.2, and exercise 6.9), held in full and
  distilled: the model stated most completely — only able managers receive an informative signal,
  the same one, so a manager under threat of replacement copies an earlier choice — with the forces
  that work against herding (valued creativity, superstars' rents, differentiation) and Zwiebel's
  benchmarked-performance variant; and Chevalier and Ellison's funds that trail after three quarters
  gambling while leaders turn conservative. A model and funds' behaviour, with no sign on prices.
- **Froot, Scharfstein and Stein** (1992), in the *Handbook of Commodity Investing* (Fabozzi, Füss
  and Kaiser, 2008): short-horizon traders gain by herding on the same information, and when
  chartist methods are popular it pays speculators to chart — a mechanism, with no measured sign.

The library gives reputational herding several price signs, none of them TM-029's own on the lab's
data. Hirshleifer and Teoh report that buying by career-driven traders predicts underperformance and
selling overperformance (Dasgupta, Prat and Verardo, p. 35); that stocks mutual funds buy after
consensus analyst upgrades first outperform, then underperform, "suggestive of either reputational
or imperfectly rational herding" (Brown, Wei and Wermers, p. 27); and that institutions are a source
of positive serial correlation in the returns of the portfolios they hold (Sias and Starks, p. 35).
Zacks's *Handbook of Equity Market Anomalies* (2011) reports Jiang (2010), who reads institutional
herding as reputational and finds the value premium at 1.22% a month where institutions herd into
growth and out of value, against 0.26% where they herd the other way. Ang (chapter 15) reads agency,
herding among its channels, as a source of short-term persistence, long-term reversal and momentum
in the asset classes institutions dominate. Each either conditions on the traders' own trades,
holdings or analysts' revisions, or reads prices alone and is claimed by another theory, as the next
section shows.

## What the lab can read of it, and where each form already stands

The lab holds the daily closes and traded volumes of funds and of bitcoin, and the Treasury bill's
yield; a fund's volume is its own shares' trading, not its holders' (TM-028-01); it holds no
holdings, flows or trades of managers, and no record of their careers. Volumes do not open it: a
fund's volume is neither signed nor split between institutions and individuals, and the library ties
no herding sign to traded volume; the institutional trading it reports, in Zacks's *Handbook* —
Shu's (2010) share of volume, Irvine, Lipson and Puckett's (2006) buying before analysts'
recommendations — is measured stock by stock and signs the value premium or a tip, not herding.
Every refutation TM-029 names reads managers' trades or the demand they create:

- **Managers' trades correlated beyond their information, and herding that fades with experience**:
  holdings and managers' careers, which the lab does not hold; institutional herding itself is
  **FP-009**'s claim, and behavioural herding **TM-031**'s.
- **Correlated institutional demand followed by price trends**: the demand, which the lab does not
  hold; the trend on prices alone is momentum, read on the lab's funds — TM-017-01, CA-001-01,
  TM-003-01 and TM-001-01 — and the institutional inertia behind it is **TM-015**'s and **TM-017**'s
  mechanism (TM-015-01); the sign the library gives for career-driven buying, Dasgupta, Prat and
  Verardo's, is a reversal, and needs those traders' trades.
- **Herding into popular sectors by younger managers**: managers' ages and portfolios; the sector
  fund momentum it would feed is read by TM-003-01.
- **Serial correlation from institutions** (Sias and Starks): its contrast, stronger autocorrelation
  in the securities institutions hold, needs holdings; the plain positive autocorrelation of the
  lab's index funds over 2 to 16 weeks is **TM-043**'s claim, untouched, and over a month
  **TM-017**'s; within the lab's groups over a week MR-032-01 published a slight continuation, a
  weekly rank correlation of +0.0106 (standard error 0.0114); institutions against individuals read
  as large caps against IWM is **TM-041**'s (TM-015-01). Emerging markets' autocorrelation rising as
  institutions entered them (Aitken) rests on one emerging-market fund and needs flows (TM-011-01).

**The bank's siblings.** **FP-009**, institutional herding; **TM-031**, behavioural herding;
**TM-011**, informational cascades, recorded not testable; **TM-015**, slow-moving capital and
delegated management, and **TM-012**, mutual funds' momentum trading, recorded not testable;
**FP-013**, benchmarking and closet indexing; **FP-020**, momentum and reversal from investors
switching between funds; **TM-043**, positive autocorrelation of index returns; **MR-021**,
long-term reversal; **FP-011**, index inclusion; **TM-041**, size and momentum; **TM-039**,
dispersion-conditioned momentum, read by TM-039-01. TM-015-01 left the reputational herding of
career concerns to TM-029, and TM-012-01 left it funds' herding into analysts' upgrades: both are
read above, and neither reads on the lab's data.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue a card of TM-029's own:**

- **The reversal after career-driven buying** (Dasgupta, Prat and Verardo): it needs those traders'
  trades.
- **Sector funds' momentum as herding into popular sectors**: read by TM-003-01 and CA-001-01;
  herding adds a cause, not a rule.
- **Year-end indexing by winning funds and risk-taking by losing ones** (Brown, Harlow and Starks;
  Chevalier and Ellison): holdings and fund returns; the calendar of funds' reporting is TM-023's,
  read by TM-023-01, and FP-018's.
- **The value premium widening where institutions herd** (Jiang, in Zacks's *Handbook*): it needs
  institutions' holdings stock by stock, through Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny's herding measure,
  and a book-to-market sort of single stocks, which the lab does not hold.
- **Funds herding into analysts' upgrades, then reversing** (Brown, Wei and Wermers, in Hirshleifer
  and Teoh, p. 27), which TM-012-01 left to FP-009 and TM-029: it needs analysts' consensus
  revisions and funds' trades in single stocks, which the lab does not hold; the later
  underperformance on prices alone is long-term reversal, **MR-021**'s, untouched.
- **Dispersion of returns across assets as a measure of herding** (Christie and Huang; Chang, Cheng
  and Khorana): the library holds Christie and Huang's cross-sectional dispersion only in Amihud,
  Mendelson and Pedersen's *Market Liquidity*, in the chapter on the Tel Aviv exchange's transfers,
  as a descriptive measure of how prices move together over the business cycle and in extreme moves,
  with no herding reading and no sign on later returns; Chang, Cheng and Khorana and the
  cross-sectional absolute deviation are not in the library. Dispersion as a condition on momentum
  is **TM-039**'s, refuted in its form by TM-039-01, whose momentum held only after dispersed months
  lost 1.72% a year against holding it always: a rule holding it after low-dispersion, herded months
  would be chosen from that published result.

What would make it testable as its own: institutional holdings or trades, with the managers'
characteristics, frozen in the snapshot.

## Status

TM-029 is recorded `not-testable` as a theory of its own: every one of its refutations reads
managers' trades, holdings or careers, which the lab does not hold, and its herding is FP-009's and
TM-031's; the trend on prices alone is the momentum read by TM-017-01, CA-001-01, TM-003-01 and
TM-001-01, its institutional inertia TM-015's and TM-017's; the library's signs for career-driven
trading need those trades, holdings or analysts' revisions, and their shadows on prices alone are
TM-043's, MR-021's and FP-011's. No card is drawn, and no trial is spent.
