# AS-016-01 — Economies of scale: reasoning, not testable as a theory of its own

## The theory, and the forms its sources give it

AS-016 holds that incumbents able to spread large fixed costs over a larger volume keep a cost
advantage, and often a price advantage, over entrants who cannot quickly reach the efficient size —
lastingly only where scale is joined to distribution, captive demand or geographic segmentation,
since entrants with equal access to customers can converge towards that size — in distribution, some
manufacturing, software, infrastructure and sector sub-universes, and that a technological change
can lower the minimum efficient size or make large assets less flexible. Its refutations: large
incumbents with high fixed costs showing no cost or price advantage over smaller rivals; scale
advantages lasting as long with equal access to customers as where scale is joined to distribution,
captivity or segmentation; a technological change lowering the minimum efficient size leaving the
incumbents' advantage intact. The bank names stocks and sectors, over years, and fixed costs, sales
volumes, margins, market shares and daily prices; its references are Greenwald and Kahn's
*Competition Demystified* (2005) and Porter's *Competitive Strategy* (1980), which the library holds
in a 2007 printing and the 1998 edition.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library.**

- **Greenwald and Kahn** (*Competition Demystified*, 2005, chapter 3, "Economies of Scale and
  Customer Captivity" and "Defending Economies of Scale"): what counts is the incumbent's size
  against its rivals', its share of the market, not its size in itself; the advantage lasts only
  when some customer captivity is joined to it, since an entrant reaching customers on equal terms
  can grow to the same size; small, local markets are easier to dominate than large ones, Wal-Mart's
  regional scale in the south-central United States its example, which chapter 5 develops with
  Coors; the incumbent defends it by matching an entrant's every move — all signed on costs, market
  shares and profitability.
- **Porter** (*Competitive Strategy*, 1980): scale economies as unit costs falling with the absolute
  volume produced per period, present in manufacturing, purchasing, research and development,
  marketing, service networks, sales forces and distribution, forcing entrants to come in large or
  accept a cost disadvantage; technological change able to penalise the large-scale firm whose
  facilities are specialised and less flexible (chapter 1); the profitability of industry leaders
  and followers compared, the relation depending on the industry (chapter 7); proprietary technology
  eroding by diffusion, scale in research slowing the erosion (chapter 8); a minimum efficient scale
  large against world demand (chapter 13) and rising with new, larger plants (chapter 15) — signed
  on industry structure, capacity and profitability.
- **Others in the library, on single firms' profitability**: scale as possibly the most common
  source of margins kept above average (Fisher, *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits and Other
  Writings*); small firms screened out of a Buffett-style screen for lacking scale and competitive
  advantages (Chincarini and Kim, *Quantitative Equity Portfolio Management*, second edition,
  section 5.3).

**Return signs near the theory.**

- **Hou and Robinson** (2006, reported in Ziemba, *Calendar Anomalies and Arbitrage*, section 1.12):
  firms in the least concentrated industries, by the Herfindahl index of market shares, earning
  about 4% a year more than those in the most concentrated, 1963 to 2001 — the return sign closest
  to scale read as market share, and it runs against the scale holders. It is **AS-014**'s form,
  recorded there as unreadable: no concentration series for the funds' industries, and at most three
  decisions for any fixed ranking of the sector funds.
- **O'Shaughnessy** (*What Works on Wall Street*, fourth edition, 2011, chapter 5, "Ranking Stocks
  by Market Capitalization: Size Matters"): Market Leaders — large non-utility firms with sales half
  again above average, above-average cash flow, shares and capitalisation — beating large stocks,
  all stocks and the S&P 500 from 1964 to 2009, with less risk than all stocks, and nearly doubling
  the S&P 500's and the Russell 1000's return from 1979. It ranks single stocks by absolute sales,
  not relative share, in an equal-weighted portfolio no fund in the lab holds.
- **Lev and Gu** (*The End of Accounting*, 2016): Dell's share price halving from mid-2005 once
  competitors copied the model that had carried its scale — one firm, told after the fact, about
  imitation rather than a change of the minimum efficient size.
- **Ilmanen** (*Expected Returns*, 2011, chapter 12, "Fundamental index debate"): indices weighting
  stocks by the economic scale of the company rather than by capitalisation, ahead of a
  capitalisation-weighted index of large US stocks by about 2% over forty years, as the book puts it
  — **CA-023**'s form, fundamental indexation, on single stocks.

No other return sign on economies of scale was found in the library's anomaly and factor books.

## What the lab can read of it

The lab holds the daily bars and volumes of twenty-two funds and bitcoin — the funds from 2005 or
their later start, bitcoin from September 2014 — adjusted for dividends and splits, which encode the
funds' distributions, though no strategy reads them until a point-in-time reading with its own test
is built; the Treasury bill's yield from 2005 and two franc exchange rates; the funds' prices, never
their holdings' prices; and no single stock, fixed cost, sales volume, margin, market share or
concentration; its snapshot is closed, an asset or a series being added only before the first card.
Nothing was computed from the bars for this judgement. On those data:

- **Cost and price advantages of large incumbents**, the claim and first refutation: firms' costs,
  prices and shares, not held.
- **Scale with and without captivity or segmentation**, the second: firms' markets and customers,
  not held.
- **A technological change lowering the minimum efficient size**, the third: dated changes and
  incumbents' accounts, not held; the one loss of a scale advantage the library dates inside the
  lab's years, Dell's, is a single stock.
- **Large against small funds** — the S&P 500 fund against the small-cap fund — is readable, but it
  is not this theory's form: Greenwald and Kahn place the advantage in size relative to rivals
  within a market, and Porter's absolute measure is output per period, not market capitalisation.
  The library's size evidence is mixed — small stocks ahead on average, the edge mostly in the very
  smallest and gone once risk is counted (O'Shaughnessy, chapter 5), no significant premium since
  the mid-1980s (Ang, *Asset Management*, 2014, chapter 7), a realised premium partly paid for
  illiquidity (Ilmanen, chapter 18) — and none of it is read as scale; the size forms in the bank
  are **CA-029**'s (illiquidity premium), **FP-035**'s (fragile factor premia), **SC-002**'s (the
  January effect, whose card already read the small-cap fund against the S&P 500 fund) and
  **TM-041**'s (size and momentum), and no bank theory holds the plain size premium. A fixed tilt
  between the two funds makes one decision, certain to fail gate 1 whatever the prices (RUNBOOK step
  9).
- **The return forms of durable advantage**: **EF-012**'s (quality minus junk) and **EF-004**'s
  (franchise value, which names scale among the drivers of lasting returns on capital), and the
  persistence and fade of profitability **EF-015**'s and **MR-029**'s — all read single firms'
  returns on capital, while AS-016 claims the cost and price advantage itself.
- **Bitcoin**: the crypto books of the library mention economies of scale in mining and in passing
  elsewhere, and sign no return on them.

**The bank's siblings, and how AS-016 differs.** **AS-014**, barriers to entry, the family's
umbrella, of which scale is one source, and **AS-015**, switching costs and customer captivity,
without which scale does not last, both recorded not testable; **AS-017**, network effects, scale on
the demand side; **AS-018**, the experience curve, cost falling with cumulated output rather than
with size; **AS-019**, vertical integration; **AS-020**, two-sided platforms; **CA-023**,
fundamental indexation; **CA-011** and **CA-013**, sector rotation, which read the sector funds on
time-varying signals; **CA-029** and **FP-035**; **EF-004**, **EF-012**, **EF-015** and **MR-029**;
all untouched but AS-014 and AS-015; **SC-002** and **TM-041**, tested inconclusive.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue a card:** the sectors the theory names —
industrials and consumer staples for distribution and manufacturing, technology for software,
utilities for infrastructure — against the others: no source measures a sector's scale advantage,
O'Shaughnessy's ranking of the ten sectors by their compound returns from 1967 to 2009 (chapter 24)
is of past returns explained after the fact by moats and brands, not by scale, and any fixed ranking
of the sector funds makes at most three decisions — the first holding and the entries of the
real-estate fund (September 2016) and the communication fund (June 2018) — certain to fail gate 1's
thirty, so no card is drawn for it.

What would make it testable: firms' costs, volumes and market shares, or industries' concentration,
over the lab's years, with dated technological changes of the minimum efficient size — outside the
lab's data.

## Status

AS-016 is recorded `not-testable`: its claim and every refutation read firms' costs, prices and
market shares, which the lab does not hold; its sources sign costs and profitability; its nearby
return signs are AS-014's concentration premium, single stocks ranked by sales, one firm's fall and
CA-023's fundamental weights, none readable on the funds; the one readable contrast, large against
small funds, is size by capitalisation, which the theory's sources do not measure scale by, with
mixed evidence read as illiquidity or risk, never as scale; any fixed tilt or ranking of the funds
fails gate 1 by count. No card is drawn, and no trial is spent.
