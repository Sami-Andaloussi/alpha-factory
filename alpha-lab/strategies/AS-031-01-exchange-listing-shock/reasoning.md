# AS-031-01 — Exchange listing and cross-listing shock: reasoning, not testable as a theory of its own

## The theory, and the forms its sources give it

AS-031 holds that tokens listed on a new exchange, especially a large centralised one, earn a
listing premium around the event, that the listing can raise their network's activity, and that a
medium-term component follows if the listing durably changes the structure of liquidity and the
composition of holders. Its refutations: no abnormal return around listings and no rise in network
activity after them; an effect no larger for large centralised exchanges than for others, and no
more durable for tokens previously hard to reach; the premium arbitraged away even where the listing
changes the addressable market or the holders. The bank names crypto, over days and months, and
listing dates, daily prices, volumes and on-chain data; its references are Benedetti and Nikbakht
(2021) and Ante and Meyer (2021) on tokens' cross-listings, neither of which the library holds.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library.** The two references are not in the library, and
the lab does not read papers outside it. The library signs listing and cross-listing effects, on
shares and on tokens the lab does not hold:

- **On shares**: a listing widening the shareholder base of investors who were unaware of the firm
  (Merton, 1987), non-US firms from countries with weaker shareholder protection gaining when they
  announce a cross-listing in the United States while US firms do not, the gain often gone within
  the following year, and prices rising more as the base widens more (Karolyi, 1998; Foerster and
  Karolyi, 1999; Foucault, Pagano and Röell, *Market Liquidity*, 2013, sections 10.5.1 and 10.6) —
  the theory's durability refutation on shares; the market's reaction to a depositary-receipt issue
  highest for firms listing on more liquid, better-known markets (Miller, 1999, same book, chapter
  9) — its large-exchange refutation on shares; listing announcements raising prices with the gain
  in liquidity (Kadlec and McConnell, 1994, in Amihud, Mendelson and Pedersen, *Market Liquidity*,
  2012, the Tel Aviv study); initial public offerings closing their first day about 20% above the
  issue price from 1980 to 2001, about 9% without the late 1990s, and additions to the S&P 500
  gaining through investor recognition (Singal, *Beyond the Random Walk*, 2003) — **LL-030**'s,
  **AS-003**'s and **FP-011**'s forms, on single stocks.
- **On tokens**: an unlisted token hardly having any market value, only a third of 2017's tokens
  being listed anywhere, and exchanges as the gatekeepers that gave Ethereum Classic its economic
  value by listing it after its hard fork of August 2016 (Voshmgir, *Token Economy*, second edition,
  2020, Part 3, "Token Sales" and "Trading Tokens, Atomic Swaps & DEX"), a listing Burniske and
  Tatar date to 23 July 2016, after which people quickly speculated on its value (*Cryptoassets*,
  2017, chapter 5), and Antonopoulos and Wood to 24 July, after a fork on 20 July (*Mastering
  Ethereum*, 2018, Appendix A); Uniswap's token listed in September 2020, opening near three
  dollars, spiking above eight and settling at four to five (Harvey, Ramachandran and Santoro, *DeFi
  and the Future of Finance*, 2021); a listing on the large exchanges as a sign of prestige and
  credibility, and buyers at the exchange launch price hurt by later unlocks (Huang and others,
  *Web3*, 2024, section 6.3.2); being added by regulated exchanges as a stamp of approval, and the
  number of exchanges supporting an asset as a measure of its support (Burniske and Tatar,
  *Cryptoassets*, 2017, chapters 9 and 13) — past episodes or untimed claims on tokens the lab does
  not hold.
- **On access to bitcoin** (Burniske and Tatar, chapter 15): the Grayscale Bitcoin Trust's first
  trade over the counter in May 2015 at a premium near 100% to its bitcoin, the premium as the price
  of access — a wrapper's premium over its net asset value, **MR-026**'s form, as AS-009-01
  recorded; an analyst's expectation in January 2017 that a bitcoin exchange-traded fund would have
  a profound effect on the price; bitcoin reaching a new high before the fund's denial on 10 March
  2017, falling within minutes after it, and back over 1,200 dollars by the following Monday.

None gives a timed rule that the lab could read.

## What the lab can read of it

The lab holds the daily bars and volumes of twenty-two funds and bitcoin — the funds from 2005 or
their later start, bitcoin from September 2014 — adjusted for dividends and splits, which encode the
funds' distributions, though no strategy reads them until a point-in-time reading with its own test
is built; the Treasury bill's yield from 2005 and two franc exchange rates; the funds' prices, never
their holdings' prices; and no other cryptoasset, no listing calendar and no on-chain series; its
snapshot is closed, an asset or a series being added only before the first card. Nothing was
computed from the bars for this judgement. On those data:

- **Listings of tokens on new exchanges**, the claim and every refutation: a calendar of listings
  across many tokens, of which the lab holds one, traded on exchanges years before its data begin.
- **Bitcoin's listing and access events**: inside bitcoin's in-sample years the library dates about
  eleven — the trust's public trading and the NYSE's bitcoin index in May 2015, the Winklevoss
  brothers' regulated exchange in 2015, the first public funds holding bitcoin through the trust in
  September 2015, a tracker of bitcoin's dollar price in October 2015 and a euro tracker in 2016,
  SolidX's filing in July 2016, the CME's bitcoin indices late in 2016, China's restrictions on its
  exchanges in January 2017, and the denials of the Winklevoss and SolidX funds in March 2017 —
  several of them restrictions or refusals rather than listings; none is dated in the holdout, where
  the library mentions only a Hong Kong proposal of 2023, dated to no day or month, and spot funds
  not at all. A rule that enters and leaves around each makes at most about twenty-three decisions,
  fewer if events fall within five sessions of one another, and some are dated to no month, certain
  to fail its thirty whatever the prices (RUNBOOK step 9); AS-009-01 found the same for the access
  events.
- **The funds' own listings**: the real-estate fund listed in October 2015 but enters the universe
  in September 2016, after its thin early trading, and the communication fund listed and enters in
  June 2018; a strategy sees no price before an asset's first session, so no return around a fund's
  own listing can be read, and the library dates neither listing.
- **A fixed stance on bitcoin** makes one decision.

**The bank's siblings, and how AS-031 differs.** AS-031 claims the price premium and network
activity around a token's listing. **LL-030**, cross-listing and depositary-receipt price discovery,
which prices between the home and the foreign listing rather than the listing's premium; **FP-011**,
index inclusion, the investor-recognition mechanism listings share; **AS-003**, lock-up expirations
after initial public offerings, recorded not testable; **AS-009**, market segmentation, recorded not
testable, which read bitcoin's access events; **AS-028**, vesting and unlocks, recorded not
testable, the supply released after a listing; **AS-032**, exchange clienteles and comovement, what
tokens sharing a venue do together; **AS-044**, decentralised exchanges' design, where tokens such
as Uniswap's list; **MR-026**, closed-end fund discounts, the trust's premium; **MR-038**, crypto's
segmentation across exchanges; **FP-032** and **LL-040**, derivatives and funds as institutional
access; **FP-027**, exchange reserves and flows; all untouched but AS-003, AS-009 and AS-028.

What would make it testable: many tokens with their listing dates on each exchange, prices and
network activity over the lab's years — outside the lab's data — and a timed rule in the library.

## Status

AS-031 is recorded `not-testable`: its claim and every refutation read listings across many tokens,
and the lab holds one, traded on exchanges years before its data begin; the library's listing
premiums are on single stocks, a wrapper's premium or tokens the lab does not hold; bitcoin's
listing and access events are about eleven in-sample, at most about twenty-three decisions, and none
dated in the holdout; the funds' own listings cannot be read. No card is drawn, and no trial is
spent.
