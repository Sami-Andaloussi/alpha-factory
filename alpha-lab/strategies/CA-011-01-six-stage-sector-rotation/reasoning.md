# CA-011-01 — Sector rotation through the six stages of the cycle: reasoning

## Why this theory now

CA-011 holds that sectors differ in their sensitivity to growth, inflation, interest rates, credit
and commodities, so that when the macroeconomic regime changes, flows and valuations move in
sequence between defensive, cyclical, financial, energy, technology and other sectors, over swings
of weeks to months; it persists because regimes are identified slowly, managers are benchmarked by
sector, rebalancing is gradual and transitions are ambiguous. Its refutations: relative sector
returns unrelated to changes of growth, inflation, rates, credit or commodities, or leadership not
passing from sector to sector in sequence; sectors adjusting at once; leadership driven by shocks,
central banks, mega-capitalisations or changes of composition rather than by the regime. Its
references are Murphy's *Intermarket Analysis* (2004), chapters 12 and 13 — whose pages the bank
gives, 199 to 203 and 219 to 228, fall in chapters 13 and 14 of the library's edition — Ilmanen's
*Expected Returns* (2011), chapter 26, and Ang's *Asset Management* (2014), chapter 7.

Murphy's chapter 12 reads the regime from the markets themselves: Pring's six stages of the business
cycle, each marked by a turn of bonds, stocks or commodities; his chapters 7 and 13 give Stovall's
sectors for each phase of the cycle. That pairing — the stage read from three markets' trends, the
sectors held for it — is this theory's own form, and no card has drawn it.

## The sources

The lab read them in the library's books.

- **Murphy** (*Intermarket Analysis*, 2004):
  - Chapter 12, "Intermarket Analysis and the Business Cycle", section "The Six Stages of the
    Business Cycle" (pp. 183–184): Pring's six stages, each a turn of one of three markets — stage
    1, bonds turn up (stocks and commodities falling); stage 2, stocks turn up (bonds rising,
    commodities falling); stage 3, commodities turn up (all three rising); stage 4, bonds turn down
    (stocks and commodities rising); stage 5, stocks turn down (bonds falling, commodities rising);
    stage 6, commodities turn down (all three falling). For asset allocators: bonds or
    interest-sensitive stocks in stage 1, stocks in stage 2, gold as an early inflation hedge in
    stage 3, commodities and inflation hedges in stage 4, commodities, gold and oil shares in stage
    5, cash in stage 6. The sequence is not always followed: markets out of sequence warn that
    "something is amiss". Moore's leads, 1948 to 1990: bonds turn about 17 months before the
    economy, stocks 7, commodities 6; industrial commodities preferred.
  - Chapter 7, "The NASDAQ Bubble Bursts in 2000", section "Sector Rotation within the Economic
    Cycle", after Stovall's *Standard & Poor's Guide to Sector Investing* (1995): early expansion
    led by transportation and technology; late expansion by energy; early contraction by consumer
    staples; late contraction by utilities, financials and consumer cyclicals; energy and staples
    strongest together as a slowdown begins; in 2000, the rotation from energy into staples,
    utilities and financials; and real estate funds, the top group in April 2000 as the Nasdaq fell,
    sought as bear market insurance for their yields ("REITs Hold Up"), "a traditional defensive
    haven".
  - Chapter 14: real estate tied closely to the direction of interest rates, though "not always".
  - Chapter 13, "The Impact of the Business Cycle on Market Sectors": the same cycle as a circle —
    from late expansion to early contraction, energy to staples and utilities, then financials; from
    late contraction to early expansion, consumer cyclicals and technology; the sector rotation
    model, with interest rates falling in recession, bottoming in early recovery, rising in recovery
    and peaking before recession.
  - The trend of a market read by its 200-day moving average: the commodity index rising above it in
    March 1999 as "a sign that its major trend had turned up" (chapter 5); the stock averages
    falling beneath theirs in 2000 (chapter 7).
- **Ang** (*Asset Management*, 2014, chapter 7, "Factors", section 2, macro factors): growth,
  inflation and volatility as the three macro factors; from 1952 to 2011 government bonds did better
  in recessions and low growth, stocks worse, and high inflation hurt both — support for bonds in
  stage 1 and inflation hedges in stages 4 and 5, with no sectors and no timing.
- **Ilmanen** (*Expected Returns*, 2011, chapter 26, "Cyclical Variation in Asset Returns"): risky
  assets' returns high near troughs and low near peaks; small caps outperforming near troughs and
  early expansions; the turnaround in the last third of contractions often a sharp handover from
  defensive stocks to aggressive cyclicals — the rotation, untimed.

## From the claim to a signal

- **The stage.** Each week, on its first session, each of three markets is up if its signal close on
  the session before stands above the mean of its last `average` closes up to that session, read
  whether the fund trades or not: bonds by IEF, Treasury notes of seven to ten years, the bond fund
  CA-014-01 read; stocks by SPY; and commodities by DBC, the lab's commodity basket, which includes
  agriculture where Pring and Moore prefer industrial commodities — the nearest the lab holds. The
  three signs give Pring's stage. Weekly, as Murphy reads 200-day crossings as they happen and as
  CA-014-01 read its ratio.
- **The average**: 200 sessions, Murphy's 200-day moving average.
- **The sectors for each stage**, from Murphy's placing of the stages in the cycle and Stovall's
  sectors for each phase, in the lab's sector funds:
  - stage 1, the economy slipping into recession, bonds or interest-sensitive stocks the place to
    be: consumer staples, utilities and real estate (XLP, XLU, XLRE while it trades), the early
    contraction's leaders and the rate-sensitive defensive havens;
  - stage 2, stocks turning up after the midpoint of the recession: utilities, financials and
    consumer cyclicals (XLU, XLF, XLY), the late contraction's;
  - stage 3, expansion beginning: technology, industrials — which hold the transports — and consumer
    cyclicals (XLK, XLI, XLY), the early expansion's;
  - stage 4, rates rising, commodities and inflation hedges emphasised: energy and materials (XLE,
    XLB), the late expansion's energy with the metals and mining of the materials fund;
  - stage 5, stocks topping: energy and consumer staples (XLE, XLP), "strongest two" as the
    slowdown begins;
  - stage 6, all three falling, the turn into contraction, "from Energy to Consumer Staples and
    Utilities": XLP and XLU. The theory is a relative claim, "not an absolute forecast of the
    market", so the sector reading stays invested.
  The two combinations Pring does not name — bonds and commodities rising while stocks fall, and
  stocks rising alone — are out of sequence, "something is amiss": the sector funds in equal parts,
  no view.
- **The asset classes for each stage**, Murphy's own allocation: IEF in stage 1; SPY in stage 2; SPY
  and GLD in stage 3, gold added as the early inflation hedge; DBC and GLD in stage 4; DBC, GLD and
  XLE, commodities, gold and oil shares, in stage 5; cash in stage 6, "cash is king". Read
  exclusively — the class he names for the stage alone, not the classes he says to de-emphasise
  held less — so that the reading is as sharp as his text.
- **The variants.** The base, "all", holds each stage's sectors with its asset classes, as Murphy's
  own words mix them ("bonds (or interest-sensitive stocks)", "gold and oil shares"); "sectors", the
  sectors alone, the theory's claim, graded; "assets", the asset classes alone. The base's
  structure is CA-014-01's, and for the same reason: a rule holding sectors alone, with the sector
  cluster left out by gate 6, holds nothing, and fails gate 6 whatever the prices (RUNBOOK step 9);
  the graded variant is judged apart from the battery against its own sectors.
- **The universe**: the eleven sector funds, and SPY, GLD, DBC and IEF, which the stage reads and
  the asset classes hold. The battery's benchmark is the fifteen funds in equal parts.
- **The start**: DBC trades from February 2006; its first 200-session mean exists in November 2006,
  the first target on 2006-11-20, a stage-2 week, so that the first target is also the first
  holding.
- **XLV and XLC**, which no source places in the cycle's phases, are held only out of sequence.

**The pace, counted before the card on the signal alone, no return read**: 841 weekly stages from
2006-11-20 to 2022, 157 changes of stage, about ten a year. Stage 3 held 26% of the weeks, stage 2
23%, stage 4 19%, stage 1 13%, stage 5 4% and stage 6 2%; the two out-of-sequence combinations
12%. The neighbours' averages of 150 and 250 sessions give 185 and 132 changes, 100 and 300 give 232
and 109. The count was made after the weekly pace was chosen, for the power and the costs, not to
choose it.

## What the battery judges, and what to expect

**The size predicted.** Murphy's evidence is charts and episodes — 2000, 2003 — not statistics, and
Stovall's sector phases are a model; CA-014-01's sector funds, rotated on two of these trends,
earned an appraisal ratio of 0.04 against their sectors, and the graded variant's average mix alone,
by TM-002-01's published sector alphas, would lose roughly 0.2 to 0.4% a year. The card predicts, as
a judgment, an appraisal ratio of about 0 to 0.2 for the sectors alone against the sector funds in
equal parts, an alpha of about 0 to 2% a year on a tracking error of about 8 to 10% — a rule that
holds two or three sectors at a time strays far from the eleven — and for the base against the
fifteen funds, about 0 to 0.2. Costs: about ten changes of stage a year, each turning over 1.3 to 2
times the portfolio, with the weekly resets: about 0.7 to 0.9% a year at the lab's costs, doubled at
gate 2's test. Gate 4, over some seventy trials, needs an appraisal ratio near 0.9 to 1.0: the card
expects to fail gates 3, 4 and 5, possibly gate 2.

**The clause.** Judged on the variant "sectors", at the lab's costs, by its appraisal ratio against
the sector funds that trade held in equal parts, reset on each session it sets targets and when a
fund starts to trade — the battery's benchmark built on the sector funds alone — over the in-sample
sessions from the first it holds an asset into, computed apart from the battery: the annualised
Sharpe ratio of its daily returns less the benchmark's, hedged of the benchmark's excess returns
with the whole-sample beta. The theory is refuted in this form if that ratio is zero or less: the
sectors Pring's stage names, held in turn, earned nothing over the sectors held always, as the
bank's first refutation reads. Any other result short of the base passing gates 1 to 7 is not
proven, not refuted; a base that passes gates 1 to 7 while the clause refutes leaves the theory
refuted in this form. The second refutation, sectors adjusting at once, is not graded: gate 6's day
of delay reads the speed only coarsely. The third is not graded: the lab cannot tell a shock or an
intervention from a regime.

**The test's power.** Over sixteen years an appraisal ratio is measured to about ±0.25: with no
edge the clause refutes about half the time; at 0.2, the top of the prediction, about one time in
five. The prediction's lower bound, 0, sits on the refutation line.

**Measures stated before the run**, reported, not graded:

- the base's figures, as the battery gives them, and the variant "assets"'s appraisal ratio against
  the fifteen funds;
- the variant "sectors"'s average weights, held fixed and reset weekly, against the same sector
  benchmark, to tell its tilt from its timing;
- each stage's share of the sessions, and the mean daily difference between the variant
  "sectors"'s and its benchmark's returns, annualised, over the sessions held under each stage and
  out of sequence;
- the clause's hedged daily series restricted to the sessions held under one of the six stages, with
  the whole-sample beta, and restricted to 2006 to 2013 and to 2014 to 2022;
- the correlation of the variant "sectors"'s monthly returns hedged of its sector benchmark with
  CA-014-01's variant "stocks" as the registry gives it, hedged of its nine funds, over their common
  months;
- the alpha by year.

**Risks named before the run.**

- **Whipsaw.** Ten changes of stage a year: a trend that crosses its mean back and forth moves the
  whole portfolio each time.
- **Few funds held.** Two to five funds at a time in the base, two or three sectors in the graded
  variant: gate 6 refuses more than 30% of the
  profit in one fund, and energy, held in stages 4 and 5, may carry it.
- **A sequence the lab's years may not follow.** Rates near zero from 2009 to 2015 and 2020 to 2021,
  and bonds and stocks rising together through much of the 2010s, keep stage 2 and stage 3 long
  and the out-of-sequence combinations frequent (12% of the weeks).
- **Gate 6's clusters.** Leaving the sector funds out leaves the base with its asset classes alone,
  cash in stage 6: its Sharpe ratio may fall below half the base's. Leaving the bonds, metals or
  commodities out removes one or two holdings of some stages. Energy, held in stages 4 and 5, may
  carry more than 30% of the profit.

## Choices, and the options rejected

- **The economy's own dates and releases** (CA-013's form): the lab holds no macroeconomic series,
  and the dates are set long after the fact.
- **The yield curve and interest rates** of Murphy's sector rotation model: the lab holds the bill's
  yield, not a long rate; the bond fund's trend stands for the direction of rates.
- **A ratio of two markets** (CA-014-01's form): tested.
- **TLT for bonds**: IEF, as CA-014-01 read, closer to the ten-year note of Murphy's charts.
- **Monthly stages**: Murphy acts on 200-day crossings as they happen, and CA-014-01 read its ratio
  weekly; a monthly pace would lag his reading by weeks.
- **Health care and communication services**: no source in the library places them in the cycle's
  phases; real estate, which Murphy calls a defensive haven moving against rates, joins stage 1.

## Implementation

Long only, fifteen funds, each with a European (UCITS) fund that tracks it, weekly targets filled
at the close of the session on which they are set; gate 6 adds a day's delay.
