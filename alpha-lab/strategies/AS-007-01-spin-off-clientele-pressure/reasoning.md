# AS-007-01 — Institutional clientele pressure in spin-offs: reasoning, not testable as a theory of its own

## The theory, and the forms its sources give it

AS-007 holds that after a spin-off's distribution, the new firm's shares are sold by the
institutions whose size, style, sector, liquidity or governance mandate excludes them, for no
negative information, and that the price stays depressed in the short to medium term until a new
clientele forms; its mechanism extends to any asset that changes its natural holding category. Its
refutations: no abnormal institutional selling and no price depression after the distribution;
sellers that are not those whose mandate excludes the stock; declines matched by worse fundamentals
that do not reverse as the new shareholders arrive. The bank names stocks, weeks and months, and
spin-off distribution dates, institutional holdings, daily prices and analyst coverage. Its
reference, Abarbanell, Bushee and Raedy (2003), appears in the library only in Singal's reading
list.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library.**

- **Pedersen** (*Efficiently Inefficient*, 2015, section 16.2): a spin-off can open imbalances of
  supply and demand as investors re-optimise; the subsidiary often meets selling pressure at first,
  since many holders want the parent, not it, the more so when it is in another industry or when the
  parent is in an equity index and it is not; analysts cover it little at first; the pressure then
  ends, after which the subsidiary may do well over the medium term — a sign on single stocks, with
  no size.
- **Srivastava** (in Tulchinsky and others, *Finding Alphas*, second edition, 2020, chapter 25,
  "Event-Driven Investing"): about fifty spin-offs a year in the US; spun-off firms and parents beat
  the market over the following years, for their focus and a truer valuation, though the new firm
  may show short-term price weakness when it does not fit the parent holders' criteria — a smaller
  capitalisation, limits on small stocks; McConnell, Sibley and Xu (2015) on a spin-off ETF over
  2001 to 2013 are cited without a result.
- **Greenwald, Kahn, Bellissimo, Cooper and Santos** (*Value Investing: From Graham to Buffett and
  Beyond*, second edition, 2022): the spun-off firm is usually small beside its parent; the parent's
  funds, finding it too small to study or to buy, sell it on grounds that have nothing to do with
  its business, so spin-offs "intensify the small company bias"; since Greenblatt (1997) made the
  trade known, new funds pursued it and its returns fell short of his; Klarman lists spin-offs sold
  by managers for materiality and stocks that index funds must sell when they leave an index.
- **Singal** (*Beyond the Random Walk*, 2003, "Long-Term Overperformance Following Spin-offs"):
  spun-off firms earn about 33% more than their peers over three years and parents about 15%
  abnormal returns, but trading strategies built on it do not always work; he explains it by
  corporate focus, only the spin-offs that increase focus outperforming — a rival to the clientele
  reading. On the S&P 500, spin-offs cause deletions, the spun-off firm being in most cases added,
  and his deletion strategy leaves such deletions out.
- **Bali, Engle and Murray** (*Empirical Asset Pricing*, 2016, section 18.1): firms that shrink
  their assets, among them those that spin off units, tend to beat comparable firms afterwards
  (Cusatis, Miles and Woolridge, 1993; McConnell and Ovtchinnikov, 2004); **Chincarini and Kim**
  (*Quantitative Equity Portfolio Management*, 2006) list a spin-off effect for the parent (Cusatis
  and others; Desai and Jain).
- **Miller** (in Fabozzi, ed., *Short Selling*, 2004, chapter 14, "Short Selling and Financial
  Puzzles"): a clientele reading with the opposite sign — spin-offs create wealth as units in
  glamour industries find optimistic buyers, as in six gold-mine spin-offs in 1986.

## What the lab can read of it

The lab holds the daily closes and volumes of twenty-two funds and bitcoin — the funds from 2005 or
their later start, bitcoin from September 2014 — and no single stock, no distribution date, no
institutional holdings and no analyst coverage; its snapshot is closed, an asset or a series being
added only before the first card. On those data:

- **The spun-off stock after the distribution**, the claim and every one of its refutations: the new
  firms, their dates, the institutions that sell and their mandates are not held; the lab cannot see
  a single event of the theory, and every source's sign, both the selling pressure and its opposite,
  is on single stocks.
- **The parent's and the spun-off firm's outperformance afterwards** (Singal, Srivastava, Bali and
  others): single stocks; the cross-sectional form, firms shrinking their assets earning more, is
  **EF-017**'s, the asset growth anomaly, untouched.
- **A spin-off that removes a firm from an index**: a deletion, **FP-011**'s, index inclusion and
  deletion, untouched, though the spun-off firm is in most cases added (Singal).
- **The mandate mechanism generalised, an asset changing its holding category**: the one form the
  library signs on the lab's funds is the Russell reconstitution — additions lagging deletions in
  the July after (Harris), a drag on the Russell 2000 (DeSouza) — which SC-012's judgement left to
  FP-011 by a recorded decision; it is not open here.

**The bank's siblings.** **EF-017**, asset growth; **FP-011**, index inclusion and deletion;
**FP-012**, index rebalancing, and **FP-013**, benchmarking, which share the reconstitution;
**FP-018**, window dressing, which Greenwald discusses beside spin-offs; **MR-015**,
downward-sloping demand curves; **AS-008**, clientele effects; all untouched; **AS-003** and
**AS-005**, other corporate events, and **SC-012**, rebalancing windows and end-of-period flows,
recorded not testable.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue a card:**

- **Small capitalisations against large, IWM against SPY, as the spin-off's clientele discount**:
  Greenwald signs a general discount of small stocks from large funds' size constraints, but as a
  permanent tilt with no timing; a rule holding it would make about one decision, short of gate 1's
  thirty whatever the prices, and the neglected-firm effect behind it is cross-sectional and needs
  analysts' counts.
- **A fund of small capitalisations after waves of spin-offs**: no source gives a count of
  spin-offs, or a sign on a fund, within the lab's years — Srivastava's rate is an average, Miller's
  cluster is 1986.

What would make it testable: the snapshot of a universe of single stocks with their spin-off
distribution dates and institutional holdings — outside the lab's funds — or a spin-off index or
fund, which the library names only by reference (McConnell, Sibley and Xu) and the closed snapshot
does not hold.

## Status

AS-007 is recorded `not-testable`: its claim and every refutation are about spun-off single stocks,
their distribution dates and the institutions that sell them, which the lab does not hold; every
source's sign, the selling pressure (Pedersen, Srivastava, Greenwald) and its reversal (Pedersen,
Srivastava) as well as Miller's opposite reading, is on single stocks; the outperformance after
spin-offs is on single stocks, its asset-shrinkage form EF-017's; a spin-off's deletion from an
index is FP-011's, as is the Russell reconstitution, the one readable form of the mandate mechanism,
by SC-012's recorded decision; the small-stock discount is a static tilt certain to fail gate 1. No
card is drawn, and no trial is spent.
