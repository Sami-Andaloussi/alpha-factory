# SC-012-01 — Rebalancing windows and institutional flows: reasoning, not testable

## The theory, and the form its sources give it

SC-012 holds that a large part of calendar effects comes from forced flows rather than beliefs:
salaries and contributions paid at the turn of the month, interest payments, rebalancing to target
weights, the resetting of risk budgets in January, and the trades index funds must make when an
index is reconstituted. On those dates constrained investors must trade, whatever the price, so that
supply and demand become predictable, and some regularities survive their publication because the
flows behind them continue. It is not one effect but a family, and the bank's refutations name what
sets it apart from the calendar effects it explains: price pressure on the dates of forced trading
that is related to the size of the forced flows; regularities that vanish after publication only
when the flows stop; and moves around index reconstitutions and rebalancing dates larger in the
stocks index funds and benchmarked investors hold.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library's books.**

- Madhavan's study of the Russell reconstitution (a manuscript of 2002, published in 2003 as *The
  Russell Reconstitution Effect*), as Siegel's *Benchmarks and Investment Management* (2003) and
  Harris's *Trading and Exchanges* (chapter 23) report it: the Russell indices are rebuilt at the
  close of the last session of June from capitalisations at the end of May, which anyone can observe
  (since 2004, the last Friday of June, with exceptions, the rule having changed over time, as
  Christopherson, Cariño and Ferson report); from 1996 to 2001 the stocks added to the Russell 3000
  beat those deleted by 15% on average in June, more than 35% from March to June, and lagged them by
  5% in July — a reversal that shows part of the impact transitory, Harris writes. Siegel gives one
  reason for the size, the tininess of the stocks added, and notes a complication: most large-cap
  portfolios are benchmarked to the S&P 500, but a sizable share of small-cap ones to the Russell
  2000, so that a stock moving down into the Russell 2000 gains demand.
- DeSouza, in Tulchinsky and others' *Finding Alphas* (2020, chapter 28), citing FTSE Russell's
  research: buying the additions and selling the deletions on the reconstitution date cost the
  Russell 2000's returns about 0.28% a year from 2007 to 2015, with large deviations from year to
  year, from trading at the reconstitution date; DeSouza reads it as implying that the added names
  revert afterwards; owning IWM costs more than its expense ratio, and funds tracking the S&P 500 or
  the whole market bear less of the drag.
- Christopherson, Cariño and Ferson (*Portfolio Performance Measurement and Benchmarking*, 2009):
  from 2003 to 2008 the deletions from the Russell 2000 beat the additions at every reconstitution,
  according to Lehman Brothers, over a window they do not give, and Cariño and Pritamani (2007)
  found the price impact relatively small once sector and factor returns are adjusted for.
- Siegel, on the S&P 500's inclusion effect: Harris and Gurel found an excess return of 3.1
  percentage points on the announcement day for additions and −1.4 for deletions; one estimate put
  the S&P 500's underperformance from inclusions and deletions at 0.32 points a year from 1992 to
  2002, against an index with no such effect. Index funds pay the cost; arbitrageurs and some active
  managers supply the liquidity and earn it. S&P changes are made essentially ad hoc, by a
  committee.
- Madhavan's *Exchange-Traded Funds and the New Dynamics of Investing* (2016), on the Russell
  reconstitution of June 2007: authorised participants who chose to handle the tracking risk
  themselves redeemed IWM shares nearly equal to its assets, reversed within a few days; the book's
  sentence on the price, garbled in the lab's text, reads as the index staying within 1% of the
  fund's net asset value.
- On the turn of the month's payments and flows, Ogden (1990), Hensel, Sick and Ziemba, McConnell
  and Xu (2008) and the Zacks *Handbook of Equity Market Anomalies* (2011, chapter 9, Japan's salary
  dates among them), which SC-008-01, SC-009-01 and SC-010-02 read; McConnell and Xu found the daily
  flows of equity mutual funds from 1998 to 2005 neither supporting nor ruling out a payday effect,
  and Ilmanen (*Expected Returns*, 2011, §25.3), after them, that mutual fund inflows do not show
  that regularity. Harris (chapter 11) gives year-end bonuses and pension contributions as one
  possible explanation of January, beside December's tax selling; Hasbrouck (*Empirical Market
  Microstructure*, 2007) notes index trades correlated across stocks, with no prediction; Ilmanen
  (§25.2) adds year-end window dressing.

## Why the lab cannot test it

What the theory claims beyond the calendar effects it explains is a link between prices and forced
flows, and each of its refutations needs either the flows or the stocks the flows buy. The lab's
data holds neither. The snapshot holds daily prices of twenty-two funds and bitcoin, the bill rate
and two exchange rates of the Swiss franc: no fund flows, pension contributions, index-fund assets,
index memberships or reconstitution lists, and no single stocks.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue the test:**

- **The calendar windows themselves.** The turn of the month, the month's second-to-last week, the
  first half of the month, the small caps over the second half of December and the turn of the year
  are the lab's cards already, run on the same funds and published: SC-008-01, SC-009-01, SC-010-02,
  SC-002-01 and SC-007-01. Running them again under this theory's name would spend trials on figures
  the lab has, and a window's return cannot tell a forced flow from any other cause. The theory's
  first refutation asks for pressure related to the size of the flows. The sources' proxies for that
  size — Ogden's monetary stringency, December's larger payments, the months that end a quarter —
  are either outside the data (the Fed funds rate) or already read: SC-007-01 found the turn of the
  year no larger than the other turns, and SC-009-01 found the months that end a quarter no weaker
  before their end and the bill's yield unmoved. Only the flows themselves could go further.
- **The Russell reconstitution through the funds the lab holds.** The effect Madhavan measured is on
  the stocks added and deleted, which the lab does not hold. At the fund level the library does give
  a sign and a size: IWM holds the additions and not the deletions from the effective date, so the
  July reversal Harris reports, and the drag DeSouza cites, which he reads as a reversion after the
  date, about 0.28% a year from 2007 to 2015, fall on IWM, and less on SPY; Christopherson, Cariño
  and Ferson report the stock-level effect reversed from 2003 and shrinking. That form — SPY in
  place of IWM after the reconstitution, or over July — is the reversal of the temporary component
  that **FP-011**, the index inclusion effect, names in its own refutations, and FP-012 covers the
  same event; it is left to FP-011, with these sources, and not drawn here. The S&P 500's inclusion
  cost is measured against an index with no such effect, which no price in the snapshot is, and S&P
  changes fall on dates a committee sets, which no calendar gives.
- **Stocks heavily held by index funds against the others**, the third refutation: a comparison
  among stocks, with their index memberships over time.
- **Publication against the continuation of the flows**, the second refutation: it needs the flows'
  history beside the prices, before and after each effect's publication.
- **The resetting of risk budgets in January**, with January's bonuses and contributions: SC-002's
  mechanism, whose December run-up SC-002-01 read on the small caps against the large; Ilmanen reads
  it as risk appetite, strongest in carry, small caps and value, of which the lab holds only IWM.
- **Rebalancing flows at the month's and the quarter's end**: the mandated rebalancing of balanced
  and target-date funds, SPY against TLT after large moves, is FP-023's own theory; year-end window
  dressing FP-018's; the rebalancing of leveraged funds at every close FP-015's, intraday and beyond
  daily closes; recurring pressure at monthly and quarterly frequencies SC-013's.
- **The timing of announcements**, Penman's, which the bank cites: SC-011's. SC-012's reference to
  Ilmanen's §25.2 is FP-018's too.

What would make it testable: a stock universe, which the lab's scope provides for, with its
survivorship bias stated — it would fall mostly on the deletions; the Russell and S&P memberships
over time and their reconstitution dates, which are not prices but a new kind of data, as are
fund-flow and contribution series: decisions for the lab's framing, not for a card.

## Status

SC-012 is recorded `not-testable`: what it claims beyond the calendar effects it explains — price
pressure related to the size of forced flows, regularities that follow the flows, and moves around
reconstitutions larger in the stocks index funds hold — needs fund flows or single stocks with their
index memberships, which the lab's data does not hold. The calendar windows it explains are the
lab's cards SC-008-01, SC-009-01, SC-010-02, SC-002-01 and SC-007-01; the fund-level form the
library gives for the reconstitution, IWM's drag after the Russell reconstitution, is FP-011's and
is left to it. No card is drawn, and no trial is spent.
