# AS-017-01 — Network effects and installed base: reasoning, not testable as a theory of its own

## The theory, and the forms its sources give it

AS-017 holds that in network industries — platforms, software, payments, consoles and network goods
generally — a product's value to a user rises with its installed base and the density of its
complementors, so that the firm with the largest installed base draws more developers, retailers,
content or counterparties, the market tips towards its standard, and its dominance becomes partly
irreversible and path-dependent, until a technological or regulatory shock overturns it. Its
refutations: users' value not rising with the installed base and the complementors; markets not
tipping, the largest installed bases losing share as easily as smaller ones without any shock; the
largest installed base drawing no more complementors than smaller ones. The bank names stocks and
sectors, over years, and installed bases, market shares, complementor counts, margins and daily
prices; its references are Greenwald and Kahn's *Competition Demystified* (2005), held in a 2007
printing, Belleflamme and Peitz's *Industrial Organization*, whose first edition (2010) is the one
the library holds, not the second the bank names, and Porter's *Competitive Strategy* (1980), held
in the 1998 edition.

**Its sources, as the lab read them in the library.**

- **Greenwald and Kahn** (*Competition Demystified*, 2005): the network effect as a product's value
  to a user depending on how many others use it, Microsoft's operating system its example (chapter
  4); eBay's network effect as a variant of economies of scale that did not carry it into Japan
  (chapter 5); a single chip and software standard cutting costs for users and makers alike, so that
  the network effect strengthens both captivity and scale (chapter 6); Nintendo's virtuous circle,
  its installed base drawing outside game writers, undone as each new chip generation reset the
  installed base and as its hard terms with writers and stores sent them to Sega (chapter 15) —
  signed on market shares and profitability.
- **Belleflamme and Peitz** (*Industrial Organization*, 2010, chapters 20, "Markets with Network
  Goods", and 21, "Strategies for Network Goods"): direct and indirect network effects, their tie to
  switching costs, tipping and multiple equilibria in the demand for incompatible goods, standards
  wars won by building an installed base, by backward compatibility and by managing expectations,
  and public policy in network markets; empirical evidence on software prices — spreadsheets
  compatible with Lotus selling at a premium, a 1% larger installed base going with a higher price
  (section 20.1.3) — all on prices, adoption and market shares.
- **Porter** (*Competitive Strategy*, 1980): complementary products in the evolution of industries
  (chapter 8), and rival technologies contending to become the industry standard, the lack of agreed
  standards slowing an emerging industry (chapter 10) — signed on industry structure and growth; no
  installed-base or network-effect form.
- **Others in the library, on single firms' profitability and value**: network effects as the one
  source of advantage that joins a price premium to lower costs, giving increasing returns to scale
  (Koller and others, *Valuation*, eighth edition, chapter 8); Intel's network effects under the
  Intel and Microsoft standard, a health network's local network effects, the natural captivity of
  customers who would have to move together to undermine an incumbent, and first movers displaced by
  the firms that came to dominate their industries (Greenwald and others, *Value Investing*, second
  edition, 2022, chapter 7, with its Table 7.2); industries consolidating into a few winners through
  scale or network effects (Damodaran, *Investment Valuation*, fourth edition, 2025);
  winner-takes-all markets in which expectations for the winner rise as those for the losers
  deflate, with many losers for every winner, and the advice to find first the sectors where network
  effects are intense (Mauboussin and Rappaport, *Expectations Investing*, revised edition, 2021).

**Return signs near the theory.**

- **Ilmanen** (*Investing Amid Low Expected Returns*, 2022): the 2010s kind to US large-cap and even
  more to technology stocks, beside the dominance of the five largest platform firms, which
  investors believed would concentrate monopoly profits through network effects and winner-takes-all
  outcomes — one decade described after the fact, with the warning that the next may differ (chapter
  2, section 2.4); value's worst drawdown, 2007 to 2020 (chapter 6, "Risks and Pitfalls"), the
  outperformance of its last years coming from the market pricing disruptive growth stocks more
  richly ("Current Outlook"), and the belief of some that the shift to a digital world killed the
  value strategy (section 9.2) — a form of **EF-008**, the value premium, on single stocks.
- **Koller and others** (*Valuation*, eighth edition): the belief that the winner takes all driving
  the internet bubble (chapter 1), and investors overpaying for winner-takes-all claims, few digital
  businesses earning monopoly-size returns on capital (chapter 33) — a bubble and a caution, told
  after the fact.
- **Tvede** (*Business Cycles*, third edition, 2006, chapter 18, "Dot Bomb"): network effects and
  Metcalfe's law among the forces of the 1990s boom, before the 2000 crash in technology, telecom
  and internet stocks — one episode before the lab's years.
- **Greenwald and others** (*Value Investing*, second edition, 2022, introduction): technology and
  telecom stocks collapsing in 2000 to 2002, and a new generation of technology stocks giving
  sustained returns since 2009 while value investors lagged — after the fact.
- **O'Shaughnessy** (*What Works on Wall Street*, fourth edition, 2011, chapter 24): information
  technology the lowest compound return of the ten sectors from 1967 to 2009, in portfolios that
  weight their stocks equally — a ranking of past returns with no measure of network effects.
- **On bitcoin**: Glasner (chapter 3 in Caton, ed., *The Economics of Blockchain and
  Cryptocurrency*, 2022), to which AS-015-01 pointed: users' lock-in through network effects
  supporting a money's value against the backward induction that would drive it to zero once its
  future loss of value became clear, the net effect unclear in general; for bitcoin, a positive
  value kept notwithstanding a likely loss of value at some point in the indefinite future, and a
  lead not easily displaced among cryptocurrencies — an untimed claim; Chuen, ed. (*Handbook of
  Digital Currency*, 2015): an adoption curve driven by network effects against a fixed supply,
  running the risk of periodic speculative bubbles and volatility — with no direction or timing.

The library's anomaly and factor books carry no rule that reads network effects or installed bases
on the funds.

## What the lab can read of it

The lab holds the daily bars and volumes of twenty-two funds and bitcoin — the funds from 2005 or
their later start, bitcoin from September 2014 — adjusted for dividends and splits, which encode the
funds' distributions, though no strategy reads them until a point-in-time reading with its own test
is built; the Treasury bill's yield from 2005 and two franc exchange rates; the funds' prices, never
their holdings' prices; and no single stock, installed base, user count, complementor count, market
share or on-chain series; its snapshot is closed, an asset or a series being added only before the
first card. Nothing was computed from the bars for this judgement. On those data:

- **Users' value rising with the installed base**, the claim and first refutation: prices and
  installed bases of products, not held.
- **Tipping and the loss of share without a shock**, the second: market shares over time, not held.
- **Complementors drawn to the largest base**, the third: complementor counts, not held.
- **The shocks that overturn a standard**, the theory's own caveat: the library dates few inside the
  lab's years — the European Commission's inquiry of 2006, its further fine on Microsoft in 2007 —
  the decision itself, of 2004, precedes them — and the HD DVD war that ended in February 2008
  (Belleflamme and Peitz, chapter 16, case 16.5, and chapter 21) — each a single firm's or
  product's, far fewer than gate 1's thirty decisions, and none signed on returns.
- **Bitcoin's network and its price**: Glasner's and Chuen's claims are untimed, so any fixed stance
  on bitcoin makes one decision, certain to fail gate 1; the forms that read the network with data —
  a value growing with the square of users, the ratio of active addresses to a coin's value, user
  counts and the ratio of network value to transactions (Burniske and Tatar, *Cryptoassets*, chapter
  13) — are **EF-037**'s, **EF-038**'s and **EF-046**'s, and need on-chain series the lab does not
  hold.

**The bank's siblings, and how AS-017 differs.** **AS-014**, barriers to entry, the family's
umbrella; **AS-015**, switching costs and customer captivity, which network effects reinforce;
**AS-016**, economies of scale, of which the network effect is the demand-side variant; all three
recorded not testable, the count for any fixed ranking of the sector funds holding here too;
**AS-018**, the experience curve, and **AS-019**, vertical integration, advantages on the supply
side; **AS-020**, two-sided platforms, whose network effects run between two groups of users rather
than within one; **AS-021**, native network tokens; **EF-037**, **EF-038** and **EF-046**, which
read crypto networks with on-chain data, **EF-041**, adoption-driven token valuation, and
**EF-039**, the Lindy effect of surviving networks; **EF-008**, the value premium, which holds
Ilmanen's value form; **EF-004**, franchise value, which names standards and network effects among
the drivers of lasting returns on capital, on single stocks; **LL-013**, technological links, which
reads the returns of firms sharing technologies as a channel of news, not network dominance;
**CA-011** and **CA-013**, sector rotation, which read the sector funds on time-varying signals; all
untouched but AS-014, AS-015 and AS-016.

**What was considered, and why it does not rescue a card:** the sectors the theory names —
technology and communication for software, platforms and networks, financials for payments — as
Mauboussin and Rappaport's advice to start from the sectors where network effects are intense would
suggest, against the others: no source measures a sector's network effects; Ilmanen's decade,
Greenwald's and Tvede's episodes and O'Shaughnessy's ranking are past returns read after the fact,
and they point opposite ways on technology; a fixed tilt to the Nasdaq-100 fund, heavy in the
platform firms, makes one decision; and any fixed ranking of the sector funds makes at most three —
the first holding and the entries of the real-estate fund (September 2016) and the communication
fund (June 2018) — certain to fail gate 1's thirty whatever the prices, so no card is drawn for it
(RUNBOOK step 9).

What would make it testable: firms' installed bases, complementor counts and market shares over the
lab's years, or on-chain series for bitcoin — outside the lab's data — with a return sign for them
in the library.

## Status

AS-017 is recorded `not-testable`: its claim and every refutation read installed bases,
complementors and market shares, which the lab does not hold; its sources sign users' value, prices
and shares; its nearby return signs are past episodes read after the fact, a value form held by
EF-008, and untimed claims on bitcoin; the dated shocks are single firms' and too few; the
network-value forms on bitcoin are EF-037's, EF-038's and EF-046's and need on-chain series; a fixed
tilt to the Nasdaq-100 fund makes one decision and any fixed ranking of the sector funds at most
three, certain to fail gate 1. No card is drawn, and no trial is spent.
