## Mechanism

Companies that increase their number of shares outstanding subsequently earn lower returns. The
classic mechanism is market timing: managers issue shares when the stock is expensive relative to its
fundamental value. A second, behavioural reading holds that the market interprets too favourably the
projects or stories financed by the issues, and does not punish the dilution and the lower future
profitability quickly enough. A net issue of shares is often the symptom either of relative
overvaluation or of a financing need that internal cash flows cannot sustain; in both cases, the
market has overpaid for a capacity to create value that will be revised downwards. Two further
readings, not exclusive of the first, concern the supply of the stock itself and the firm's risk: a
lasting increase in the number of shares requires a broader base of buyers and can create
rebalancing pressure, since the price of a stock depends not only on news but also on the quantity
of paper available, on who holds it and on the frictions with which the market absorbs it; and
issuance often accompanies an investment or expansion policy that changes the firm's risk and its
expected returns. Arbitrage does not erase the effect immediately, because any overvaluation is
hard to measure, because issues fit into credible growth stories, and because investors can
overreact positively to the financing of projects.

Net issuance is the negative mirror of payout yield: a significant part of the aggregate growth in
profits does not come back to existing shareholders, because it is absorbed by new issues or
implicit conversions. What matters to the investor is not the growth of the aggregate profits of the
company or of the market, but the growth per share of the investor's claim on those profits. If
growth requires external capital, the existing shareholder's relative share of the future economy
can be diluted, sometimes massively. The library stresses this point at two levels: in the
cross-section, issuing companies often perform worse afterwards; in aggregate, even fast-growing
economies can deliver low stock returns if growth is captured by new issues rather than by
profitable retention inside already listed companies. It applies to equities, to inflationary crypto
tokens, and to any asset whose supply grows faster than its capacity to capture value. For
cryptoassets, the analogue is a supply that comes into circulation gradually through scheduled
issuance, protocol rewards or unlocks. The theory overlaps with balance-sheet expansion,
acquisitions paid in shares, stock-based compensation, dilution, external financing, investment and
the other corporate-finance anomalies, and the exploitation of overvaluation by managers, without
being reducible to them.

## Prediction

Companies that increase their share count earn lower subsequent returns than companies that do not;
companies that increase it strongly tend, on average, to underperform. The effect has been documented
on US stocks and then internationally, in many stock markets. The horizon is rather medium term,
consistent with a gradual revision of expectations and a slow digestion of the new float. The effect
is clearer on average than case by case, since issues sometimes finance genuinely good projects. In
aggregate, even fast-growing economies can deliver low stock returns when their growth is captured by
new issues; the same dilution concerns inflationary crypto tokens and any asset whose supply grows
faster than its capacity to capture value.

## What would refute it

- Companies that increase their share count earning the same subsequent returns as those that do not.
- Issues that do not follow periods of high valuation relative to fundamentals.
- Returns after issuance that bear no relation to the size of the increase in shares outstanding.
- A price adjustment that takes place entirely when the issue is announced, with no drift over the
  following months.
- Fast-growing economies with heavy new issuance whose stock markets deliver per-share returns in line
  with aggregate profit growth.
- Inflationary tokens whose value per unit keeps pace with the growth of their network although supply
  grows faster than value capture.
