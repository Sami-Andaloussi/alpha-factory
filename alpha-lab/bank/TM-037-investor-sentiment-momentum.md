## Mechanism

Baker and Wurgler (2006) propose a measure of investor sentiment as a predictor of future returns.
Sentiment is defined as the propensity of investors to speculate, and is measured through proxies
such as the volume of initial public offerings, the premium of closed-end funds, market turnover and
inflows into equity funds.

When sentiment is bullish, retail and less sophisticated investors tend to buy risky assets and to
extrapolate recent trends, through the representativeness bias, which creates a short-term momentum
fed by their flows. Anomalies, momentum among them, are stronger when sentiment is high, because the
limits to arbitrage bind more at those times (Stambaugh, Yu and Yuan, 2012, 2014).

By analogy, the mechanism applies to crypto futures, where the share of retail investors with strong
behavioural biases and a strong reaction to sentiment is extremely high. There, the funding rate of
perpetual futures reflects sentiment: a high positive funding rate means that longs pay shorts, a
sign of excess demand for long positions and of very bullish sentiment, and the persistence or
reversal of this sentiment produces momentum signals. By analogy, it can also concern commodity
futures, through positioning data from the Commitments of Traders reports.

## Prediction

Return anomalies, momentum among them, are stronger when sentiment is high than when it is low. When
sentiment is bullish, the risky assets that retail and less sophisticated investors tend to buy show
a short-term momentum fed by their flows. The horizon depends on the sentiment regime: reversals of
sentiment can come quickly, within days or weeks in crypto-assets, or slowly, over months in
equities. Baker and Wurgler document the effect mainly on US stocks.

## What would refute it

Momentum and other anomalies no stronger when sentiment is high than when it is low. Sentiment
proxies, such as IPO volume, closed-end fund premiums, turnover or fund inflows, carrying no
information about subsequent returns.
