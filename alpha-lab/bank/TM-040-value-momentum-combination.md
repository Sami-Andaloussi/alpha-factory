## Mechanism

Combining price momentum with value measures, a low price-to-earnings, price-to-book,
price-to-cash-flow or price-to-sales ratio, or a high dividend yield, earns more than either factor
alone while lowering volatility. The combination joins two orthogonal anomalies. Momentum captures
short-term underreaction, prices that keep rising; value captures long-term mean reversion, prices
abnormally low relative to fundamentals. The two are negatively correlated: momentum stocks tend to
be expensive, and value stocks tend to be recent losers. Combining them therefore diversifies factor
risk. In practice the combination tends to raise absolute returns, to reduce the volatility of the
portfolio, and to avoid both value traps, cheap stocks that keep falling, and momentum bubbles,
expensive stocks whose momentum is about to collapse.

## Prediction

Portfolios that select on both value and momentum outperform portfolios that select on either alone,
with lower volatility. From 1928 to 2009, the "Trending Value" strategy, which combines value and
momentum among small stocks, earned a compound annual growth rate of 15.98% against 10.55% for the
small-stock universe, beat the universe in every rolling ten-year period of the sample (a ten-year
base rate of 100%), and had a volatility of 19.10% against 23.19% (O'Shaughnessy, 2011). Asness,
Moskowitz and Pedersen (2013), cited in Ilmanen (2011), document the same phenomenon across asset
classes. Momentum is measured over 6 to 12 months and value on annual valuations, with portfolios
typically rebalanced every six or twelve months.

## What would refute it

Value and momentum returns that are positively correlated, or a combination that earns no more than
the better of the two factors alone, or with no reduction in volatility. Combined portfolios that
fall into value traps or momentum bubbles as often as single-factor portfolios.
