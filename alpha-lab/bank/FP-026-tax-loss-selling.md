## Mechanism

Some investors sell their losing securities before the end of the tax year to realise a capital
loss, offset capital gains and reduce their tax bill. The result is a selling flow induced by
taxation that puts downward pressure, in the last weeks of the tax year (late December where the tax
year ends with the calendar year), on the securities with the largest losses, which are often small
and less liquid. It is sometimes followed by a later repurchase after the regulatory waiting period,
by a reallocation of capital, or by a normalisation of the price once the tax window has passed, so
that prices rebound at the start of the following year. This is not an abstract calendar effect: it
rests on a category of participants, a precise institutional constraint and a predictable selling
calendar. The sellers are forced or quasi-forced: the marginal seller does not act because of
anything new learned about intrinsic value, but because the tax calendar gives an incentive to sell
now. The logic is close to a relative-value dislocation: a non-informative flow moves the price
temporarily, and the normalisation of the flow brings a reversal.

Because the mechanism rests on an explicit institutional constraint, the taxation of capital gains,
the effect should depend closely on the tax calendar, on whether the holders are taxable, on the
market depth of the stocks concerned and on the size of unrealised losses. It should be stronger
where capital is held by taxable investors and where arbitrage is costly; it seems stronger on
illiquid assets, held by tax-sensitive investors, that have fallen sharply beforehand. It is not
fully arbitraged because arbitrageurs must tie up capital in securities that are often illiquid,
with uncertainty about the size of the flow and the risk that the weakness of the security also
reflects a real deterioration of its fundamentals, and because timing risk, the concentration of the
effect around the year-end and implementation costs limit its elimination. It persists because it is
tied to real tax, accounting, reporting and governance constraints of the calendar and of the
clientele. The size of the effect depends strongly on the tax regime and on the dominant investor
base.

Dimson (1988), Keim and Ziemba (2000), Singal (2003), Shefrin (2008), Ilmanen (2011) and Carver
(2015) present tax-loss selling and window dressing by fund managers as major explanations of the
price distortions around the end of the year, above all in small stocks, illiquid securities and
recent losers; they also stress that neither can be said to suffice everywhere: in some samples
individual investors appear to matter most; in others, institutional constraints and year-end
communication play the key role. Tax-loss selling is associated with the January effect. The
literature has also shown that tax-loss selling is distinct from window dressing and other
institutional calendar effects that act at the turn of the year. By analogy, the mechanism may apply
to crypto-assets where the domestic tax regime treats their capital gains like those on other
assets; this depends on the jurisdiction and on the tax treatment of capital losses.

## Prediction

Selling pressure accumulates before the tax year-end on the securities with the largest losses over
the year, often small and illiquid stocks held by tax-sensitive investors, which fall in the last
weeks of the tax year; the pressure then eases, producing a rebound in early January, or after the
wash-sale period, depending on the jurisdiction. The rebound grows with the prior-year loss, and the
pattern is stronger where holders are taxable and where arbitrage is costly, and it seems stronger
where assets are illiquid. Its timing follows the end of the tax year.

The effect has been documented mainly in equities, notably in small capitalisations and in stocks
that have underperformed strongly. Large losses over the previous year predict a rebound in early
January (Reinganum and others). Studies of individual investors' account data confirm tax-motivated
selling at the end of the year. Tax-loss selling does not explain the whole January effect, however:
past winners can also outperform in January, and markets without a tax structure comparable to that
of the United States sometimes show similar patterns.

## What would refute it

- No additional price weakness in the year's biggest losers ahead of the tax year-end, and no
  year-end selling pressure in stocks with accumulated losses.
- No rebound in those securities once the tax window has passed; a turn-of-the-year rebound that is
  not concentrated in prior losers, or that is unrelated to the size of the prior-year loss.
- A rebound as large in stocks held mainly by tax-exempt investors as in stocks held by taxable
  ones; the same pattern in markets or periods where capital losses bring no tax benefit, or where
  the dominant investors pay no capital gains tax.
- A timing of the pressure that does not follow the tax year-end when it differs across
  jurisdictions, or that does not change when tax rules change.
- No excess selling of losers by taxable individual investors at the end of the year in
  account-level data.
- January outperformance of past winners as large as that of past losers; a turn-of-the-year pattern
  fully explained by window dressing or other institutional calendar effects.
