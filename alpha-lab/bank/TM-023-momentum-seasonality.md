## Mechanism

Momentum is not uniform across the calendar: its strength depends on recurring institutional
frictions, above all tax-loss selling and window dressing. At the end of the year, taxable investors
have a tax incentive to sell their losers and crystallise losses; US investors sell losers in
December, which puts an artificial selling pressure on them. Fund managers have reputational
incentives to show more "presentable" portfolios at reporting dates: before quarter-ends and the
year-end they sell losing positions so that they do not appear in their reports, and keep or buy the
visible winners, then buy the losers back at the start of the next period. These behaviours create
predictable pressures that modulate the profits of momentum. The synchronised flows prolong some
moves before the close of the year and then sometimes favour a rebound of the losers at the start of
the next: in January the selling pressure disappears and these assets rebound towards their
fundamental values.

The mechanism is structural and institutional rather than only psychological: it depends on the
reporting calendar, on the incentives of asset managers, on tax constraints and on the sensitivity
of some participants to the appearance of their portfolio on a given date. It is not a mere seasonal
curiosity but a structural consequence of taxation, reporting and agency constraints. It is a
concrete form of flow inertia, and it shows how constraints external to the fair price can sustain
the continuation of winners and the temporary sinking of losers. It persists because the incentives
return every year and are tied to institutional obligations that are hard to neutralise. The theory
does not seek to explain all of momentum, but a large part of its seasonality, especially around the
year-end and January. The books hold that this explanation counts but does not suffice on its own to
explain intermediate momentum as a whole: it is a partial theory, a modulation of momentum by
calendar, which says when a momentum signal is likely to be reinforced and when it is exposed to a
rebound of losers. Continuation depends on the participants' calendar, not only on information.

In commodity futures, seasonality has a different nature: it follows natural cycles of production
and consumption, such as those of natural gas, driven by winter heating and summer cooling. These
seasonal trends are predictable and can amplify or counter pure momentum signals. By analogy, in
futures more generally the calendar acts through rebalancing flows, the dressing of institutional
exposures and the tax calendar.

## Prediction

Momentum is systematically weakened or reversed in January compared with other months: recent losers
rebound in January, which hurts the short side, and recent winners underperform (Jegadeesh and
Titman, 2001). Conversely, the continuation of winners is reinforced around quarter-ends and the
year-end. The effect is documented mainly in equities, with a marked importance of January. The
seasonal horizon is monthly for the January effect, and quarterly or annual for the agricultural and
energy cycles that shape commodity seasonality (Fabozzi, Füss and Kaiser, 2008).

## What would refute it

Momentum returns in January no different from those in other months, and no rebound of past losers
at the turn of the year. No reinforcement of winners before quarter-ends and the year-end. Seasonal
patterns in momentum that persist in markets or periods without tax-loss incentives or reporting
dates. Commodity momentum signals unaffected by the production and consumption cycles of the
commodity.
