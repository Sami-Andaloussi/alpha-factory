## Mechanism

The seasonal affective disorder (SAD) theory, or "winter blues", links the seasonality of returns to seasonal variations in mood and in risk aversion caused by the shortening of daylight, especially in autumn and winter. The idea is that, in regions where the length of the day varies strongly over the year, a large enough share of investors or intermediaries becomes more risk-averse as the days shorten, which raises the risk premia they require and weighs on prices.

The mechanism is behavioural but meant to be economically structured: if risk aversion varies with the season, expected returns must vary too. More broadly, it belongs to a more behavioural group of calendar effects, less structural than the effects of salary payments or taxation, in which appetite for risk varies with collective mood, itself influenced by light, by the season and possibly by some biological rhythms, a group the books theorise as a case in which exogenous changes in sentiment can produce different average returns at recurring dates or periods. Authors point to a possible link with the Sell in May pattern or with a seasonal taste for risk.

The theory can shed light not only on aggregate market patterns but also on some cross-sectional seasonalities, if some stocks are more sensitive to mood than others; it thus resonates with work on mood beta.

## Prediction

Prices are weighed down while the days shorten in autumn and winter, as risk aversion and required risk premia rise, so that expected returns vary with the season. The effect concerns regions where the length of the day varies strongly over the year, and its empirical signature is a difference between the northern and the southern hemispheres. Stocks more sensitive to mood can show stronger seasonal patterns in the cross-section.

Where it has been observed: it has been tested in international equity markets, with particular attention to the differences between the northern and the southern hemispheres.

## What would refute it

- No seasonal pattern in returns related to the length of the day.
- The same seasonal timing in the northern and southern hemispheres, which would point to calendar causes such as taxes or holidays rather than daylight.
- An effect no stronger in regions where the length of the day varies strongly over the year than in the others.
- A pattern fully accounted for by the other seasonal forces that move with the calendar (taxation, holidays, climate, information, monetary policy), which is the reading of those who see the theory as a behavioural over-interpretation of composite seasonal effects.
- No seasonal variation in the risk aversion of investors themselves, or a pattern that does not survive outside the samples in which it was first found.
