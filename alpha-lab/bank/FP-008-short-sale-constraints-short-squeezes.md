## Mechanism

When opinions diverge but short selling is constrained, pessimistic investors cannot fully express
their information. Prices then reflect the optimists more than the average consensus, which can
lead to overvaluation. The price depends on who is able to take a position, not only on who is
right. The books link the mechanism to the cost of borrowing shares, to relative short interest, to
the dispersion of opinion, and to regulatory or institutional restrictions. It persists because
shorting is costly, because some institutions are long-only in practice, and because the most
overvalued securities are often precisely the hardest to borrow. Prices can thus sit at levels
propped up by the inability of the bearish view to be expressed.

The same structure extends to the short squeeze. If a stock is heavily shorted and the float that
can be mobilised shrinks, a rise in price or a tightening of the supply of lendable shares can force
short sellers to buy back, which feeds the rise. The squeeze is structural, not only behavioural: it
involves leveraged shorts, borrowing constraints, variable borrowing costs, margin calls, risk
limits, and sometimes a clientele of long-only investors that refuses to lend its shares. Aggressive
buyers can exploit this structure. Short covering creates a particular asymmetry: the potential
sellers disappear precisely when the price rises, since the rise weakens those who were carrying the
selling pressure. By analogy, in crypto, the squeeze often runs through the liquidation of short
positions in derivatives, whose impact spills over into the spot market.

Short constraints are not smooth transaction costs: they depend on the stock of shares available to
borrow, institutional rules, the ownership structure and the market cycle. The mechanism is close to
ownership fragility, retail attention and crowding episodes; squeezes, predatory trading and some
crowding bubbles follow the same logic, whose core is the asymmetry between the ability to buy and
the ability to short.

## Prediction

Stocks with strong short-sale constraints and high disagreement among investors can be overvalued,
and they tend to underperform afterwards; the correction of the overvaluation can be delayed for a
long time. In heavily shorted stocks whose available float shrinks, a price rise or a tightening of
the supply of lendable shares can force short sellers to buy back, which feeds the rise; squeezes
can produce violent moves over short horizons. By analogy, in crypto, squeezes often run through
liquidations of short derivative positions, whose impact carries over to the spot price.

## What would refute it

- Stocks with high borrowing costs, high relative short interest and high dispersion of opinion
  performing no worse afterwards than comparable unconstrained stocks.
- Overvaluation no more frequent where short selling is restricted than where it is free.
- Sharp rises no more frequent among heavily shorted, hard-to-borrow stocks than among others.
- In heavily shorted stocks with a shrinking float, price rises or a tighter supply of lendable
  shares never followed by short covering.
