## Mechanism

Trend profits do not come from any magic in the signal but from the fact that certain environments
produce a lasting divergence between assets, sectors or pockets of risk, and between possible states
of the world. When a crisis, a public intervention, a wave of deleveraging or a macroeconomic break
pushes prices apart persistently, trend-following strategies are structurally placed to capture this
divergence: they are "long market divergence". For Greyserman and Kaminski, trend followers are
divergent risk takers: they accept many small losses to catch a few large gains, so their
performance depends on the quality of the signal relative to noise and on the degree of divergence
across markets.

Greyserman and Kaminski measure divergence as the distance of a market from its reference level,
normalised by its volatility, for instance (current price minus a long moving average) divided by
historical volatility, a variant of normalised momentum that makes trends comparable across assets;
a high divergence indicates a strong momentum relative to the asset's usual volatility. A market
with strong positive divergence is in a clear uptrend; a market with weak divergence oscillates
without direction. In their framework, tradability separates markets whose trends can be captured
(strong autocorrelation, low noise relative to signal) from markets whose trends are too short, too
noisy or too costly to trade. When spot moves, roll, microstructure and noise offset each other, a
trend may exist in the raw price history without being tradable. In futures markets, diversification
across markets is native and the trend can be decomposed into spot, roll, sectors and risk
allocations. Their market divergence index formalises the idea, and the dispersion of returns across
the assets of a universe is a key determinant of trend-following profits; dispersion is particularly
high during crises and strong macroeconomic cycles.

Crisis alpha follows from divergence; it is a structural property that derives directly from the
mechanics of trend following. In a crisis, markets go through powerful and prolonged trends, and
trend-following strategies, being long the rising assets and short the falling ones, naturally
position themselves in the direction of these trends; the stronger and more persistent the trends,
as is typical of prolonged crises, the larger the gains. In bad episodes for traditional assets,
trends become sharper, forced sales more violent and safe havens more sought after. Under stress,
correlations, liquidity, margin calls and balance-sheet constraints change participants' behaviour
abruptly: moves become more directional in some assets, divergence between sectors and asset classes
rises, herding intensifies as investors sell risky assets at the same time, creating prolonged and
powerful downtrends that trend followers capture, and inflexible or long-only participants often
adjust late. A system that cuts losses, can sell short and follows trends across many markets can
then capture a behavioural and structural convexity that traditional portfolios lack. The convexity
of trend-following payoffs with respect to the equity market is the key property: the profile
resembles a long option on directional volatility. Even without fine psychological biases, a
financial system under balance-sheet constraints can produce long directional moves. In the
framework of the adaptive markets hypothesis and of a speculative risk premium, these episodes
reward a rare capacity to carry and manage the risk of divergence when many participants cannot.

## Prediction

Trend-following returns rise with market divergence and with the dispersion of returns across
assets: when divergence increases, especially in extreme regimes, the conditional performance of
trend following improves strongly, and in flat markets with little dispersion there is no signal to
capture. The most profitable episodes are often periods of stress or regime change rather than calm
markets. Trend following tends to earn positive, or strongly decorrelated, returns during crises,
precisely when long equity, carry and value strategies suffer their worst drawdowns: in 2008,
equities fell, the dollar rose, oil fell and bonds rallied, all as prolonged trends. Greyserman and
Kaminski (2014) show that trend-following strategies generated positive returns in almost all the
major market crises of the last century: 1929 to 1932, 1937 to 1938, 2000 to 2002 and 2008 to 2009;
they document the property over several centuries in some markets. The effect plays out over months
to a few years, the length of a crisis, and is incompatible with very short, intraday horizons. In
Greyserman and Kaminski's framework, the mechanism also explains why multi-market programmes are
more robust than signals on a single contract, and why portfolio construction matters as much as the
raw signal.

Crisis alpha is conditional, not a guarantee of gain in every market fall: it appears only in real,
prolonged crises in which trends actually form. The authors stress that it is not the same as being
purely long volatility, that it can be absent in a crash that is too abrupt or a violent rebound
without a trend, and that it is neither automatic nor permanent. In range-bound markets, or in
markets rising steadily without directional volatility, trend following underperforms significantly.

## What would refute it

Trend-following returns unrelated to market divergence or to the cross-asset dispersion of returns.
Trend following losing during most prolonged crises, or doing no better in stress periods than in
calm ones. Crises in which prolonged trends form across equities, bonds, currencies and commodities
without trend followers profiting. Returns without convexity with respect to the equity market.
Single-contract signals as robust as diversified multi-market programmes.
