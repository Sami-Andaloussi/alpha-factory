## Mechanism

Different types of investors have neither the same tax constraints, nor the same income needs, nor
the same mandates. A stock therefore attracts a particular shareholder base according to its dividend
policy, the stability of its payout and sometimes its legal structure: stocks that distribute a high
yield are not held by the same clientele as stocks that retain most of their earnings.

The mechanism is specific to the structure of each stock's investor base rather than a mere return
pattern, and it creates recurring price behaviour peculiar to certain firms or sub-universes. The
inefficiency persists because the frictions of tax, regulation, mandate and preference cannot be fully
arbitraged. The effect weakens when the differences between clienteles narrow or when the cost of
rotating a shareholder base falls.

## Prediction

Stocks are held by distinct clienteles according to their payout policy, the stability of their
payout and sometimes their legal structure, and the prices of the firms and sub-universes concerned show
recurring behaviour tied to those clienteles. The effect is larger when the tax, income and mandate
differences between investor types are large and when the cost of rotating the shareholder base is
high.

## What would refute it

- The shareholder base of a stock does not vary with its dividend policy, the stability of its payout
  or its legal structure: high-payout and high-retention stocks are held by the same types of investors.
- Stocks with different payout policies show no recurring price behaviour tied to their clientele.
- The effect is no weaker where the tax, regulatory and mandate differences between investor types are
  small or where the costs of rotating a shareholder base are low.
