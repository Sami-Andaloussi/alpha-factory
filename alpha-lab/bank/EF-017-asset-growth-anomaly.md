## Mechanism

Strong growth in total assets predicts low future returns, and the literature treats its mirror
image, weak asset growth, as a signal of more favourable ones. The mechanism can be read in two
ways. In the behavioural reading, the market extrapolates the expansion of the balance sheet too
favourably, whether it comes from acquisitions, organic investment or external financing, and
underestimates the decline in the marginal return on capital. In the agency reading, managers
overinvest when the market cost of capital is too low or when governance constraints are weak, and
shareholders later pay for that overinvestment through mediocre stock returns.

Companies that deploy a lot of capital at high speed often end up taking on marginal projects of
lower quality, overpaying for acquisitions, or pursuing empire building rather than returns.
Investors easily confuse asset growth with value creation, whereas high asset growth often reflects
a heavy need for capital, dilution to come or a falling marginal return. Asset growth aggregates
several neighbouring effects: security issuance, capital expenditure, acquisitions, the accumulation
of low-return assets and empire building. Exceptionally profitable investment protected by a moat
also exists, but it is rarer than destructive investment. The respective weights of overreaction and
of risk in the effect are debated.

## Prediction

Firms with high asset growth earn lower subsequent returns than firms with low asset growth. The
effect has been documented on US stocks and then in other markets. Its natural horizon is one year
to several years, because it takes time for the market to realise that the growth in assets has not
created as much value as expected. The effect is also tied to market conditions: it can be stronger
in environments of optimism, weak capital discipline and low attention to cash flows. The effect
concerns in particular universes where growth narratives dominate.

## What would refute it

- No negative relation between asset growth and subsequent returns, in the United States or
  elsewhere.
- An effect no stronger in periods of optimism, weak capital discipline and low attention to cash
  flows than in other periods.
- An effect absent in universes where growth narratives dominate.
- Lower returns after high asset growth that are matched by correspondingly lower risk, with no sign
  of extrapolation or overinvestment.
- High asset growth followed, on average, by marginal returns on capital that do not decline.
