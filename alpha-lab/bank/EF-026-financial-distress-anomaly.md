## Mechanism

Companies closest to financial distress often show abnormally low future returns despite their high
apparent risk, against the naive intuition that more risk means more return. The dominant behavioural
interpretation is that distressed stocks look like lotteries: a low price, a large perceived upside
and the possibility of a spectacular turnaround. They attract investors inclined to overpay for the
option of survival. Frictions on short selling and analyst optimism aggravate the overvaluation. A
weakened balance sheet, high leverage and a higher probability of default should normally discipline
the valuation, yet the market can keep prices too high on "recovery stories".

Distress also explains why some stocks look cheap only because they carry a very high risk of
distress, of failed refinancing or of permanent loss of capital. When leverage is too high, a small
fall in profits or a liquidity shock can transfer almost all the residual value to creditors, or
force the company to issue capital on bad terms. Distressed stocks can therefore trade at very low
multiples without being genuine value opportunities. Conversely, some discounts reflect excessive
fear rather than near-certain insolvency, depending on the structure and maturity of the debt and
the variance of enterprise value. The library also shows that the equity of a distressed firm
sometimes resembles a deep out-of-the-money option: very asymmetric, very volatile, and sensitive to
a lengthening of the maturity of the debt.

The phenomenon is not fully arbitrageable because credit information is complex, refinancing depends on
market conditions, and the line between severe cyclicality and bankruptcy keeps shifting.

## Prediction

The stocks of the companies closest to financial distress often earn abnormally low subsequent
returns, despite their high apparent risk. The effect is observed on equities, particularly in
small-capitalisation, speculative or costly-to-arbitrage segments. Distressed stocks can trade at
very low multiples without being genuine value opportunities, and some discounts reflect excessive
fear rather than near-certain insolvency, depending on the structure and maturity of the debt and
the variance of enterprise value.

A contrary literature attempts to reconcile part of these results with structural models of risk and
of residual shareholder value.

## What would refute it

- Distressed stocks that earn returns in line with, or above, what their risk would warrant.
- Low returns of distressed stocks fully explained by structural models of risk and residual
  shareholder value.
- Low returns that are as large among large, liquid, cheaply shorted stocks as among small,
  speculative and costly-to-arbitrage ones.
- Discounts that the structure and maturity of the debt and the variance of enterprise value mark as
  excessive fear, which end in insolvency as often as the others.
