## Mechanism

The experience curve adds an essential nuance to economies of scale: what matters is no longer only
the current size of the firm but its cumulative experience. If producing, distributing or serving more
lets a firm learn, improve its processes, reduce waste and refine its organisation, its costs fall
over time.

The mechanism becomes a real moat only if this learning is at least partly proprietary, hard to copy
or to hire away. Not every experience curve is therefore a durable barrier. The mechanism concerns
manufacturing, technology and logistics industries where learning turns into a defensible cost
advantage. If knowledge spreads quickly, or if the technology is abruptly renewed, the advantage
collapses.

## Prediction

In industries where learning is at least partly proprietary, the firms with the largest cumulative
output have falling unit costs and a lasting cost advantage over their followers. The advantage
collapses when the knowledge spreads quickly or when the technology is abruptly renewed.

## What would refute it

- Unit costs do not fall with cumulative output.
- In industries where learning is proprietary, the leaders in cumulative output have no cost advantage
  over their followers.
- The leaders' cost advantage persists unchanged after the knowledge has spread or the technology has
  been renewed.
