## Mechanism

A disproportionate share of equity returns is concentrated around the end of the month and the first days of the next. The window varies from study to study, but it often runs from the last trading day of the month to the first three trading days of the next one. Outside this short window, average returns are low or negative.

The mechanism is debated, and the effect may be the meeting point of several flows synchronised in time rather than a single mechanism. The explanations put forward start with a predictable inflow of liquidity. The economic idea documented in the books is that payments of salaries, contributions and retirement contributions, interest payments, institutional purchases and other cash flows cluster around the end and the start of the month, and that part of these flows goes into equities and equity index futures; the books tie the effect to periodic institutional and payroll flows, to interest payments and to the calendars on which cash is deployed. They also include institutional rebalancing, month-end cash constraints, clustered macroeconomic news and reporting behaviour. Mechanisms of information release and of behaviour add to these: bad news sometimes delayed at the end of the month, better news more readily released at the start of the month, and window-dressing and investment-timing practices.

The effect persists because the flows that generate it are themselves institutionally periodic. Payroll, contribution and accounting calendars are not arbitraged at the pace of prices; they rest on deep institutional infrastructure. Even when the effect is known, arbitraging it at a significant size requires capital, repeated execution and acceptance of the risk of a regime shift. The books note, however, some anticipation: once the effect is known, part of the return slides to the preceding days; they also report a partial anticipation in futures, and a tendency of the effect to move slightly earlier once it is known.

## Prediction

Equity returns are concentrated in a short window around the turn of the month, often from the last trading day of a month to the first three trading days of the next, and are low or negative on average outside it; a very large share of the historical monthly return of equities was concentrated on these few days. The effect does not appear to be limited to small capitalisations or to stocks with low nominal prices, which sets it apart from some versions of the January effect. As it becomes known, part of the return slides to the days just before the window.

Where and when it has been observed: over long periods and in many countries. Several books show that it is not limited to the United States and that it appears in other markets too, sometimes with the window shifted according to local habits of salary payment and other local mechanisms. It has been confirmed on cash indices and on S&P 500 and Russell 2000 index futures, and examined over a long history of the S&P 500 and in an emerging stock market; the literature shows that it has been found again in many markets and re-tested in more recent contexts, including some major cryptocurrencies.

## What would refute it

- Returns over the last trading day and the first three trading days of the month no higher than on the other days of the month.
- An effect confined to small capitalisations or to low-priced stocks.
- No market in which the window shifts with local salary-payment dates.
- No concentration of salary, contribution and institutional cash flows into equities at the turn of the month, or turn-of-the-month returns unrelated to the size of these flows.
- An effect that stays in place in periods and markets where payroll and contribution calendars are spread evenly across the month.
- Turn-of-the-month returns that disappear once other nearby calendar effects are controlled for.
