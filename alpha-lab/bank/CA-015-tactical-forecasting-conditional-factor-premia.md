## Mechanism

The theory holds that expected returns and factor premia are not constant: they depend on the
information available at each date, on the point reached in the business cycle, on the mix of growth
and inflation, on the level of valuations and on the market's capacity to bear risk. Relative value
then becomes a question of conditional allocation between asset classes, styles, factors, sectors
and countries: instead of value, momentum, low risk or illiquidity always earning the same price of
risk, their relative rewards vary with the regime.

The variation persists because macroeconomic signals diffuse slowly, mandates are rigid, benchmarks
create inertia, and many investors do not adjust their factor exposures dynamically.

## Prediction

Variables observed at a given date (the phase of the cycle, the mix of growth and inflation, the
level of valuations, the market's capacity to bear risk) predict the relative returns of asset
classes, styles, factors, sectors and countries, and the premia earned by value, momentum, low risk
and illiquidity differ from one regime to another. The effect plays out at monthly to quarterly
horizons, and rarely intraday. It can weaken as investors crowd into the same positions.

## What would refute it

- Factor premia and the expected returns of asset classes are constant across regimes: the premia of
  value, momentum, low risk and illiquidity do not vary with the cycle, the mix of growth and
  inflation, valuations or the capacity to bear risk.
- The conditioning variables do not predict relative returns out of sample.
- The predictable variation disappears as investors crowd into the same conditional positions.
