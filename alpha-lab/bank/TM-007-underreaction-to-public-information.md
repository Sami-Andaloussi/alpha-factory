## Mechanism

Underreaction is the most general behavioural core of momentum. When economic, accounting or
operational news is published (announcements of earnings, dividends, share buybacks or other
fundamental signals), investors do not update their beliefs with the intensity a Bayesian benchmark
would require: they adjust their beliefs too slowly and underweight the new information relative to
their prior beliefs. Prices do not adjust immediately to their fair value: they move in the right
direction, but not fast enough, and the market stays temporarily behind the fundamentals. The later
continuation is simply the rest of the adjustment: a predictable drift, over several weeks or
months, in the direction of the information. As a result, "stale" public information keeps
predictive power for future returns, which is incompatible with an instantaneous and complete
incorporation of information into prices. The mechanism is the bridge between a real improvement in
fundamentals and its delayed translation into prices.

The psychological form cited most often is conservatism, formalised by Edwards (1968): people give
too little weight to new data and cling to their earlier beliefs, so investors absorb slowly the
implications of good or bad news for a stock, a sector or an asset. In the framework presented by
Shleifer, this underreaction comes in part from conservatism: investors believe too much in the
stationarity or the reversion of earnings, and do not recognise quickly enough that some good or bad
news has persistent content. Anchoring contributes too: investors start from a level or a prior and
adjust insufficiently. The cause may also be inattention, distraction, the difficulty of processing
ambiguous signals or the use of overly rigid mental models. Barberis, Shleifer and Vishny (1998)
formalise the belief dynamics with two cognitive regimes, a mean-reverting model and a trending model
of the world, between which investors switch; an investor who, out of excess conservatism, is in the
mean-reverting regime underreacts to good news, and the positive drift persists. The hypothesis of
slow information diffusion (Hong and Stein, 1999) adds a complementary, structural explanation:
information does not reach every investor at once but first the best informed (institutions, parties
close to the issuer) and then spreads progressively.

Underreaction is not incompatible with overreaction at longer horizons: a series of good news can
first be under-incorporated and later extrapolated to excess. The two are not mutually exclusive and
coexist at different horizons, and the same market can first underreact and then overreact, which is
why momentum and long-run reversals coexist. The model of Barberis, Shleifer and Vishny explains both
through the dynamics of investors' beliefs.

The mechanism unifies several phenomena and serves as their conceptual basis: price momentum, the
post-earnings announcement drift, the continuation after analyst revisions of earnings forecasts,
some drifts after corporate actions, and some effects linked to share repurchases or to liquidity
shocks. Even without earnings, the slow incorporation of macroeconomic information, of supply and
demand constraints, of regime changes or of flows follows the same momentum logic. The frictions
that keep it alive are the cost of access to information, the heterogeneity of processing capacities
and the limits of attention. Rational arbitrageurs could exploit it, but arbitrage requires patient
capital, tolerance of interim losses and often the ability to sell short or to bear a high tracking
error; these capacities are limited, and transaction costs and short-term market risk limit the
arbitrageurs' intervention.

## Prediction

After public news, prices drift in the direction of the news over the following weeks and months, at
short to medium horizons of 1 to 12 months, and public information that is already stale predicts
returns. The drift fades at long horizons, where overreaction takes over and produces mean reversion
over 2 to 5 years: underreaction at 3 to 12 months (momentum), overreaction at 2 to 5 years (mean
reversion).

The phenomenon is observed mainly on stocks, around earnings announcements, analyst revisions and
other public events, and more generally in portfolios formed on past returns. It is documented on
stocks, corporate bonds, commodities and futures markets, and is particularly visible around
earnings announcements (the post-earnings announcement drift), analyst revisions and, in futures
markets, macroeconomic shocks. Momentum is often stronger when information arrives gradually, when
stocks have little analyst coverage, or when the news strongly contradicts the dominant narrative;
Hong, Lim and Stein (2000) show empirically that momentum is stronger for stocks with low analyst
coverage, where diffusion is slower.

## What would refute it

- Prices that incorporate public news (earnings, dividends, buybacks and others) fully at release,
  with no drift in the direction of the news over the following weeks and months; stale public
  information that carries no predictive power for returns.
- No stronger drift among stocks with little analyst coverage or when information arrives
  gradually.
- Investors who update too strongly rather than too weakly on the release of news with persistent
  content, producing reversals rather than drifts in the following months.
- No momentum-then-reversal sequence: returns that do not continue over three to twelve months, or
  continuation after news that persists at 2 to 5 years instead of giving way to reversal; long-run
  reversals that appear without any earlier underreaction.
- Drifts that disappear once transaction costs and short-sale constraints are removed, with no
  remaining trace of slow belief updating.
