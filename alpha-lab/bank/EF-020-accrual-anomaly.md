## Mechanism

Not all earnings are worth the same: the cash component of earnings is more persistent than the
accrual component. If investors "fixate" on total earnings without separating accruals from cash
flows, they overvalue companies whose current earnings are inflated by high accruals and undervalue
those whose earnings rest more on cash flows. When the error corrects, future returns reflect the
difference in persistence. An increase in earnings driven by high accruals often contains a temporary
component, tied to working-capital needs, to the timing of accounting recognition, or to adjustments
that will reverse later: the mechanism is a mean reversion of fundamentals inside the income
statement and the balance sheet.

The cause is twofold. Accruals give managers a latitude, legitimate or opportunistic, to smooth,
accelerate or delay the recognition of some components of earnings. And the market, focused on
earnings per share, underestimates the lower persistence of that "accounting" part of earnings and
too often extrapolates it as if it had the same quality as the rest. The accounting information is
not badly "received", in the sense of an event, but badly "interpreted", in terms of the future
persistence of earnings. The error need not be gross: it can come from investors' limited capacity
to separate the persistent and transitory components of earnings. The more "abnormal" or unreliable
the accruals, the stronger the later reversal of earnings and the more excessive the initial price
is likely to be. The logic is general: when a performance measure mixes a durable component and a
reversible one, the market can overpay for the temporary part. The theme overlaps with earnings
quality, net operating assets and earnings management.

The effect persists because the information is public but costly to process correctly, because
delegation favours simple heuristics, and because investors can have incentives to focus on
headline metrics.

## Prediction

Firms with high accruals earn lower subsequent returns than firms with low accruals, as the accrual
component of earnings reverses and prices adjust, over a natural horizon of about six to twenty-four
months. The effect was observed on US stocks and then widely studied elsewhere; it is documented
mostly on equities, at quarterly to annual horizons. It is often stronger among small, less liquid
and less followed stocks, and among those more costly to arbitrage; the more abnormal or unreliable
the accruals, the stronger the later reversal of earnings and the more likely the initial price is
to be excessive. The library notes that after publication the anomaly was traded more heavily and
its raw premium appears to have declined; part of the literature stresses attenuation out of sample
and a partial absorption of the signal by professional investors.

## What would refute it

- Accrual and cash components of earnings that turn out equally persistent.
- No return difference between high- and low-accrual firms over six to twenty-four months, prices
  weighting accruals and cash flows correctly.
- No stronger effect for abnormal or low-reliability accruals.
- High-accrual firms whose earnings do not subsequently revert.
- A spread no larger among small, illiquid and little-followed stocks than among large, liquid ones.
- A spread that disappears entirely out of sample.
