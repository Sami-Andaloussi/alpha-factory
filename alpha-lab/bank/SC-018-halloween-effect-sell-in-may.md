## Mechanism

The Halloween effect, or "Sell in May and go away", is the regularity by which equity returns have historically been higher from November to April than from May to October: most of the return on equity risk is earned between November and April, while May to October is structurally weaker. It is a half-year seasonality rather than the effect of an isolated month. It works at a slower frequency than intra-week or intra-month effects, so it can capture broader macroeconomic, institutional and behavioural clocks. The books treat it as more macro-calendar in nature than the January effect. Bouman and Jacobsen found it in their international studies.

No single explanation has prevailed definitively. The optimism cycle hypothesis of Doeswijk holds that towards the end of the year investors become excessively optimistic about the year ahead, which supports winter returns, and that a gradual return to realism then weakens returns over the summer. The books also treat it as possibly linked to the January effect by a common risk driver: appetite for risk is high around the New Year, is then anticipated by the market, and weakens during the summer and autumn. Other explanations point to a seasonal variation in the risk premium or in risk itself; to the annual cycle of economic activity and investment; to summer holidays, lower participation, lower market activity (the "summer doldrums") and holiday and liquidity behaviour; to the budget and political calendar; to seasonal mood; to a change, over the year, in the composition of the investors who are active; and to the accumulation of weak episodes in September and October.

## Prediction

Equity returns from November to April exceed those from May to October.

Where and when it has been observed: over long historical samples and in many equity markets internationally, on cash indices and on S&P 500 and Russell 2000 index futures. On Bitcoin, tests generally do not find a Halloween effect.

## What would refute it

- Returns from November to April no higher than returns from May to October, in particular in samples other than those in which the effect was first found.
- A winter outperformance that disappears once dividends and the interest earned on cash are accounted for.
- No seasonal cycle in investor optimism, risk appetite, risk premia or market participation matching the half-year pattern, which would remove the proposed mechanisms.
