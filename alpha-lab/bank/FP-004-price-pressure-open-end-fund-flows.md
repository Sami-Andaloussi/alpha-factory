## Mechanism

Subscriptions to and redemptions from open-end funds generate a transitory, and sometimes partly
persistent, price pressure on the securities these vehicles hold, buy or sell. The mechanism is
structural: a fund that receives net inflows must deploy the capital, and a fund that suffers
redemptions must sell. If the securities concerned cannot absorb these quantities at once without a
price concession, the flows themselves move prices. No new information about fundamental value is
needed; the effect rests on the inelasticity of the demand for securities, the inventory costs of
intermediaries, the segmentation of arbitrage capital and liquidity constraints.

A large part of the literature separates a transitory component from a more durable one. The
transitory part comes from the immediate need to absorb the orders. The more durable part arises
because flows can be autocorrelated, because managers spread their executions over time, or because
counterparties demand a premium for bearing inventory risk. The mechanism is stronger when the
securities are less liquid, harder to short, or held by a homogeneous investor base.

The effect persists because arbitrage is costly. Neutralising uninformative selling pressure means
holding the corresponding inventory, financing it, bearing the risk of new waves of redemptions, and
accepting a convergence of prices whose timing is uncertain. Over a few days to a few weeks, these
costs are enough to let an effect persist. The theory is closely tied to downward-sloping demand
curves for stocks, flow-driven return predictability, ownership fragility and common fund flows.

## Prediction

Securities bought by funds receiving net inflows rise, and securities sold by funds facing
redemptions fall, beyond what news about their fundamentals explains. The pressure is transitory,
and sometimes partly persistent: it leaves an effect over a few days to a few weeks, and part of it
can last when flows are autocorrelated, when managers spread their executions over time, or when
counterparties demand a premium for bearing inventory risk. The effect is larger for less liquid,
harder-to-short and homogeneously held securities. The effect has been documented mainly on stocks;
by analogy, the logic extends to any spot market in which vehicles that pool capital must execute
orders in proportion to their assets.

## What would refute it

- No abnormal price change in the securities traded by funds with large net inflows or outflows.
- Flow-driven price changes that are entirely explained by news about fundamentals.
- Price moves that never revert, even partly, once the flows stop.
- An effect no larger for illiquid, hard-to-short or homogeneously held securities than for liquid
  and widely held ones.
