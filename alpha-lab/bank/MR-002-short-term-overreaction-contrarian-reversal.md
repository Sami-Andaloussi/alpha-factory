## Mechanism

Recent price moves can go further than fundamentals or genuinely new information imply, because some investors extrapolate a run of good or bad news too quickly, overreact to idiosyncratic surprises, or buy and sell under flow constraints. The result is not mere volatility: the price ends up temporarily above or below a more reasonable value, then partly comes back as the emotion, the flow or the expectation error fades. The contrarian return comes from buying recent losers, or assets that are temporarily depressed, and betting on the partial closing of this excess.

In the books, this mechanism appears as the overreaction side of mean reversion. The behavioural books also stress that it does not require every investor to be irrational: it is enough that a fraction of participants extrapolates too much, and that arbitrageurs are risk-averse, financed at short term, or reluctant to add to a losing position, for the overreaction to last long enough to be measurable. The mechanism is formalised in behavioural models of investor sentiment and of under- and overreaction in asset markets.

It coexists with phenomena of opposite sign at other horizons: reversal at short horizons, momentum at medium horizons, then a later reversal. The same universe of assets can therefore show continuation over one horizon and correction over another.

## Prediction

Recent losers outperform recent winners over short to intermediate horizons, as prices partly correct the excess left by extrapolation, overreaction to idiosyncratic surprises or constrained flows. The effect is observed mainly in stocks. Across horizons the sign of the relation between past and future returns changes: reversal at short horizons, continuation at medium horizons, reversal again at longer ones.

An "oversold" asset can stay oversold if the shock in fact reveals fundamental information worse than was believed.

## What would refute it

- Short-term reversals that vanish once returns are measured on midquotes, or once bid-ask effects are removed, showing them to be microstructure effects rather than valuation errors.
- "Oversold" assets that stay depressed or keep falling because the shock revealed fundamental information worse than first believed.
- Reversals fully accounted for by liquidity rebounds after flow-driven moves and by transaction-price noise, leaving no component tied to extrapolation or excessive reaction to news.
- No reversal after extreme recent moves at short to intermediate horizons, or continuation instead.
