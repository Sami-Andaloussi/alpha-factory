## Mechanism

Part of price moves comes not from new information but from an overreaction to information that is already known, recycled or made salient. When investors, often retail, react to redundant news or to heavy media concentration, the price can move temporarily away from fundamental value, then reverse as the attention effect fades.

The mechanism is behavioural: attention is scarce, novelty and salience matter, and small investors react more to what they see than to what they should estimate. If the "news" contains little truly incremental information, the price pressure it generates is transitory, and the later correction is a normalisation of attention rather than a change in expected cash flows. A price can therefore move away from equilibrium not only because of a constrained flow but also because of a flow of attention.

The effect persists because retail attention, media circulation and narrative coordination create waves of demand that are hard to arbitrage in real time, especially when short-selling or career constraints apply. By analogy, the logic applies to crypto-assets, a market where public attention, social networks and informational redundancy have historically played a very strong role.

## Prediction

Reactions to stale or redundant news, and to heavy media concentration that brings little new information, can move the price temporarily away from fundamental value, and the move then reverses as the attention effect fades. The theory has been documented in stocks through reactions to redundant press stories and through the literature on salience and reversals. Pham et al. (2024) study the reversal in the cryptocurrency market before and during COVID-19.

## What would refute it

- Reactions to stale news that do not reverse, or that persist as much as reactions to fresh news.
- No link between retail attention or media intensity and the size of later reversals.
- Reversals of equal size after news that carries substantial new information.
