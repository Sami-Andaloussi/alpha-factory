## Mechanism

The accounting practice of expensing research and development understates both the assets and,
sometimes, the durable profitability of innovative companies. If the market takes the published
accounts too literally, without rebuilding the stock of capitalised research and development,
companies rich in knowledge capital can look more expensive, less profitable or less sound than they
are economically. The price can then reflect imperfectly their future capacity to create cash
flows, because the relevant capital does not appear properly on the balance sheet.

The phenomenon concerns in particular sectors where economic value comes through the accumulation of
intellectual property, product pipelines or technical capabilities. Conceptually it is a targeted
variant of intangible value, anchored in the accounting for research and development. The literature
is not unanimous on the respective shares of mispricing and of growth or innovation risk: high
research and development spending can also proxy for riskier growth options.

## Prediction

Companies rich in knowledge capital whose research and development is expensed can look more
expensive, less profitable or less sound than they are economically, and so be undervalued by a
market that takes the published accounts too literally, without rebuilding the capitalised stock of
research and development. The effect varies strongly across sectors and with the ex post success of
the innovation.

## What would refute it

- No relation between the capitalised stock of research and development and subsequent returns.
- Higher returns of research-intensive firms that are fully accounted for by the higher risk of their
  growth options.
- Prices that already reflect the capitalised stock of research and development, with the reported
  accounts exerting no misleading effect on valuation.
