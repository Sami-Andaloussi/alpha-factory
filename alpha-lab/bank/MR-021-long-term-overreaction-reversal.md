## Mechanism

Investors extrapolate good or bad news excessively, which pushes prices too far from fundamental value. Extreme losers become too cheap relative to their normal medium-term prospects, and extreme winners too expensive. As expectations return to more plausible levels, future returns reverse. In the form of the overreaction hypothesis, agents overweight striking news and extrapolate too much, pushing prices beyond the justified level, and reversals follow as beliefs normalise and evidence accumulates.

The central behavioural mechanism is non-Bayesian extrapolation: agents give too much weight to recent and salient information, especially when it confirms a narrative. Waves of optimism or pessimism move prices beyond what expected cash flows justify, with investors overreacting over long periods both to bad news, by selling too much, and to good news, by buying too much. The correction can take years because narratives, reputation, constraints on short selling and career concerns delay arbitrage. Persistence also comes from the costs and risks of arbitrage, which prevent an instantaneous correction, and from the heterogeneity of investors' horizons. Value investors buy the losers; momentum traders sell losers in the short run, before the reversal.

Several formal behavioural models, reviewed by Ilmanen (2011), generate momentum from short-term underreaction followed by reversal from medium-term overreaction. In Barberis, Shleifer and Vishny (1998), investors alternate between a regime of underreaction, anchored to their past forecasts, and a regime of overreaction, in which they extrapolate too much. In Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam (1998), investors' overconfidence amplifies initial moves, creating short-term momentum, and generates a later correction, a medium-term reversal. In Hong and Stein (1999), information spreads gradually among news watchers while price traders extrapolate, which creates first momentum and then reversal. In De Long, Shleifer, Summers and Waldmann (1990), noise traders create persistent deviations that fundamental arbitrageurs eventually correct. Crowding in momentum and value strategies amplifies the reversals.

The phenomenon is broader, more macro, than microstructure reversals. It often blends with the value premium, the correction of narratives, style rotation and the reversal of growth expectations. In futures, which concentrate macro views and leverage, overshoots of sentiment and positioning can be amplified; the correction can then be fast (days to weeks) or slower, depending on the asset and the regime.

## Prediction

Portfolios of stocks with the worst performance over three to five years tend to outperform portfolios of the best performers over the following three to five years (De Bondt and Thaler 1985, 1987, on NYSE stocks); the phenomenon is documented on the extremes of past performance. Variance-ratio tests over long periods of US data show a significant transitory component of price variance at horizons of three to five years (Poterba and Summers 1988). Broad indices show a statistical reversal as well; in futures, the reversal applies to sector or geographic indices. The behavioural models above all predict the same sequence in time: momentum at horizons of three to twelve months, then reversal at horizons of twelve to sixty months. On index futures, the medium-term behavioural reversal runs over twelve to thirty-six months: assets that have strongly outperformed over the past twelve to thirty-six months tend statistically to underperform over the following twelve to thirty-six months. Assets that have gone through a narrative capitulation, or extreme falls not fully justified by fundamentals, are expected to recover. The horizon is long, beyond a year; over shorter horizons momentum dominates.

## What would refute it

- No reversal of three- to five-year extremes: past long-term losers that do not outperform past long-term winners.
- Long-term reversal fully explained by tax effects or other structural factors rather than by overreaction; part of the literature has debated whether the reversal reflects genuine overreaction or tax and structural factors.
- Long-term reversal fully absorbed by a value risk premium.
- No transitory component of variance in long samples of index returns.
- No momentum-then-reversal sequence: past winners over three to twelve months that do not reverse over twelve to sixty months.
- Reversals over twelve to thirty-six months explained entirely by genuine changes in fundamentals rather than by the correction of an overreaction; reversals no stronger when momentum and value strategies are crowded.
