## Mechanism

A firm that generates a lot of internal cash but has few disciplining investment opportunities becomes
vulnerable to overinvestment, to value-destroying acquisitions and to the private consumption of
managerial rents. Dividends, debt service or repurchases can then play a disciplinary role by taking
discretionary cash out of management's hands.

The mechanism is specific to each firm, because it depends on the firm's maturity, its capital
intensity, its growth opportunities and its internal governance. It persists as long as agency
frictions remain large and investors cannot instantly force an efficient use of the cash. The typical
setting is mature, cash-rich firms with low organic growth. Inefficient cash hoarding is distinct
from the legitimate accumulation of cash for future investments.

## Prediction

Mature, cash-rich firms with low organic growth and few disciplining investment opportunities are
vulnerable to overinvestment, to value-destroying acquisitions and to the private consumption of
managerial rents; dividends, debt service or repurchases can limit this by taking discretionary cash
out of management's hands.

## What would refute it

- Cash-rich firms with few investment opportunities do not overinvest, and do not make more
  value-destroying acquisitions, than comparable firms with little free cash.
- In such firms, higher dividends, debt service or repurchases are not followed by a better use of the
  remaining capital.
- The cash accumulated by mature, low-growth firms is, on average, later spent on profitable
  investments rather than on value-destroying ones.
