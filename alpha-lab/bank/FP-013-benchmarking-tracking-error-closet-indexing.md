## Mechanism

A benchmark is not only a yardstick: it generates flows. When managers are judged against an index
or a peer group, they have an interest in avoiding deviations that are too visible, even when they
believe a fundamental bet could pay. The result is benchmark hugging, closet indexing, and a
systematic demand for the large names of the benchmark.

This is not a mere psychological bias. It is written into management contracts, investment
committees, pay structures and career risk. It persists because "pure" alpha is hard to prove,
whereas tracking error and comparison with peers are immediately observable. Managers who are
active in principle therefore behave in practice like a conditional passive flow. This raises the
density of capital in some names and reduces the willingness to take the other side in securities
outside the benchmark.

The strength of the effect varies with the latitude of the mandate, the sophistication of the client
and the tolerance for active risk. Benchmarking feeds index reconstitution effects, crowding among
peers, and the link between performance and flows.

## Prediction

Portfolios of nominally active managers stay close to their benchmark weights, and the large
constituents of benchmarks receive a steady demand that comparable securities outside the benchmark
do not. In equities, benchmarking is a powerful driver of flows around large capitalisations,
benchmark reviews, and episodes of strong divergence between the benchmark and managers'
convictions. Securities outside the benchmark find fewer buyers willing to take the other side of
their flows.

## What would refute it

- Active managers' holdings showing no tilt towards benchmark weights beyond what their stated
  convictions explain.
- No additional demand for benchmark constituents relative to comparable securities outside the
  benchmark.
- Benchmark hugging no stronger under tight mandates and low tolerance for active risk than under
  wide mandates.
