## Mechanism

The value of a crypto network rises with the size and density of its user base. If a token serves as
a medium of exchange, as a reserve asset within a network or as a key to access an infrastructure,
the utility of the network grows more than proportionally as the number of users and connections
rises. The best-known version of the idea is a Metcalfe-type valuation.

The mechanism combines network effects, liquidity of use and coordination: the more the network is
adopted, the more useful and the more liquid it becomes, and the higher the value its native asset
can justify. The books converge on the idea that many cryptoassets are to be analysed not as simple
scarce commodities but as networks whose marginal value rises with adoption: the more users,
validators, developers, complementors or merchants a network has, the more useful and defensible its
asset becomes. The price is then not only speculative: it can be anchored to an observable level of
economic adoption.

The mechanism produces path dependence, winner-take-most dynamics, and regime breaks when a network
crosses or loses a critical mass. It persists because infrastructure, liquidity, standardisation and
complementors build up around the dominant network. Network effects do not protect against a
governance shock, a security flaw or a superior competing standard.

The same network externality can be read in observable measures of the protocol. Some cryptoassets
draw part of their value from the intensity and the quality of the activity observed on their
network: active addresses, transactions, hashrate, validation activity, user participation, and so
on. The mechanism is not simply "more activity means better momentum". It rests on the idea that a
network that is used more, or that is harder to attack, has a greater economic usefulness or
credibility, which can be capitalised into the token. The relevant actors are end users,
arbitrageurs, validators and miners, the applications built on top of the protocol, and the investors
who read these metrics as signals of real adoption. The phenomenon can persist because on-chain
measures are noisy, manipulable at the margin and hard to interpret out of context, and because the
market aggregates imperfectly the difference between speculative activity and utility activity. This
imperfect interpretation leaves room for a structural valuation specific to each asset, according to
its technical architecture and its use.

## Prediction

The value of a crypto network rises with its adoption, the utility of the network growing more than
proportionally with its number of users and connections. The winner-take-most dynamics concentrate
value in the dominant networks, around which infrastructure, liquidity and complementors build up,
and regime breaks occur when a network crosses or loses a critical mass of participants; a governance
shock, a security flaw or a superior competing standard can end the dominance of a network despite
its network effects.

The literature shows that certain blockchain characteristics have explanatory power in the
cross-section of cryptoasset returns. The horizon depends on the variable: some on-chain metrics
capture slow transitions in adoption, others react immediately to speculative or congestion
episodes. The setting is essentially crypto.

Network effects are not uniform from one protocol to another, and some studies stress that these
relations are not always stable. Usage can grow without a strong monetisation of the token.

## What would refute it

- Network values that do not grow more than proportionally with the number of users and connections.
- A relation between value and adoption that comes from bots and non-economic activity rather than
  from use, and holds in no protocol once they are removed.
- Adoption and value that move independently of each other over long periods; cryptoasset values
  that do not rise with the number of users, validators, developers, complementors or merchants of
  their network.
- Leading networks that lose their position as easily as smaller ones in the absence of any
  governance shock, security flaw or superior competing standard, with no concentration of
  infrastructure, liquidity or complementors around them.
- Networks that cross or lose a critical mass of participants with no change of regime in their
  prices.
- Blockchain characteristics and network-activity metrics that have no explanatory power in the
  cross-section of cryptoasset returns.
- Networks that are harder to attack valued no differently from comparable networks that are easier
  to attack.
