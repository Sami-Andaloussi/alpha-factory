## Mechanism

Here the driver of momentum is not first individual psychology but the institutional organisation of
capital. End investors allocate money to managers, or withdraw it, according to past performance;
managers are judged against benchmarks and peers; mandates, investment committees, governance
constraints and funding frictions prevent capital from being reallocated instantly towards arbitrage
positions. In the model of Vayanos and Woolley, flows towards winning managers push prices further in
the direction already taken, before a reversal later. Benchmark hugging, career risk and the effects
of consultants and peer comparison add to this. Even investors who know that an asset is expensive or
has already risen too much may be unable to oppose the move, or may even be pushed to go along with
it.

Slow-moving capital is the structural slowness with which large institutional investors (pension
funds, insurers, sovereign funds, mutual funds) reposition their portfolios in response to new
information or new asset prices; this slowness creates an inertia of flows that prolongs trends
beyond what an immediate adjustment would allow. Capital moves slowly for several structural
reasons. Mandates impose strategic allocations that can be changed only after an investment
committee approves, which takes weeks or months. Benchmark and tracking-error management keeps
managers from moving away from benchmark weights too quickly. Liquidity constraints force large
investors to split their orders over several days or weeks to minimise market impact. Quarterly
reporting cycles lead many managers to adjust their portfolios at quarter-end. Duffie (2010)
describes the same slowness in how investment capital moves towards opportunities or comes to absorb
supply and demand shocks: prices can react strongly to an initial shock and then keep evolving over
a longer period as intermediaries raise capital, find counterparties and overcome search and
financing frictions. Mitchell, Pedersen and Pulvino (2007) formalised the concept for hedge funds and
arbitrage capital, showing that even professional arbitrageurs face capital constraints that prevent
them from acting at once, and Duffie (2010) extended it to the bond market.

The slowness is social and institutional as well as physical. Delegated investors tolerate prolonged
deviations from the benchmark badly, and managers adapt to that constraint. Gray and Vogel describe a
career-risk premium: a momentum strategy can fail spectacularly relative to the benchmark for
non-trivial periods, which few managers can bear, so even when the economic logic of the signal is
understood, the professional cost of sticking to it prevents it from being fully arbitraged. The
persistence is not irrational: it comes from institutional impediments (the search for
counterparties, capital, the constraints of intermediaries). Reallocations into or out of a style are
neither instantaneous nor symmetric, so flows can themselves reinforce trends. Slow-moving capital is
one of the institutional mechanisms that explain why momentum exists and persists beyond what
standard models of efficiency would predict; it is structural rather than behavioural, and it
coexists with and amplifies the behavioural mechanisms of underreaction. It concerns in particular
indices, styles and liquid assets, and in futures above all macro and cross-asset exposures. It
explains why momentum appears not only in equities but "in every major liquid asset class", in Ang's
phrase: institutions cannot move at the speed of information.

## Prediction

After an initial shock, the portfolio adjustments of institutions create persistent directional
flows for weeks or months that support the trend, with predictable entries in the direction of the
initial move, typically over 1 to 6 months. The slow adjustment of the capacity to absorb shocks can
generate spread-out directional price paths and a conditional predictability, especially when supply
or demand pressures persist or spread between markets. In futures markets the slowness is visible in
the Commitments of Traders data: the positions of large non-commercial traders, the institutional
managers, change slowly and persistently, reflecting the inertia of their mandates. The phenomenon is
documented in equity, bond and futures markets, and momentum is observed in every major liquid asset
class.

## What would refute it

Institutional allocations that adjust to new prices or information within days rather than weeks or
months, without committee delays or split orders. Large-trader positions in futures that change
abruptly rather than persistently after shocks. No relation between institutional flows and the
continuation of trends. Momentum absent from the major liquid asset classes, indices and styles.
Delegated investors who tolerate prolonged deviations from the benchmark, and managers who do not
limit such deviations.
