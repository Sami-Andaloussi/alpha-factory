## Mechanism

The library treats capital allocation discipline as an autonomous fundamental driver of value
creation or destruction. Management receives capital through retained profits, debt, share issuance
or asset sales, then chooses how to redeploy it: organic reinvestment, acquisitions, share buybacks,
dividends or debt reduction. Two companies with the same starting business can therefore produce
very different stock market trajectories depending on the quality of that allocation.

The theme is closely tied to value traps: a cheap asset with mediocre management can keep destroying
value by reinvesting below the cost of capital, while a quality business can become a compounding
machine if its excess cash flows are reallocated with discipline. The phenomenon persists because the
quality of allocation is hard to measure with simple ratios and often reveals itself with a delay,
notably after acquisitions or after a long period of buybacks or share issuance.

## Prediction

Companies with the same starting business can produce very different stock market trajectories
depending on the quality of their capital allocation: a cheap company whose management reinvests
below the cost of capital can keep destroying value, while a quality business can become a
compounding machine if its excess cash flows are reallocated with discipline. The quality of
allocation often reveals itself with a delay, notably after acquisitions or after a long period of
buybacks or issuance, which is why the phenomenon persists.

## What would refute it

- Companies with similar businesses whose stock trajectories do not differ with the quality of their
  capital allocation.
- Cheap stocks whose managements reinvest below the cost of capital that perform as well as cheap
  stocks with disciplined managements.
- Allocation decisions priced in full when they are announced, with no delayed revelation after
  acquisitions or after long periods of buybacks or issuance.
