## Mechanism

The Santa Claus rally is the tendency of stocks to rise over a short window made of the last five trading days of December and the first two trading days of January. It is a specific component of end-of-year seasonality, distinct from the full January effect and from the simple pre-holiday effect. Studies have mostly treated it as a phenomenon in its own right, sometimes with a strong small-cap component.

The proposed mechanisms lie where several theories meet: end-of-year and holiday optimism, and a temporary fall in risk aversion; reduced activity by institutional investors, which leaves the field to individual investors; end-of-year flows, together with Christmas and end-of-year shopping (higher retail spending), and the late booking of premiums and bonuses ("decking"); anticipation of the January effect; and cosmetic portfolio adjustments. The window often coincides with few major announcements and with bullish sentiment. The rally is therefore often read as an overlap between sentiment and end-of-year market microstructure rather than as a separate mechanism, and its origin remains composite.

The window is very narrow. An outperformance over this sub-period that does not extend to the whole of December or to the whole of January points to finer explanations than tax-loss selling alone. Some studies also find an interaction with firm size, which brings the rally closer to limits to arbitrage and to the small-cap effect.

## Prediction

Stock prices tend to rise over the last five trading days of December and the first two of January; the rise can be larger in small capitalisations. Since 1950, this window has produced an average gain of about 1.3% for the S&P 500, with a rise in about 80% of years.

Where and when it has been observed: the effect has persisted. It has been studied in markets around the world, including India, and in relation to firm size.

## What would refute it

- No excess return over the last five trading days of December and the first two of January compared with other windows of seven trading days.
- A frequency of rises over this window close to that of any other seven-day window, rather than about 80% of years.
- An outperformance spread evenly over the whole of December or the whole of January, which would point to the broader end-of-year effects rather than to this window.
- A rally no stronger in years of low institutional activity, few announcements and bullish sentiment than in other years.
