## Mechanism

Switching costs and customer captivity describe a situation in which customers no longer choose only
on the marginal price of the day: if they change supplier, they must pay in time, money, learning,
compatibility or migration risk. This gives the incumbent supplier an economic power specific to it.

The mechanism persists because exit costs accumulate as the customer uses the system, invests in
complements or trains its teams. The books show that these effects are naturally linked to network
effects and to lock-in. Open standards or strong interoperability can reduce captivity abruptly.

## Prediction

Firms whose customers face high switching costs keep an economic power over those customers that firms
whose customers can switch freely do not have. The mechanism is observed in software, brokers,
financial services, industrial inputs, some recurring consumer goods, and even networks built on
standards. Open standards or strong interoperability can reduce captivity abruptly.

## What would refute it

- Customers of firms with high switching costs leave as often, and accept price increases no more
  readily, than customers of firms with low switching costs.
- Firms with captive customers show no more durable margins or economic power than comparable firms
  without them.
- The arrival of open standards or of strong interoperability leaves the market shares and margins of
  incumbent suppliers unchanged.
