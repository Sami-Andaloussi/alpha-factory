## Mechanism

The January effect is the recurrent outperformance historically observed in January, often concentrated in the first trading days of the year and particularly strong in small capitalisations: the books describe a pattern in which small firms, and often the previous year's losers, outperform strongly around the turn of the year. It is one of the oldest of the modern calendar effects. It amounts to a concentration of the small-cap premium around the turn of the year, and the books insist that it is not a simple "January rally" of the whole market: it is more concentrated in small stocks and in certain profiles of stocks.

As a phenomenon it is not one mechanism but an aggregate of several possible forces: tax-loss selling of losers at the end of the year, then the return of buyers and the rebuilding of positions in January; institutional window dressing and other reporting and rebalancing practices; the reallocation of portfolios; liquidity constraints; inflows of liquidity at the start of the year, with bonuses and contributions invested in January; the resetting of risk budgets and drawdown limits; and sometimes measurement components tied to size and illiquidity. A central point of the literature is that the total effect is not uniform. One part is essentially a small-cap, illiquidity and microcap effect, one part comes from the mechanics of the change of tax year, and one part from the behaviour of delegated managers. The literature is divided on the relative share of these mechanisms.

Its persistence is imperfect. The better known it is, the more it can be arbitraged in large capitalisations, whereas the segments most affected are precisely those where arbitrage costs most: transaction costs are too high to arbitrage a basket of small caps cleanly in the cash market, and the books point out that much of the raw effect lies in the most illiquid and least investable stocks. Part of the effect can move earlier, into late December, if investors anticipate the January buying, and it sometimes starts earlier and earlier, from late December, when arbitrageurs and anomaly traders anticipate it; it can also shrink when tax rules, institutional ownership and market microstructure change. The authors explain that trading it through a spread of small-cap against large-cap index futures mainly reduced the frictions rather than changing the economic logic. The books show that the effect is not only a cash-equity pattern: once it is known and can be arbitraged through futures, part of the return is anticipated and moves into the second half of December, especially in the small-minus-large spread. The anomaly is therefore not static: it migrates through the calendar under the pressure of arbitrage, and futures do not necessarily make it disappear but change its place in the calendar.

## Prediction

Stocks, above all small capitalisations and often the previous year's losers, earn abnormally high returns in January, often concentrated in its first trading days, relative to large capitalisations and to other months. Once the effect is known and can be arbitraged through futures, part of it moves earlier, into the second half of December, especially in the small-minus-large futures spread. The effect is irregular from one year to the next and sensitive to tax regimes, the composition of the small-cap segment and anticipation by the market.

Where and when it has been observed: mainly in US equities, then in many international markets, including Japan, with considerable heterogeneity according to the tax calendar, the ownership structure and firm size; also in US index futures, where the anticipation of the small-firm January effect has been shown. The books show that its historical shape has shifted over time.

## What would refute it

- No excess return in January, or in the first trading days of the year, for small capitalisations relative to large ones.
- A January outperformance as large in large, liquid capitalisations as in small, illiquid ones, which would contradict the role of costly arbitrage.
- An effect whose timing and size show no link with the local tax calendar, or that does not change when tax rules change, which would contradict its tax component.
- No shift of the effect into late December once it is traded through small-minus-large futures spreads, which would contradict its migration under arbitrage.
- A broad January rally of the whole market with no concentration in small firms and past losers.
