## Mechanism

Earnings momentum is the persistence of good and bad earnings surprises, and the continuation of the
revisions of the fundamentals behind them. A firm that reported a good surprise in quarter T has a
probability significantly above 50% of reporting another in quarter T+1. Prices do not only follow
an abstract trajectory: they follow the trajectory of economic information that itself turns out to
be persistent, and the theory holds that price momentum is often the visible expression of a deeper
informational momentum tied to earnings. The theory ties momentum to an economic source more
"anchored" than price alone.

The mechanism is twofold. First, fundamental quality is persistent: competitive advantages, the
quality of management and market positions do not vanish from one quarter to the next, and a firm's
economic quality, or its deterioration, does not appear at a single point in time: results, margins,
orders, guidance revisions and analysts' expectations come out in waves. Second, investors, analysts
and managers underreact to this material public information. They adjust their models gradually,
especially when the signal is ambiguous or contradicts established beliefs, when attention is scarce,
when the announcement comes amid a dense flow of news or when the firm is little followed; analysts
make systematic forecast errors by not fully capturing these fundamental persistences, notably
because of an anchoring bias on their previous estimates. When a firm publishes a positive surprise,
the market does not reprice it fully and at once, and the adjustment continues for weeks or months.
The continuation is tied to cognitive limits rather than to a risk premium of its own, and its
frictions are the same as those of the post-earnings-announcement drift.

The idea attributed to Novy-Marx is that a large part of price momentum would in fact be a
manifestation of earnings momentum ("fundamentally, momentum is fundamental momentum"); Gray and
Vogel discuss the thesis. Several layers can be distinguished: price momentum, momentum in earnings
surprises (standardised unexpected earnings, SUE), momentum in analyst revisions, and a broader
fundamental momentum. The books show that these signals behave alike but not identically, and that
their combinations can be more informative than each taken alone. Latané and Jones (1979) formalised
SUE as actual earnings minus expected earnings, divided by the standard deviation of past forecast
errors, and showed that stocks sorted on SUE earn persistent abnormal returns.

By analogy, in futures the theory applies through equity indices, sectors, or aggregated signals of
revisions and surprises.

## Prediction

Stocks sorted on earnings surprises (SUE), on earnings revisions or on cumulative abnormal returns
around announcements earn persistent abnormal returns in the direction of the surprise over horizons
of weeks to months, typically 6 to 12 months, about four quarters of effect. The phenomenon is
documented mostly in equities, over short to intermediate horizons. Earnings momentum is distinct
from and complementary to price momentum: the combination of the two, used by Chan, Jegadeesh and
Lakonishok (1996), earns more than either signal alone, with a moderate correlation between them
(0.29). O'Shaughnessy (2011) shows the impact of filters on earnings on factor returns. The effect
depends on the announcement calendar and can erode as competition to trade on standardised
announcements increases.

## What would refute it

Earnings surprises that do not persist from one quarter to the next, a good surprise making another
no more likely. Stocks sorted on SUE or on revisions earning no abnormal returns in the following
months. Price momentum that is unchanged once earnings momentum is controlled for, and earnings
momentum that adds nothing to price momentum. Analysts' forecast errors that show no persistence and
no anchoring on previous estimates.
