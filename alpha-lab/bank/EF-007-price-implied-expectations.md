## Mechanism

Excess returns do not come from a company being "good", but from the revision of the expectations
built into its price. The market already values a large part of public information, so the alpha
comes from the gap between the expectations implied by the price and the reality that follows. The
approach reads the price backwards: from the share price, the cost of capital and assumptions about
the value drivers, it infers the length of the period of excess profits, the implied growth and the
expected margins. Valuation is thereby tied directly to revisions of expectations, not to absolute
levels.

This explains why an objectively cheap stock can be a trap if its price already embeds a strong
recovery, and why an expensive stock can keep rising if the duration of its competitive advantage is
underestimated. The central variables are the market-implied forecast period, the value growth
duration and the fade rate.

## Prediction

Returns follow the revision of the expectations implied by prices, not the absolute level of
valuation multiples. Stocks whose price implies a shorter period of excess profits, lower growth or
lower margins than what later materialises earn higher subsequent returns; stocks whose price implies
more than what materialises earn lower returns. A cheap stock can be a trap if its price already
embeds a strong recovery, and an expensive stock can keep rising if the duration of its competitive
advantage is underestimated.

## What would refute it

- Subsequent returns explained by the absolute cheapness of stocks, with no additional information in
  the gap between implied and realised expectations.
- Market-implied forecast periods, growth rates and fade rates that match the realised ones, so that
  no revision of expectations follows.
- Cheap stocks whose price embeds a strong recovery that outperform as much as other cheap stocks, or
  expensive stocks with an underestimated competitive advantage that do not keep rising.
