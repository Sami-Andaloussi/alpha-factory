## Mechanism

The illiquidity premium rests on the idea that an asset that is hard to trade must offer a higher
expected return to compensate for the cost of its price impact, for the risk of not being able to
exit quickly, and for its unfavourable covariance with aggregate market liquidity. The relation is
one of relative value because it compares similar assets that are exposed differently to expected
illiquidity and to liquidity beta, and separates the assets that are better rewarded for their
liquidity risk from those that are less well rewarded.

The premium persists because patient capital is scarce, many mandates prohibit less liquid holdings,
investors fear redemptions, and liquidity has a common component that deteriorates precisely when it
is most costly.

## Prediction

Less liquid assets, and assets whose returns covary more unfavourably with market liquidity, earn
higher average returns than comparable liquid assets. The effect has been observed both in the
cross-section and in the time series, particularly among small-capitalisation stocks. Illiquid assets
come under extreme stress in crises.

## What would refute it

- Illiquid assets earn no higher returns than comparable liquid assets, in the cross-section or over
  time, including among small-capitalisation stocks.
- Assets whose returns covary more with aggregate liquidity earn no premium over those whose returns
  covary less.
