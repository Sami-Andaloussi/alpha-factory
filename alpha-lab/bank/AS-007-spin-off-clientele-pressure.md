## Mechanism

Spin-offs often create an asset whose natural base of holders differs sharply from that of the parent
company. When a firm distributes the shares of a subsidiary to its shareholders, many institutions
find themselves holding a stock that falls outside their mandate in terms of size, style, sector,
liquidity or governance. They then sell it, not because they have specific negative information, but
because the new asset does not fit their "holding box". The price of the spun-off stock can therefore
be depressed by structural selling pressure from its clientele.

The mechanism persists because this pressure is not arbitraged immediately: specialised buyers are
not necessarily present at the exact moment of the distribution, the subsidiary is sometimes small,
little followed and poorly understood, and its initial analyst coverage is limited. Information costs
are also high just after the separation. The phenomenon thus has an institutional basis: the
mandate constraints and the preferences of the inherited holders create a disorderly transition from
the old shareholder base to a new, better-suited one.

The structural logic goes beyond spin-offs in the strict sense: whenever an asset changes its
"natural holding category", it can undergo forced or semi-forced flows.

## Prediction

After the distribution, the spun-off stock is sold by the institutions whose size, style, sector,
liquidity or governance mandate excludes it, not because they hold specific negative information,
and its price can be depressed in the short to medium term, for as long as it takes a new clientele
of investors to form. The setting is equities.

## What would refute it

- Newly spun-off stocks show no abnormal institutional selling and no price depression after the
  distribution.
- The institutions that sell the spun-off stock are not those whose mandate it falls outside.
- The price declines of spun-off stocks are matched by a deterioration of their fundamentals and do
  not reverse as the new shareholder base forms.
