## Mechanism

The 52-week high is a neighbour of price momentum. A recent annual high serves investors and
analysts as a psychological anchor and a public reference point. Investors use salient reference
points, notably former price peaks, to judge whether an asset "has already risen too much" or "has
fallen too much". When a stock approaches or exceeds its 52-week high, many investors keep reasoning
relative to this salient level instead of immediately updating their estimate of value. Close to the
high, they hesitate to buy, judging the stock "expensive" relative to the prices they have seen, or,
in Gray and Vogel's account, some of them wrongly read a price near a peak as a reason to sell, when
it may simply signal that a fundamental revaluation is still incomplete. This psychological
resistance, a mix of anchoring and framing, slows the upward adjustment of the price towards the
value implied by the information, so the market is still reluctant to pay what the information
already justifies; the delayed adjustment then produces a positive drift once the level is crossed.

The mechanism works symmetrically on the downside. When a stock is far below its high after a strong
fall, investors hesitate to sell, the anchor feeding an overconfidence that "it will come back",
which delays the downward adjustment. This psychological support eventually gives way and a
downtrend follows.

Salient levels organise attention, conditional orders, "breakout" narratives and the reactions of
less sophisticated investors, and the reaction is gradual: investors do not all update their beliefs
or their decision constraints at the same time. It is therefore not merely a charting device but a
specific way of capturing underreaction around a collective anchor. Its persistence is
compatible with the limits of arbitrage: when the anchoring is widespread, betting against the
collective belief is risky and costly, and the phenomenon can survive. By analogy, the same
reference-point mechanism can delay the incorporation of information in futures markets where
previous highs and lows are salient for managers and traders, although the recent high of a futures
contract does not necessarily carry the same collective psychological meaning as that of a stock.

## Prediction

The nearness of the current price to its 52-week high predicts future returns: stocks close to their
annual high keep outperforming, and stocks far below it keep underperforming, over horizons of 6 to
12 months. George and Hwang (2004) found, on US stocks, that this nearness predicts future returns
better than standard momentum measured on 6- to 12-month past returns, in some specifications; the
continuation after the high is crossed can sometimes dominate the simple sort on past returns. It
has been documented mostly on equities, and the mechanism is conceived mainly for individual stocks;
the regularity partly overlaps with traditional price momentum, and Bali, Engle and Murray count it
among the momentum regularities linked to investor inattention.

## What would refute it

Stocks near their 52-week high earning no more over the following 6 to 12 months than stocks far
from it, once past returns are controlled for. Nearness to the high adding no predictive power
beyond standard 6- to 12-month momentum. No acceleration of returns after the high is crossed, or
prices near their high adjusting to good news as fast as prices elsewhere. Stocks far below their
high adjusting downwards without delay.
