## Mechanism

This theory concerns the relative ranking of stocks rather than the average level of the market on a given date. Heston and Sadka show that stocks that performed well in a given calendar month in past years tend to outperform again in that same month, year after year; a stock also tends to underperform repeatedly in the same calendar month: some stocks have a recurring "season" of their own. Seasonality thus moves from the aggregate time series to the cross-section of returns.

The effect cannot be reduced to standard momentum or to long-term reversal. It differs from twelve-month momentum because what matters is the recurrence of the calendar month, not only recent continuation, and it is superimposed on those effects with a calendar signature of its own. Several mechanisms are possible: recurring cycles of demand; a stable investor base with calendar routines, or recurring investor clienteles; a seasonal exposure to risk factors; and recurring calendars of corporate information and publication, of commercial activity, of taxation or of institutional constraints that come back at the same time every year. That the effect survives across many annual lags suggests repeated economic clocks rather than a statistical accident.

## Prediction

Stocks that performed well in a given calendar month in past years tend to outperform other stocks again in that same month, year after year, and the relation survives over many annual lags; it is not reduced to standard momentum or to long-term reversal. Stocks that have repeatedly underperformed in a given month tend to underperform again in that month.

Where it has been observed: in US stocks, then in international markets.

## What would refute it

- No relation between a stock's past returns in a calendar month and its relative return in the same month of later years.
- A same-month relation fully accounted for by standard momentum and long-term reversal, which would leave no calendar signature of its own.
- A relation that holds at the one-year lag but vanishes at longer annual lags, which would point to a statistical accident rather than to repeated economic clocks.
- A same-month effect that disappears once industry membership is controlled for, which would make it an industry pattern rather than a property of individual stocks.
