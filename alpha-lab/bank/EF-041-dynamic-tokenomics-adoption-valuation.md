## Mechanism

Beyond its immediate utility, a token can capitalise today the expected growth of a future network
or protocol. If the token is needed within the ecosystem, the expected rise in future adoption can
show up in its present price, and the token becomes a way of internalising the growth of the
platform. The economic logic is not foreign to ordinary shares: the market capitalises future cash
flows or rents; here it capitalises a future demand for use and the value created by decentralised
coordination.

The token coordinates, at the same time, the use of the network, the financing of the protocol and
the expectations of future value. Its price is not only the reflection of a stream of cash flows, as
for a share, nor only that of an immediate transactional use: it depends on the dynamics of the
platform's adoption, on the rights or utilities embedded in the token, on the issuance policy, and
on the way the protocol internalises network externalities, so the protocol's own structure creates
a price mechanism specific to the asset. The actors include users, validators and miners,
developers, investors, founders and the treasury or foundation of the protocol. The phenomenon
persists because the token can solve coordination problems ex ante: it finances the launch of the
network, gives incentives to the first participants, and makes future value very sensitive to the
widening of the ecosystem.

The mechanism the literature documents turns on a loop between price and adoption: a high price
sometimes makes financing, attention and adoption easier, while stronger adoption supports the value
of the token. It is fundamental but reflexive. A token can thus be "expensive today" if it
represents a credible option on a widely adopted future protocol. Arbitrage is limited by the
extreme uncertainty about adoption, by changes in governance, by the non-linearity of network
effects and by the difficulty of valuing future utilities, which is why differences in design or
issuance can produce durably distinct market behaviour between tokens that look comparable. The
setting is platform-native cryptoassets, but any structure in which an asset serves at once as an
internal medium of exchange, an incentive mechanism and a financing tool can fall under this
mechanism.

## Prediction

Token prices can incorporate expected future adoption: a high price sometimes makes financing,
attention and adoption easier, while stronger adoption supports the value of the token. Over the
medium to long term, token prices depend on the adoption path of their platform, with rapid repricing
episodes when the market revises the adoption path or the incentive architecture of the protocol, and
tokens that look comparable but differ in design or issuance can show durably distinct market
behaviour.

The economic rights attached to a token are often less clear than those of a share, and many designs
fail to turn adoption into value for the token holder.

## What would refute it

- Token prices that do not respond to changes in expected adoption, or to changes in the protocol's
  incentive architecture, and that bear no relation to the adoption of their platform over the medium
  and long term.
- Tokens of comparable platforms that differ in design or issuance behaving the same in the market.
- High token prices that never ease financing, attention or adoption.
- Realised adoption that does not translate into value for token holders, whatever the design.
