## Mechanism

The semi-month effect, sometimes called the half-month or intramonth effect, is the tendency of returns to be higher in the first half of the month than in the second. In Ariel's original version, most of the equity return is concentrated before and during the first half of the month, while the second half shows average returns close to zero. It extends the turn-of-the-month effect: not only are the end and the start of the month strong, but a large part, or even nearly all, of the monthly return is concentrated in the first half, while the second half is often noise or slightly negative.

It is close to the turn-of-the-month effect but not identical to it. The semi-month effect splits the month into two segments, whereas the turn-of-the-month effect looks at a short window around the change of month. The semi-month effect can therefore capture more diffuse effects of the payroll calendar, of macroeconomic releases or of the gradual adjustment of allocations. Intramonth seasonality is not only a spike of two or three days: it can extend over a wider segment of the calendar, and calendar regularities can reflect flows being digested over several days rather than a single precise session.

Several explanations coexist, and, as for the turn-of-the-month effect, the phenomenon is probably composite: inflows of liquidity at the start of the month; the concentration of important macroeconomic announcements; the behaviour of individual investors after they receive their income; and possible routines of institutional rebalancing. Another proposed mechanism is the staggered deployment of flows received between the 20th and the 25th of the month, together with sales pushes by brokers, the placement of collective savings, and careful management of market impact in less liquid stocks. The persistence of the effect depends on relatively stable institutional clocks. Its arbitrage may be limited because it is low-frequency, sensitive to the definition of the windows and partly contaminated by other monthly effects.

## Prediction

Average returns are higher in the first half of the calendar month, together with the last days of the previous month, than in the second half, where they are close to zero, often noisy or slightly negative. The books on Japan document it with windows of the -5 to +7 type around the turn of the month, longer than those observed in the United States because of local features of the calendar and of the market.

Where it has been observed: in US equities and in several international markets, including Japan, with an intensity that varies with the period. It has also been studied on the FTSE indices of the London Stock Exchange.

## What would refute it

- Average returns in the first half of the month no higher than in the second half.
- A difference that disappears once the few days of the turn-of-the-month window are removed, which would reduce it to the turn-of-the-month effect.
- No link between the timing and length of the strong window and local payment dates and market features, for instance a window in Japan no longer than in the United States.
- A first-half excess return unrelated to the timing of macroeconomic announcements, income receipts and rebalancing flows.
