## Mechanism

A more fundamental form of mean reversion concerns not the price directly but the economics of the firm itself. Returns on operating assets, residual operating income and, more generally, abnormally high profits tend to revert towards more ordinary levels as competitors enter, imitation spreads, margins normalise and competitive advantage erodes. Symmetrically, very poor profitability can recover when excess capacity leaves the industry or when the firm reorganises. This fade gives a structural basis to the return towards fundamental value; Penman (2011) discusses it through fade diagrams and the competitive advantage period.

If economic profitability fades towards a mean, part of pricing errors comes from errors about the speed, the size or the asymmetry of this fade. The value investor then gains not only because the multiple normalises, but because the market overestimates the permanence of a poor current state or, conversely, overpays for the permanence of exceptional excellence.

## Prediction

Extreme profitability, high or low, tends to revert towards more ordinary levels, and prices follow: firms whose poor current profitability the market treats as permanent earn higher returns as their profitability recovers, and firms priced for lasting excellence earn lower returns as their profitability fades. Firms with a genuine moat can sustain above-average profitability for a long time, and firms in structural decline may never recover.

## What would refute it

- No reversion of extreme returns on operating assets, or of residual operating income, towards the mean.
- Firms with a genuine competitive moat that sustain above-average profitability for long periods, and firms in structural decline whose profitability never recovers, on a scale that outweighs the average fade.
- Prices that anticipate the fade correctly, leaving no difference in later returns between firms with extreme current profitability and others.
