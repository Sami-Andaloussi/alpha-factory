## Mechanism

This theory extends the customer-supplier effect to the aggregate level of vertical chains between
industries. Markets are not informationally integrated in a uniform way: in networks of production,
supply or final demand, a shock to an upstream or downstream industry carries fundamental information
for the other industries that are economically connected to it, but that information travels slowly
from one segment of the chain to the next.

The cause is the segmentation of attention and expertise, a form of market segmentation. Investors
often specialise in one industry or one segment of the value chain, acquire information
endogenously, trade first within their own segment and do not immediately carry all the implications
of a shock over to the connected sectors. An unexpected rise in demand in a downstream industry can
take time to be fully priced into its upstream suppliers; conversely, a shock to inputs can enter the
prices of the consuming industries only gradually. The predictability is not merely a descriptive
anomaly: it is the outcome of an equilibrium in which acquiring information is costly, so that market
participants mostly watch the assets they cover, and the valuations of an industry incorporate only
with a delay the information already reflected in the industries upstream or downstream of it.
Input-output networks thereby become a stable source of structural comovement and of temporary
deviations, and cross signals stay alive even when the initial information is public. The speed of
diffusion depends on how specialised investors are and on how transparent the economic chains are.

The information involved is diffuse and fundamental, not a mere statistical comovement. The effect is
close to that of customer-supplier links between firms, but its central friction is segmentation
itself. The effect concerns in particular settings where industry classifications hide economic
dependencies across sectors that are finer than the standard groupings.

## Prediction

The literature shows that returns cross-predict between vertically linked industries: the lagged returns
of the industries that supply an industry or buy from it, as mapped in input-output networks, predict
that industry's subsequent returns. The effects are often stronger for stocks with lower institutional
ownership, thinner analyst coverage and more complex businesses, and for stocks further from the main
centres of investor attention.

## What would refute it

- The lagged returns of upstream and downstream industries carry no information about an industry's
  subsequent returns; or the cross-predictability disappears once common exposures that are slow to
  be recognised are accounted for: part of the signal can reflect such exposures rather than
  diffusion along the chain.
- Industries that are not vertically linked show the same cross-predictability as linked ones, so the
  input-output link is not the channel.
- The effect is no weaker where investors are less specialised and where the economic chains are more
  transparent; the cross-predictability is no stronger for stocks with low institutional ownership,
  thin coverage, complex businesses or little investor attention than for widely held and
  well-covered ones.
- A shock in one industry is priced into the industries linked to it in input-output networks at the
  same time as into the industry itself, even where investors specialise by segment.
