## Mechanism

Revisions of analysts' earnings forecasts, upward ones especially, contain fundamental information
that the market does not incorporate immediately and completely. Upward revisions are followed by
positive returns and downward revisions by negative returns: when the consensus rises, the stocks
concerned tend to keep outperforming, and when it falls, to keep underperforming. A positive revision
often means that the future path of profits is better than what the price embedded; yet the price
reaction remains incomplete, and a drift follows. Revisions change expected value because they alter
expected sales, margins, cash flows and sometimes the duration of competitive advantage, and they
often capture fundamental information that goes beyond a single quarter, which makes them a channel
for price continuation at intermediate horizons. Analysts have privileged access to management,
through guidance calls and meetings, and when they update their models they incorporate non-public or
semi-public information that the price does not yet fully reflect.

Several frictions can produce the slow adjustment. Revisions are published in reports that most
investors do not read at once; investors treat revisions as sell-side "noise", although part of them
is genuinely informative; not all revisions carry the same amount of new information; sell-side and
buy-side analysts themselves update their views with a delay and move gradually towards the true
information, especially when information arrives piece by piece, and investors follow the revisions
progressively: individual revisions spread to the consensus, from the consensus to portfolios and from
portfolios to prices, a diffusion that takes several weeks or months. Some revisions signal a deeper
improvement in the fundamental drivers, and trading on the signal can be costly, especially in
low-priced or less liquid stocks. The theory describes underreaction to intermediate fundamental
improvements or deteriorations, between official reporting and market expectations.

It overlaps with post-earnings announcement drift and with earnings momentum without reducing to
them: the signal is the adjustment of intermediate expectations, not only the raw accounting
announcement or the observed earnings surprise, and revisions occur continuously between
announcements. By analogy, on equity index futures the revisions are aggregated over the
constituents of the index, and on commodity futures the analogue is revisions of estimates such as
harvest estimates in USDA reports.

## Prediction

Stocks whose earnings forecasts are revised upwards earn higher subsequent returns, and stocks whose
forecasts are revised downwards earn lower returns, often over one to six months after the revision;
upward revisions of consensus forecasts are associated with positive returns, and downward revisions
with negative returns, over the following 6 to 12 months, and Chan, Jegadeesh and Lakonishok (1996)
find a return differential of 7.7% over 12 months between the extremes of consensus revisions. The
effect is documented on US and international stocks. Revisions are positively correlated with
earnings surprises and earnings momentum but carry distinct information: the books stress that
revisions and surprises keep their predictive power even after controlling for past returns, so the
effect is not a cosmetic restatement of price momentum, and the combination of analyst revisions,
earnings surprises and price momentum is the most predictive. Several studies find the drift
attenuated in more recent samples, especially after transaction costs fell and information came to
spread better.

## What would refute it

- No drift after forecast revisions: prices that adjust fully on the day a revision is published;
  stocks with upward revisions performing no better over the following months than stocks with
  downward revisions.
- A revision drift that disappears entirely once subsequent earnings announcements are accounted for,
  or revisions that lose their predictive power once past returns and earnings surprises are
  controlled for.
- A drift no larger in low-priced and less liquid stocks than in liquid ones.
- Revisions that carry no information about subsequent sales, margins and cash flows.
