## Mechanism

Information diffuses slowly between firms held by the same institutional investors. An institution with
many positions learns something about one stock and then shifts its attention, its models or its
portfolio towards other stocks linked through its own holdings. Prices can therefore show a lead-lag
between firms that are apparently unrelated by industry but linked by common ownership: the "real"
economic link is replaced by an informational and organisational portfolio link.

The mechanism can be informational, a matter of coordination between shareholders, or one of portfolio
reallocation. An institution that discovers sector, macroeconomic or idiosyncratic information often
revalues several linked positions, but not necessarily at the same time: constraints of organisation,
delegation, turnover, processing and trading can spread its reaction over time. Meanwhile, the rest of
the market can take time to work out what institutional trades imply for the other stocks of the
network.

More than half of institutional investors hold highly concentrated portfolios of fewer than 60 stocks.
These specialised investors assimilate information firm by firm: when they obtain news about a sector or
a theme through one of their positions, they can pass it on slowly to their other, linked positions. The
process is reinforced if passive investors, such as index funds and ETFs, managing these concentrated
portfolios adjust their positions only after a delay. The effect rests on the inattention of passive
investors.

## Prediction

The literature shows cross-predictable returns between firms with common shareholders. A "leader" firm
that carries the information moves first, and its "peers" held by the same institutions move later.
Some studies emphasise shareholder coordination, others the gradual diffusion of information in
common-ownership networks. Firms with common institutional owners have fundamentals and prices that are
correlated with a lag (Ying, 2024). In Ying's results, a "peer" momentum based on common ownership
outperforms peer portfolios based on industry or on shared analysts and earns significant alphas. The
lagged correlations between the fundamentals (sales, assets) of commonly owned firms are stronger than
those with their industry peers or with firms covered by the same analysts. The effect is short to
medium term. An ETF redemption or a rebalancing can eventually arbitrage it, but Ying shows that
concentrated institutions that keep investing according to the past performance of the peers they hold
earn persistent gains.

## What would refute it

- The cross-predictability disappears once common exposure to the same factors is accounted for.
- The cross-predictability comes from portfolio pressure rather than from the diffusion of
  information.
- The lagged correlation between the fundamentals of commonly owned firms is no stronger than with their
  industry peers or with firms covered by the same analysts.
- Peer portfolios built on common ownership do no better than those built on industry or shared
  analysts.
- Information that concentrated institutional investors obtain through one position reaches their
  other, linked positions at once.
