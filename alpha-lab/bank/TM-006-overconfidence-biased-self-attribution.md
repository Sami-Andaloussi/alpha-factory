## Mechanism

Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam (1998) build a model on two psychological biases. Overconfidence
is an excessive belief in the precision of one's own private information. Biased self-attribution is
the habit of crediting successes to one's own skill and failures to external causes, so that
successes raise confidence more than failures lower it. A private signal triggers an initial
reaction. After favourable news that confirms the investor's decision, the investor reads every
favourable sign, including noise, as a confirmation, his confidence rises, and he trades further in
the same direction, pushing the price too far in the direction of the initial shock: an initial
overreaction to news. These asymmetric updates of confidence create a drift, the momentum, before a
later correction, when the news turns contradictory, produces possible reversals in the longer run.

In the model, momentum does not come from a slow incorporation of information or from an arbitrage
delay. It comes from an initial excess of optimism, built up by self-attribution and corrected only
partly and later: the price overshoots and then reverts. The mechanism rests on traders who seek to
confirm their recent success, whether individual traders or managers. It is not specific to
equities: it can arise in any asset class where traders overestimate their information, including
crypto-assets, given the intensity of speculative trading there. The persistence of the phenomenon is
compatible with limits to arbitrage: the "irrational" pricing is not simply crushed, because opposing
an overconfident crowd involves risk, capital and an uncertain timing.

## Prediction

Returns show positive autocorrelation in the short run (momentum, over horizons under 6 months) and
negative autocorrelation over longer horizons (reversal). The model's Proposition 7 establishes that
when investors' confidence changes gradually, updated through biased self-attribution, prices display
short-term return momentum followed by long-run reversals.

## What would refute it

No positive autocorrelation of returns in the short run (under 6 months), or no subsequent reversal
over longer horizons. Momentum that is fully explained by the slow incorporation of information, with
no overshooting of prices beyond fundamental value later corrected. Traders whose confidence does not
rise after successes more than it falls after failures, or whose rising confidence brings no further
trading in the same direction.
