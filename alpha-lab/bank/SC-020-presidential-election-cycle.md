## Mechanism

The presidential election cycle is a multi-year seasonality, studied mainly in US equities: equity market performance varies in a recurring way over the four-year US electoral cycle. Returns are higher in some years of the presidential term, historically often the third and fourth years, the second half of the term, and they also differ according to the party in power.

The explanation most often given in the books is the political business cycle, or political-budgetary cycle: before elections, the authorities would have an interest in supporting activity, liquidity or macroeconomic expectations, which would improve stock returns, so that the last two years of the term tend to be more favourable than the first two. The cycle is a recurring period defined by institutions, and it can change risk premia, volatility or appetite for risk. Other proposed mechanisms are a climate of public opinion acting on the taste for risk, and a redistribution of winners and losers according to firm size, with small and large capitalisations behaving differently depending on the party in power. The political calendar also includes a sub-cycle tied to Congress: market dynamics differ depending on whether Congress sits or not, and one hypothesis is that regulatory uncertainty is higher when Congress is active. Post-election effects are discussed as well.

The cycle is not as "mechanical" as the turn-of-the-month effect: it depends on a political, fiscal and monetary framework, and therefore on regimes, and it rests on a specific political and institutional setting.

## Prediction

US equity returns tend to be higher in the second half of the presidential term, historically often the third and fourth years, than in the first two. Among the regularities of the US political calendar that the books retain, returns also differ between Democratic and Republican administrations, and market dynamics differ according to whether Congress is in session; the books also discuss how small and large capitalisations behave differently depending on the party in power, and post-election effects.

Where and when it has been observed: in US equity data over long historical periods. Part of the literature presents this logic as a form of political business cycle; other work shows that the puzzle is real in the data but hard to tie cleanly to the macroeconomic policy actually observed. The books stress that the regularities depend on the political regime and on the structure of the market.

## What would refute it

- No difference in US equity returns between the first and the second half of the presidential term.
- Stronger returns late in the term that bear no relation to pre-election fiscal or monetary support, or that occur equally when policy was not expansionary, which would contradict the political business cycle.
- No difference in returns between administrations of different parties, or between periods when Congress is in session and periods when it is not.
- A cycle that does not recur out of sample.
