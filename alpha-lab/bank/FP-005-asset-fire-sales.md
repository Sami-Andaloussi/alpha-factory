## Mechanism

A fire sale is a forced sale of assets by constrained investors, at prices below long-term
fundamental value. The typical sellers are funds hit by redemptions, institutions facing margin
calls, regulated entities that must reduce risk, and investors bound by internal limits on
value-at-risk, leverage, concentration or liquidity. The point is not only that they sell, but that
they sell because they must, at the wrong moment, to intermediaries whose capacity to absorb the
flow is limited. The price move comes from these forced trades rather than from any revision of
fundamental information.

The mechanism rests on an asymmetry of balance sheets. The sellers face a hard and urgent
constraint; the potential buyers do not always have the capital, the risk tolerance or the
infrastructure to absorb the flow quickly. The price therefore falls further than fundamental
information warrants. Funds facing large outflows sell their existing positions rather than
re-optimising their portfolios, and when several funds hold the same names and face redemptions at
the same time, the selling concentrates on a subset of stocks. The assets hit hardest are usually
those most held by the constrained investors, the most illiquid ones, or the ones that are easiest
to sell within the portfolio, not necessarily those whose fundamentals have deteriorated most. The
last case sometimes produces a "paradoxical liquidation", in which the best or most liquid holdings
are sold first. The literature shows that the same pressure exists on the buying side: extreme
inflows push managers to scale up their existing positions quickly, so the effect runs in both
directions. The liquidation is also progressive: execution is spread over time and counterparties
are missing, so the imbalance in order flow can create a persistent directional path over a finite
window before the reversal.

The discount is not arbitraged away at once because the arbitrageur must buy what others are forced
to sell, often exactly when risk and the cost of capital are at their highest: the funding shocks
that force the sellers often hit the natural buyers too, so spare capital is scarce precisely when
buying is most profitable. Funding limits, the risk of further outflows and systemic risk prevent
the discount from being eliminated immediately. The correction comes when the constraints fade, or
when buyers of liquidity (contrarians, market makers) absorb the imbalance, and the later reversal
pays the investors able to supply capital and liquidity at the right moment. In equities, the forced
sellers include long-only managers facing redemptions or mandate constraints; by analogy, in
futures, the constrained participants can be commodity trading advisers, commodity merchants and
hedge funds on margin, in deleveraging episodes in which derivatives are used to adjust exposure
quickly; in crypto, they are centralised players or large holders that must liquidate inventory for
reasons of solvency, collateral or risk management. Fire sales are closely linked to liquidity
spirals, deleveraging, crowding and ownership fragility.

## Prediction

Assets sold by constrained investors fall below their fundamental value over the period during which
the sellers liquidate. The assets hit hardest are generally those most held by the constrained
investors, the most illiquid ones, or the ones easiest to sell within the portfolio; sometimes the
best or most liquid holdings are sold first. The deviation is temporary, and the selling phase can
be followed by high returns as prices recover. Symmetrically, stocks bought under pressure by funds
with extreme inflows rise and later revert. The effect tends to be stronger during episodes of
stress, in stocks with concentrated institutional ownership, and when arbitrage capital is itself
constrained. When several portfolios hold similar positions, fire sales can also spread across
assets. Fire sales have been documented empirically in equities and on mutual funds, in credit
crises, among hedge funds, and in stress episodes in which market liquidity and funding liquidity
deteriorate together.

## What would refute it

- Assets sold under constraint losing no more value than news about their fundamentals explains, or
  forced sales concentrated in the stocks with the worst fundamental news rather than in commonly
  held, easily sold stocks.
- No recovery, and no high returns, after the selling phase: price drops that are permanent. Stocks
  bought by funds with heavy inflows showing no price pressure, or no later reversal.
- Price pressure absorbed at once rather than spread over the liquidation window.
- Illiquid assets, and assets concentrated among constrained holders, falling no more than others
  during forced selling; no difference in price pressure between stocks held in common by distressed
  funds and comparable stocks that are not.
- Effects no stronger in stress episodes or when arbitrage capital is constrained; well-capitalised
  buyers absorbing forced sales without a price discount in periods when funding liquidity is
  scarce.
