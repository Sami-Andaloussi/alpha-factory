## Mechanism

Herding is the tendency of investors to imitate the decisions of others, which creates collective
price moves that can persist well beyond what fundamentals justify. Greyserman and Kaminski (2014)
count herding among the four main behavioural biases that feed trend following, with loss aversion,
home bias and panic.

Under uncertainty, individuals, professional managers included, look at what others do to validate
their own decisions, and this collective validation produces correlated and persistent market moves.
An institutional manager who sees the prices of an asset class fall and sees peers cut their
exposures is pushed to do the same, for fear of reputational risk, which amplifies the fall. The
process feeds itself: the fall in prices triggers herding out of the asset, the herding amplifies
the fall, and the deeper fall triggers more herding. The cycle can produce very strong downtrends
during crises.

## Prediction

Herding is particularly visible during market crises, such as 2008 and 2020, and during speculative
manias, such as the technology bubble of 2000 and the crypto-asset boom of 2017 to 2018. It is
documented on stocks, bonds, commodities and currencies. Trends created by herding can be irrational
and end abruptly: momentum crashes (Daniel and Moskowitz, 2016) often occur during a rapid reversal
of herding.

## What would refute it

Investors, professional managers included, whose decisions do not depend on the observed decisions of
their peers under uncertainty. Price falls that do not accelerate as more investors cut their
exposures. Herding no more visible during crises and speculative manias than at other times. Momentum
crashes unrelated to reversals in collective positioning.
