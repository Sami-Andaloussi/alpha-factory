## Mechanism

The market recurrently underpays for the economic quality of companies. Quality is an aggregate:
profitability, durable growth, safety and balance-sheet strength, stability, and sometimes payout. In
principle, higher-quality assets should trade at higher prices. They do trade somewhat higher, but
not enough, so that they subsequently deliver abnormally high returns. The mechanism put forward in
behavioural work is that investors and analysts focus more on stories, spectacular growth or
"lottery-like" assets than on sober but persistent economic quality.

The theory crosses the value family without being identical to it: it says that a high-quality asset
is not expensive enough relative to what it "should" be worth, even when it does not trade at a low
multiple in the traditional sense. Part of the effect overlaps with profitability, low risk,
investment discipline and solvency.

## Prediction

High-quality stocks earn higher subsequent returns than low-quality ("junk") stocks, although they
trade at only moderately higher valuations. The effect is documented on US and international stocks,
over a rather medium-term horizon of several months to several years, and is closely tied to the
gradual revaluation of expectations about profits and risks. The price of quality itself goes through
episodes of compression and expansion, which makes the effect cyclical.

## What would refute it

- Quality stocks priced at a premium commensurate with their quality, so that no excess return
  follows over full cycles of the price of quality.
- No residual effect of quality once profitability, low risk, investment discipline and solvency are
  taken into account separately.
- Valuations that give no premium to stories, spectacular growth or lottery-like features over sober,
  persistent quality.
