## Mechanism

The true fundamental yield returned to shareholders is not the simple dividend yield but the total
net payout: dividends plus share buybacks, possibly net of share issuance, sometimes supplemented by
debt reduction. Shareholder yield aggregates dividends and buybacks to measure what the company
actually returns to its shareholders; in its most careful version it also deducts share issuance,
without which the carry actually received by existing shareholders is overstated. The market often
looks at incomplete valuation metrics: a company that pays few dividends but sharply reduces its
share count, or avoids dilution, can be economically "cheap" despite a low dividend yield.

The mechanism is not only mechanical. It also captures capital allocation discipline: companies able
to return capital without damaging their balance sheet, and without depending on the equity market,
often have sounder cash flows and more disciplined management. Companies that distribute a lot of
capital while remaining healthy often signal that they do not need to overinvest to maintain their
franchise, or that they know how to avoid marginal, value-destroying projects. Conversely, companies
that promise both growth and yield but dilute heavily do not offer the same economics per share. The
theory overlaps with value, quality and capital discipline.

## Prediction

Stocks with a high net payout yield earn higher subsequent returns than stocks with a low one, in
time series as well as in the cross-section, depending on the study. Part of the academic literature
concerns the predictive power of payout measures at the aggregate level. In the empirical results
the library presents, shareholder yield is more robust than some simpler value ratios over long
periods, often with lower volatility.

A high payout can also signal a lack of attractive growth opportunities, so the same payout reads
differently across sectors and levels of business quality. The effect can be less clear where
buybacks are financed with debt or carried out opportunistically.

## What would refute it

- Net payout yield that predicts returns no better than the dividend yield, at the aggregate level or
  in the cross-section.
- No return difference between high and low net payout yield stocks over long periods.
- High-payout companies whose later returns are low because the payout reflected an absence of growth
  opportunities, across sectors and levels of business quality.
- Companies that promise growth and yield while diluting heavily and that deliver the same returns per
  share as companies with a high net payout.
