## Mechanism

Cross-sectional momentum is the relative form of price continuation: at a given date, the assets or
securities that have performed best over the recent past relative to their peers tend to keep
outperforming those that have performed worst, while the laggards keep underperforming. The signal
is relative. What matters is not an asset's absolute direction but its rank in the performance of
its universe, so the effect can exist even when every asset rises together: some rise faster and
more cleanly, and keep doing so. Practitioners long called it relative strength; academic work
later imposed the word momentum. The word describes price behaviour and does not by itself give the
economic cause. The effect is also distinct from growth investing: the winners are not necessarily
"good companies" but securities whose recent path reveals that favourable information has not yet
been fully incorporated, or that buying flows persist.

The proposed explanations converge on a combination of causes. The first is underreaction to
information. Through conservatism (Edwards, 1968, applied to markets by Barberis, Shleifer and
Vishny, 1998), investors put too little weight on new information and cling to their priors, so
after good news about a firm or a sector the price underreacts at first and then keeps drifting in
the direction of the shock for several months; Chan, Jegadeesh and Lakonishok (1996) highlight in
particular this angle of earnings news and underreaction. The second, complementary to the pure
behavioural bias and partly an alternative to it, is the slow and uneven diffusion of news (Hong,
Lim and Stein, 2000): information takes time to reach every participant, and analysts and managers
adjust with delay. The third is the pursuit of past winners through flows: winners progressively
attract more capital, which creates a persistent buying pressure, and losers the reverse. Part of
the continuation is specific to the firm; another part comes from industries, countries or common
factors that are transmitted with a delay.

The effect is not arbitraged away because arbitrage is limited. Rational arbitrageurs who would
correct the mispricing face market risk (the mispricing can widen before it closes, the risk created
by noise traders), transaction costs, short-selling constraints, and leverage and margin
constraints. Holding the strategy also means bearing rare but violent crashes and asymmetric tails,
a dependence on the short book, high turnover, and long spells of underperformance against
benchmarks. The literature on the causes remains plural: behavioural, risk-based and institutional
explanations coexist.

Cross-sectional momentum differs from time-series momentum. The latter asks whether an asset keeps
its own direction; the former asks which asset is strongest relative to the others. The two can
coexist, complement each other or offset each other depending on the environment.

## Prediction

A portfolio long past winners and short past losers earns abnormally high returns at intermediate
horizons, historically 3 to 12 months; the regularity appears mainly at horizons of a few months,
often 6 to 12 months. Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) found that US stocks (NYSE, AMEX and NASDAQ, 1965
to 1989) with the highest returns over the previous 3 to 12 months kept outperforming over the
following 3 to 12 months; they tested whether this persistence was a reward for systematic risk and
found that the CAPM-adjusted alphas remained positive and significant. In equities the optimal
formation period is 6 to 12 months, with the most recent month skipped to avoid contamination by the
short-term reversal, and the holding period is 3 to 12 months. The books stress that the effect is
not stable at every horizon: very short horizons are often polluted by microstructure effects and
very long horizons by reversals. Rouwenhorst (1998) extended the finding to 12 European markets, and
the regularity has been documented over decades and in many international and cross-asset universes
(Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen, 2013).

Several conditional patterns follow. Momentum is significantly stronger among stocks with low
analyst coverage, where information travels more slowly (Hong, Lim and Stein, 2000). A large part of
individual stock momentum is explained by industry momentum, winners tending to belong to winning
industries (Moskowitz and Grinblatt, 1999). Momentum is particularly weak or negative in January,
when recent losers rebound strongly after year-end tax-loss selling and window dressing. It suffers
violent crashes during regime reversals or rebounds after a bear market: when a sharply falling
market rebounds, recent losers surge and the short side loses heavily (Daniel and Moskowitz, 2016);
such crashes are more frequent when market volatility is high. Momentum and value are negatively
correlated in the short run, since value stocks are often recent losers and the reverse, and a
combination of the two has a higher Sharpe ratio than either style alone (Asness, Moskowitz and
Pedersen, 2013).

## What would refute it

Past winners over 3 to 12 months, with the latest month skipped, earning no more than past losers
over the following 3 to 12 months, in the US or in other markets, or the spread vanishing once
exposure to systematic risk is accounted for. Momentum no stronger among stocks with low analyst
coverage than among well-covered stocks. A continuation that is entirely explained by industries or
common factors, leaving no firm-specific part. Firm news fully incorporated in the price on
announcement, with no subsequent drift in the direction of the news. No weakening of momentum in
January, and no losses on the short side when markets rebound after a sharp decline.
