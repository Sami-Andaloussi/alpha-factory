## Mechanism

A large part of mean reversion plays out not only against the overall market but within groups of economically close assets. When a stock strongly outperforms or underperforms its sector or industry peers after a non-informative shock, a localised flow or an order imbalance, the relative spread then tends to normalise. The mechanism is "close" relative value: firms in the same industry share macroeconomic exposure, technology, value chain and investor clientele, so a non-fundamental idiosyncratic shock, or a flow concentrated on a single name, can make its relative performance temporarily inconsistent with that of its peers. The later correction is a return to a more plausible relative structure.

Ilmanen (2011) and Narang (2024) describe sector relative value as a strategy that exploits the deviations of an asset's valuation from its peers within the same sector or group: two assets in the same sector share the same macroeconomic and sector factors, and differences in their relative valuation are often transitory and mean-reverting. In futures it shows up as intra-sector spreads, such as WTI against Brent crude or corn against soybeans, that return towards their historical levels. The reversion operates through arbitrageurs who trade intra-sector fundamental deviations and through the sector rotation of institutional investors who rebalance between assets of the same sector. Narang (2024) distinguishes intrinsic reversion, of an asset towards its own historical value, from relative reversion, towards its peers; relative value is generally less sensitive to changes in the macroeconomic regime. Structural changes (mergers, new entrants, technological disruption) can break relative-value relationships. The traders involved are long/short sector funds, statistical arbitrage funds and commodity spread traders.

The phenomenon persists because it requires absorbing very specific imbalances, sometimes in securities where few investors are mandated to trade relative value within a group. Sector flows, thematic ETFs and specialised mandates can also sustain the gaps for a while.

## Prediction

Assets that strongly underperform their industry or sector peers tend subsequently to outperform them, and the reverse for strong outperformers. The literature shows that intra-industry reversals of stock returns are often stronger than standard reversals measured against the market, and that they remain present even among large, liquid stocks, which supports the idea that they are not only a matter of microcaps or quote noise but of price pressure and localised non-informational imbalances. In commodities, intra-sector spreads such as WTI–Brent and corn–soybeans return towards their historical levels.

## What would refute it

- Relative performance against industry peers that does not normalise.
- Intra-industry reversals no stronger than reversals measured against the market.
- Reversals confined to microcaps and illiquid securities, which would point to quote noise rather than price pressure.
- Relative-value relationships broken durably by structural changes such as mergers, new entrants or technological disruption.
