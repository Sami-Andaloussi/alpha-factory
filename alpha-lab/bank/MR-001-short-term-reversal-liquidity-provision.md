## Mechanism

Short-term reversals are explained as the remuneration of supplying immediacy. When impatient investors must buy or sell at once, they push the price away from its short-term fundamental value. The counterparties who absorb this flow (market makers, dealers, arbitrageurs with risk capital) bear inventory risk, adverse-selection risk and funding risk, and they demand compensation for absorbing a temporary imbalance: the demand for immediacy is not free. The later rebound of the price is the dissipation of this liquidity concession rather than a genuine discovery of information.

Read as asset pricing, short-term reversals can remunerate the supply of liquidity: buying after a fall, and selling after a rise, typically means taking the other side of a liquidity trade. When the market-making and liquidity-provision sector is constrained (by capital, margin or risk aversion), or when volatility rises, the compensation it requires rises too, which reinforces, at least episodically, the size of the observed reversal. The theory is close to models of funding constraints in which prices become more sensitive to non-informational flows while the constraints bind, then correct as the constraints loosen. By analogy, in futures, part of short-term reversal can be read not as an inefficiency but as a liquidity risk premium that depends on the state of the market: stress, volatility, drawdowns.

The effect is not fully arbitraged away because providing liquidity consumes balance sheet and exposes the provider to the risk that the flow continues, to funding constraints and to execution costs. It is less a free lunch than a premium for absorbing temporary imbalances. In markets where the investor base is more retail and depth is lower, the same logic can be even more visible.

## Prediction

Assets that have fallen over the past days to a month outperform assets that have risen, over the following days to month: short-term returns reverse. The pattern is observed in stocks, mostly at daily to monthly horizons. It is stronger:

- in illiquid stocks;
- in periods of market stress, when the capital of liquidity providers appears most constrained;
- after large idiosyncratic falls;
- when the past move is unrelated to fundamental news: studies show explicitly that past returns not explained by cash-flow news or fundamental revisions are the ones that reverse most.

The literature shows that the profits of short-term reversal behave like returns to liquidity provision: they vary strongly over time and are higher when market stress is high. Farag et al. (2025) study returns from liquidity provision in cryptocurrency markets.

## What would refute it

- Reversal returns that do not rise with market stress, volatility or the tightness of liquidity providers' capital, or that are constant over time.
- Reversals as strong in liquid stocks as in illiquid ones.
- Past moves driven by cash-flow news reversing as much as moves unexplained by news.
- Price concessions after large liquidity-driven trades that persist instead of reverting.
- Reversal profits that remain after paying the full cost of supplying immediacy (execution, funding, inventory risk), which would make the pattern an anomaly rather than a compensation.
