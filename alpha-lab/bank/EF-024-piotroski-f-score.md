## Mechanism

The market treats value stocks too homogeneously. Among stocks with a high book-to-market ratio, some
are companies temporarily neglected but financially sound, while others are genuine value traps whose
fundamentals are deteriorating. Among apparently cheap stocks, those that combine a discount with a
fundamental improvement of the balance sheet, liquidity, profitability and operating efficiency have
a much better economic quality than "rotten" discounts. The F-score seeks to isolate them with simple
but informative accounting signals: profitability, cash flow generation, the change in leverage,
liquidity, dilution, margins and asset turnover, that is, operating efficiency.

The cause is twofold. On one side, investors focus too much on raw cheapness and not enough on the
direction of the fundamentals, and too often lump all discounted stocks together, although some are
improving and others deteriorating fast. On the other, companies that are quietly improving are less
salient than spectacular stories. The price corrects as financial quality and operating improvement
become visible. The logic is consistent with Graham and Dodd's philosophy: a discount marks an
undervalued stock only if the balance sheet can absorb the cycle, liquidity holds and the
deterioration is not structural. The mispricing persists because synthesising several signals takes
more work than using a single multiple.

## Prediction

Within high book-to-market stocks, companies with a high F-score earn higher subsequent returns than
companies with a low F-score; the setting is mainly equities, with annual rebalancing. The signal
lives mainly inside the value segment and is not a general statement about the whole market; it may
also capture a combination of lower distress, less manipulation and improving profitability.

## What would refute it

- No return difference between high and low F-score stocks among high book-to-market stocks.
- Value stocks with improving fundamentals priced at a premium over deteriorating ones from the
  outset, so that no correction follows when the improvement becomes visible.
- Improvements in profitability, cash flow, leverage and liquidity that do not persist, so that the
  F-score does not separate future winners from losers.
