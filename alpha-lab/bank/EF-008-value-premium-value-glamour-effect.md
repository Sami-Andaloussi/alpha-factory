## Mechanism

The market pays too much for assets whose recent growth looks attractive, and too little for those
whose existing fundamentals are unloved, cyclical, complicated or temporarily impaired. The classic
behavioural version is extrapolation: investors extend past growth trends too far, underestimate
competitive reversion, and overvalue "glamour" stocks at the expense of stocks trading at low
multiples. Cheap assets often become cheap because the market extrapolates bad recent news, slowing
growth, compressed margins or narrative disaffection too far, while glamour assets embed
over-optimistic expectations: naive investors overweight recent events, overestimate the persistence
of poor performance and underestimate future normalisation. Value assets are priced low because
markets over-extrapolate their poor current prospects (over-pessimism), and the correction, through a
return to normal levels of profitability, is a reversion towards fundamental value. Relative
valuations eventually normalise as extreme growth slows or expectations cool. The reversion does not
come only from a multiple that "recovers", but from a readjustment of expectations of growth,
profitability and risk: the driver is a mean reversion of expectations more than a simple preference
for low accounting ratios.

A more structural version stresses mandate constraints, career risk, benchmark hugging and a
reluctance to buy "ugly" but cheap companies; the books insist that the premium can also be
understood as the reward for holding a position that is psychologically costly and structurally
unpopular, taken against the extrapolative consensus. The mispricing is not fully arbitraged away
because the correction can be slow, socially costly for the fund manager, and can pass through long
phases of underperformance: it can take months or years to materialise, which explains both its
persistence and the difficulty of holding it, and value assets can stay cheap for a long time, suffer
a real deterioration of their fundamentals or go through deep drawdowns. The mechanism mixes
psychology, accounting, macroeconomic risk and the limits to arbitrage.

Three explanations are debated for the resulting premium: compensation for risk, mismeasurement of
fundamentals, and behavioural mispricing. In the risk-based version, value stocks carry specific
cash-flow or distress risks. Ilmanen (2011) distinguishes several channels: the migration of
price-to-book ratios towards the median ("value migration"); the convergence of profitability, as
returns on equity revert towards normal sector levels; and a countercyclical risk premium, since
value is risky in crises.

By analogy, on equity index futures value can be approximated by aggregate ratios such as the
earnings yield or the dividend yield of the index, relative to its own history or to its peers; on
commodities, by the gap between the current price and the marginal cost of production, taken as the
long-term fundamental value. The theory is related to long-term reversal, momentum, carry and factor
risk premia.

## Prediction

Stocks trading at low multiples of their fundamentals (value stocks) earn higher subsequent returns
than stocks trading at high multiples (glamour stocks). The effect has been documented on US stocks,
then on international stocks, with several fundamental anchors: book-to-market, earnings yield,
cash-flow yield, sales-to-price and sometimes dividend yield. Stocks with low prices relative to
book value (the HML factor) have historically outperformed (Fama and French 1992, 1993), and the
value premium is observed in many countries and asset classes; Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny
(1994) emphasise investors' extrapolation. The natural horizon is rather medium to long, typically
several months to several years, because the effect rests on the gradual revision of expectations
about profitability, cash flow and implied growth, and on the progressive normalisation of
multiples, of the growth spread and of implicit expectations; the horizon is long, three to five
years, for the migration of price-to-book ratios. Other work shows that value and momentum are
distinct and often opposite forces, value capturing rather a convergence towards a fundamental
relation. Value interacts with quality: a large part of the literature shows that "pure" value does
better when cheap but sound companies are separated from genuine value traps.

Some discounts reflect genuine structural problems, some expensive stocks stay expensive for a long
time, and the cyclicality of the value style can be violent. The effect depends on reporting
standards, on the intensity of intangible assets, on sector composition and on the macroeconomic
regime. The value premium has been compressed since 2010, a change attributed to crowding and to
structural changes.

## What would refute it

- No return difference between value and glamour stocks over several months to several years,
  whichever fundamental anchor is used.
- Past growth trends of glamour stocks that continue as extrapolated, so that expectations are not
  revised downwards, and value stocks whose depressed fundamentals do not recover; no normalisation
  of valuation multiples or of the growth spread after extreme readings.
- A value premium fully accounted for by the higher risk of value stocks (distress or cash-flow
  risk), leaving no role for extrapolation, for expectation errors in the reversion of growth and
  profitability, or for institutional constraints.
