## Mechanism

Mitchell, Pedersen and Pulvino (2007) study how the capital constraints of arbitrageurs prolong
price imbalances, hence momentum. When arbitrage funds, or a small group of investors, suffer
losses, their capital shrinks: their investors withdraw money or demand liquidity, and the managers
must liquidate positions massively to meet redemptions and margin calls. The forced selling
accentuates the falls of the losers and postpones the normalisation of prices, while purchases of
winners are limited. Because no new entrants with comparable infrastructure and information are
ready to step in, the positions are not rebuilt immediately, even when the market recovers, and
prices stay away from fundamental value for longer than expected, even without any new fundamental
information.

The economic mechanism is the inertia of reinvestment: capital that has been lost does not come back
at once to exploit the arbitrage. The actors are hedge funds, their investors and the providers of
credit and margin that finance the positions. After the 1987 crash, hedge funds liquidated their
value positions by selling losers, which prolonged the downtrend because nobody bought along the way;
after the LTCM crisis of 1998, arbitrageurs sold heavily and no fresh capital arrived immediately to
restore fundamental prices. In convertible bond arbitrage, funds first sell their underperforming
securities to cover losses, pushing prices below what is justified, and it takes several months for
capital to be reinvested. Even in assets that are not constrained on the short side, the slow return
of capital acts as a temporary force of momentum. The mechanism belongs to the wider framework of the
limits to arbitrage (market frictions), and it is at work mostly in periods of stress (high
volatility).

## Prediction

After losses and redemptions among arbitrageurs, the prices of the securities they held stay away
from fundamental value for longer than expected, in a prolonged downward (or upward) trend, and the
normalisation of prices is postponed. The momentum horizon is typically a few months, the length of
the "famine" of arbitrage capital; the theory suggests a horizon of several weeks to a few months,
limited by the speed at which capital returns. In practice the phenomenon is observed in debt and
convertible bond arbitrage strategies, and it can also concern equities when markets are stressed.
Studies of arbitrage crashes (LTCM in 1998, 2008) show that price anomalies are corrected slowly
after the crisis.

## What would refute it

Price dislocations that close quickly after the arbitrageurs' losses, without waiting for their
capital to be rebuilt. Fresh capital that enters immediately after forced liquidations. Forced sales
by arbitrage funds that leave no lasting trace in the prices of the securities they held. No stronger
continuation of dislocations in periods of stress than in calm periods.
