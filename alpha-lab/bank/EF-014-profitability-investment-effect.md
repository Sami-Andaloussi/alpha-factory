## Mechanism

The starting point is a valuation identity: at equal price, a more profitable company is worth more;
at given profitability, a company that invests aggressively without sufficient profitability must
offer lower future returns or be overvalued. In the rational reading, the profitability and
investment patterns fit a q-theory of expected returns. In the behavioural reading, the market too
often overestimates the value of growth financed by heavy investment, and underestimates the value of
profits that are already observable but "boring".

It is not a "pure anomaly" in the narrow sense: part of the literature presents it as compensation
for risk, another part as a mistaken extrapolation of profits and investment. The logic is more
general: the price of an asset should reflect the combination of current cash flows, the marginal
return on capital and reinvestment discipline. In practice, the effect overlaps with value,
profitability, asset growth and the quality of management.

## Prediction

At equal price, more profitable firms earn higher subsequent returns than less profitable ones; at
given profitability, firms that invest aggressively without sufficient profitability earn lower
subsequent returns, or are overvalued. Empirically, this logic has been formalised in the
profitability factor (RMW, robust minus weak) and the investment factor (CMA, conservative minus
aggressive), and related to many other fundamental phenomena. Its main setting is the monthly and
annual cross-section of equities.

## What would refute it

- At equal price, no relation between profitability and subsequent returns.
- At given profitability, firms that invest aggressively earning subsequent returns no lower than
  firms that invest conservatively.
- Growth financed by heavy investment that is priced no higher, relative to the profitability it
  later delivers, than observable but unexciting profits.
