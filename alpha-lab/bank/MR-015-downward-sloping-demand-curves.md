## Mechanism

Even for financial assets, demand is not perfectly elastic. Because assets are not perfect substitutes, a non-fundamental demand shock can move the price substantially, sometimes beyond what fundamental information justifies in the short run. When a constrained or captive clientele must buy a security, its price rises; when it must sell, the price falls. Part of the move can be permanent if the shock durably changes the base of holders, but part is transitory and reverses as the demand elasticity of other investors comes into play. The correction, a mean reversion or normalisation, can occur if part of the pressure is transitory and other investors (arbitrageurs, value investors, market makers) take the other side when costs and risks allow.

The mechanism does not rest on a fundamental miscalculation but on client segmentation, mandate constraints, benchmark effects, index management, regulatory restrictions and, more generally, the imperfect substitutability of securities. A price can move away from a coherent level not because information has changed but because a specific population must trade. A major empirical channel is index flows: when captive investors (index funds, trackers) must buy or sell for mechanical reasons, they create price pressure. The theory concerns in particular markets where there are buyers or sellers who do not maximise their Sharpe ratio or who are constrained in their timing. By analogy, in futures, which are preferred instruments for beta exposure, flows of indexing, rebalancing or hedging can create temporary misalignments (in the basis, in spreads between contracts) that later normalise.

The effect persists because arbitrage capital is finite, risky and sometimes itself benchmarked. Even when the pressure is recognised as non-fundamental, the correction can be slow: it takes balance sheet, a long enough horizon, and the capacity to tolerate non-fundamental demand that lasts longer than expected. Arbitrage frictions (capital, margin, balance-sheet constraints) and the concentration of the flows explain the persistence.

## Prediction

Non-fundamental demand shocks can move prices in the direction of the forced trade, and part of the move can reverse afterwards as other investors take the other side. The effect has been documented in index inclusions, benchmark changes, large blocks, ETF flows, regulated clienteles, and markets where some securities have a scarce float or a particular captive demand. Schnitzler (2018) studies S&P 500 inclusions and stock supply. By analogy, in futures, indexing, rebalancing or hedging flows can create temporary misalignments of the basis and of inter-contract spreads, which then normalise.

## What would refute it

- Prices that do not move in response to large, predictable, non-fundamental demand shocks, as perfectly elastic demand would imply.
- Price moves after such shocks that are entirely permanent, with no transitory part, or entirely explained by information revealed by the event.
- Price effects unrelated to the substitutability of the security, the size of the forced trade or the capital available to arbitrageurs.
