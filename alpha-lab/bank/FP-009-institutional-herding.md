## Mechanism

Institutional herding is the tendency of professional managers to buy or sell the same securities at
the same time. It can come from shared information, but also from career incentives, benchmarking,
strategic imitation, regulatory constraints, or uniform reactions to the same signals of performance
and risk. Part of institutional investors also behave as positive feedback traders, follow each
other, or both, which can mechanically produce an autocorrelated demand: changes in institutional
holdings are correlated with contemporaneous returns (Nofsinger and Sias 1999), and the demand of
institutions in one quarter is correlated with their demand in the previous quarter (Sias 2004).
This inertia is read as institutions following other institutions and following their own past
trades, a direct source of persistence in flows. Herding turns dispersed decisions into concentrated
aggregate flows, which amplify demand or supply in particular names; predictable imbalances form out
of the similar constraints that managers share.

Herding is not arbitraged away because it follows from the structure of delegated management.
Managers are judged relative to one another, benchmarked, overseen by risk committees and compared
with their peers, so it can be rational for them to be wrong together rather than right alone.
Arbitrageurs cannot always stand against coordinated waves of institutional allocation, especially
when these are backed by client flows or index reallocations. By analogy, in futures, the
corresponding channels are the management of allocated portfolios, progressive reallocation and the
capacity and risk constraints of some operators, without requiring high-frequency trading. Herding
is closely connected with crowding, ownership fragility and common fund flows.

## Prediction

Institutions trade the same stocks in the same direction more often than independent decisions would
produce, and the stocks they herd into or out of move in the direction of the herd. Institutional
demand in a quarter is correlated with institutional demand in the previous quarter, and changes in
institutional ownership move with contemporaneous returns. Part of the literature shows that herding
is stronger in some segments, for example large capitalisations, highly visible stocks or periods of
stress; another part shows that herding can be destabilising in the short run but sometimes reveals
information over the medium run, depending on the category of institutions. The phenomenon has been
studied in equities.

## What would refute it

- Institutional trades in the same stocks no more correlated than independent decisions would
  produce; institutional demand in a quarter unrelated to institutional demand in the previous
  quarter.
- Changes in institutional holdings unrelated to past or contemporaneous returns.
- Herding no stronger in large, highly visible stocks or in periods of stress than elsewhere.
- Stocks bought or sold in concert by institutions showing no price pressure in the direction of the
  herd.
- Herding no stronger among managers who are benchmarked and compared with their peers than among
  investors free of such incentives.
