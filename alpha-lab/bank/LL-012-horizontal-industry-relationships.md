## Mechanism

Information diffuses slowly between firms or industries that are linked horizontally rather than
vertically (customer-supplier): direct competitors, producers of substitutes, firms and industries
that serve the same demand base or share the same customers, the same inputs or the same demand
shocks, and players in the same competitive space. News revealed about one firm often has
implications for peers that are not linked to it vertically, but the market does not always
incorporate them at once. A rise in energy prices, a technological innovation, a regulatory change or
a shift in demand can show up first in a "source" industry and migrate with a delay to substitute or
competing industries.

Two channels carry it. The first is economic substitution: when prices or margins change in one
industry, the customers and the profits of substitute sectors are eventually affected too. The second
is exposure to a common but secondary segment: a piece of news can be immediately relevant to the
industry in which that segment is central, and only later to another industry in which it is just one
part of the business model; this asymmetry of salience creates a lead-lag.

How the information travels depends on the kind of horizontal relationship. Good news about one firm can
be good for its peers when it reveals stronger aggregate demand, and bad for them when it reveals that
the firm is taking market share. The information is therefore harder to interpret than a simple
customer-supplier link, and this ambiguity slows diffusion: investors first have to work out whether the
news is a common demand shock, a cost shock, a competitive displacement or a technological read-through.
The literature documents predictability of returns between horizontally connected industries, which
reinforces the idea that the market processes the primary signal faster than its general-equilibrium
implications across the competitive landscape. The phenomenon seems particularly likely in complex
settings, where relations of substitution and complementarity are numerous but not easy to observe. The
effect concerns sectors that look different but are linked by demand, competition or inputs.

## Prediction

Returns are predictable between horizontally connected industries: news priced first in a source firm
or industry is reflected later in its competitors, its substitutes, the industries sharing its
customers or its inputs, and the industries for which the affected segment is a secondary line of
business, so that the returns of the source industry predict the subsequent returns of these related
industries. The sign of the transmission depends on the nature of the news and can be unstable: good
news about one firm can later be good news for its peers when it reveals stronger aggregate demand, and
bad news for them when it reveals that the firm is taking market share.

## What would refute it

- The returns of an industry carry no information about the subsequent returns of its competitors,
  its substitutes, or the industries that share its customers or inputs; horizontally linked firms or
  industries show no more cross-predictability than pairs that are merely correlated: the relation
  would then be correlation, not an economic link.
- The sign of the predictability bears no relation to the nature of the news.
- Ambiguous news, whose meaning for peers is unclear, reaches the peers' prices as fast as
  unambiguous news; industries for which a segment is secondary price news about that segment at the
  same time as the industries in which it is central.
- The apparent lead-lag between horizontally related industries is entirely due to customer-supplier
  links between them.
