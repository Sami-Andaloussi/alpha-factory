## Mechanism

On proof-of-work crypto assets, money creation runs through the block rewards paid to miners: mining
produces a continuous flow of new units. Miners receive this inventory as payment for their
contribution to the security of the network, and they bear real, recurring costs, in electricity and
energy, hardware, debt and financing, hosting, maintenance, capital tied up and opportunity risk.
They therefore often have to sell part of the newly created tokens, converting them into fiat,
stablecoins or more liquid collateral to fund their operations, which makes them an identifiable
source of potential sales. The resulting selling pressure has nothing to do with the immediate use
value of the asset and is distinct from mere speculative profit-taking: it is a recurring,
non-discretionary flow of supply native to the protocol, from a clearly identifiable category of
participants. The price thus depends not only on utility or speculative demand but also on the way
the security design produces a flow of new tokens and of potential sales.

Miners face a real cost function that is often rigid in the short run, and cash constraints: they
are not "neutral" holders. Their selling depends on the price of the token, the hashrate,
competition among miners and the way they are financed. When margins are squeezed, because
difficulty rises, the price falls sharply or the price of energy rises, miners may be forced to sell
a larger share of their inventory, or even to liquidate the reserves they have accumulated; in
periods of stress they can become non-discretionary sellers. When margins are comfortable, they can
keep a larger share of their output, which reduces the supply reaching the market at once. The
pressure also grows when rewards are large relative to the depth of the market. The flow thus arises
from the production technology itself. It is reinforced by the concentration of mining in pools, by
investment in specialised hardware, and by the transition of the reward from the block subsidy to
transaction fees, and it can change after halvings, which abruptly alter the issuance structure. The
theoretical economics of Bitcoin stresses this coupling between security, issuance and price, and
other work explores the concentration of mining pools and its implications for asset pricing. The
same channel exists, in another form, for proof-of-stake validators. The speed at which rewards are
recycled varies, and some validators can keep, hedge or refinance them. The theory is more specific
to certain crypto assets than to equities.

The pressure persists because it is written into the protocol and the economics of mining: issuance
is built into the protocol, the security of the network must be funded continuously, and as long as
block rewards exist and operating costs are paid in fiat currency or in stable value, part of the
newly created coins tends to be sold. It is not fully arbitraged because it is a genuine inflow of
primary supply that buyers must absorb, because it is repeated, because it can become heavy relative
to marginal demand at times, and because miners do not always have the financial room to wait.

## Prediction

Newly mined coins create a permanent background of selling pressure on proof-of-work assets, with
episodes of acceleration when miners' margins are squeezed: after sharp price falls, rises in
difficulty or rises in energy prices, miners can be forced to sell a larger share of their output,
or even to liquidate their accumulated reserves; the pressure is also stronger when rewards are
large relative to market depth. When margins are comfortable, miners can retain more of their
production, which reduces the supply reaching the market at once. The horizon can be continuous,
with regime breaks around halvings, energy-cost shocks or episodes of stress in the mining sector.
The phenomenon has been documented mainly on Bitcoin and, in another form, on proof-of-stake.

## What would refute it

- Miners' sales that do not rise when their margins are squeezed by lower prices, higher difficulty
  or higher energy prices, or when rewards are large relative to market depth.
- Flows from miners' wallets to the market unrelated to subsequent prices.
- No difference in the supply reaching the market between periods of squeezed and comfortable
  mining margins.
- Newly issued coins mostly kept, hedged or refinanced by miners rather than sold, with no link
  between issuance and selling flows.
- Halvings, which abruptly cut issuance, leaving the selling flow of miners unchanged.
