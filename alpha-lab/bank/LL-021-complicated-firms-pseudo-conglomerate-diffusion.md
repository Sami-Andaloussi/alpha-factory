## Mechanism

Some firms are "complicated": their value depends heavily on stakes, divisions or exposures nested in
other companies. The market often prices the simple, observable component faster than its indirect
implications for the linked entities, so a lead-lag can appear between the underlying firm and the
complicated firm.

The mechanism is informational complexity. When a firm holds stakes in others, runs very heterogeneous
segments or resembles a pseudo-conglomerate, investors have to aggregate several layers of information
before they can revise its price correctly. News about the related companies is therefore passed on to
the complicated firm with a delay. The gap can persist because mapping the economic structure is costly
and not very salient, and few players other than specialists arbitrage it. The delay rests less on the
raw slowness of the market than on a structural difficulty in aggregating information.

## Prediction

The literature shows that the returns of the underlying firms, or of the firms economically nested in a
complicated firm, predict the later returns of the complicated firm. The pattern concerns a relatively
specific set of firms: those with observable corporate structures, such as stakes in other firms or
several heterogeneous segments.

## What would refute it

- The returns of the linked entities predict the returns of complicated firms no better than they
  predict the returns of simple firms with the same exposures.
- Complicated firms reflect news about their related companies as fast as those companies do.
- The later move of the complicated firm reflects a mismeasurement of its fundamentals rather than a
  slow transmission of news from the linked entities.
