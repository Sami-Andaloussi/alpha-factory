## Mechanism

The value theory holds that an asset can remain under- or overvalued relative to its fundamentals
for a long time because investors over-extrapolate recent trends, overpay for growth stories and
underweight the distant cash flows of neglected assets, and because professional investors bear a
career cost if they look contrarian too early. Comparing assets, sectors, countries or asset classes
on price relative to fundamentals separates cheap groups from expensive ones. The books stress that
the mechanism reaches well beyond the selection of individual stocks: it appears in country allocation
and, more broadly, in several asset classes.

It persists because convergence to fair value is slow, expensive assets can keep rising for a long
time, benchmarks push managers to follow the market, and arbitrage is risky.

## Prediction

Assets, sectors, countries and asset classes that are cheap on price relative to fundamentals
outperform those that are expensive. The horizon ranges from several weeks to several years,
depending on the asset. The limits of the effect are value traps, structural changes in fundamentals
and prolonged periods dominated by momentum.

## What would refute it

- Groups that are cheap relative to fundamentals do not outperform expensive ones, whether among
  stocks, across countries or across asset classes.
- Low prices relative to fundamentals turn out to be value traps or to reflect structural changes in
  fundamentals, so that cheapness predicts nothing about subsequent relative returns.
- Expensive assets outperform cheap ones across full samples, and not only during the prolonged
  phases dominated by momentum that are the effect's known limit.
