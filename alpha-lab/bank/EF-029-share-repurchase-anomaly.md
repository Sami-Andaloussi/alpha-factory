## Mechanism

Share repurchases, especially open-market ones, are often a managerial action consistent with
undervaluation. The market reacts positively to the announcement, but not enough, and abnormal
returns persist afterwards. Two mechanisms are put forward. The first is the signal of
undervaluation: management, better informed about prospects and investment needs, chooses to buy
back when the price is too low relative to intrinsic value. The second is the correction of an
earlier overreaction of the market to temporary bad news.

The mechanism is the reverse of net share issuance. The literature on repurchases shows both an
informational component and a structural component of float reduction: a repurchase reduces the
effective supply of shares available to outside investors, so that the residual demand of the market
bears on a smaller base of shares. The effect is therefore not a mere announcement signal: the
tradable capital structure really changes, sometimes gradually and durably, and two fundamentally
comparable firms can behave differently if one of them shrinks its float aggressively. The books
treat repurchases as a payout policy distinct from the dividend; a repurchase can convey several
things at once: a signal of undervaluation, discipline in the use of cash, tax optimisation relative
to the dividend, and an adjustment of the capital structure. Unlike the dividend, the buyback is
flexible and discretionary; it lets management express a view on the value of the stock without
committing to a stable flow. By analogy, the mechanism bears on crypto protocols with burns,
buyback-and-burn schemes or similar mechanisms that withdraw supply durably.

A buyback is also a capital allocation decision, and it creates value only under a strict condition,
the "golden rule" the library makes explicit: the company must buy its shares below their expected
value and must not have a better use for the capital. A buyback below intrinsic value transfers
value from the selling shareholders to those who stay; a buyback above it does the reverse. When a
company retires capital at a low price, it mechanically creates value for the remaining shareholders
if the business is truly undervalued. The mispricing persists because many buybacks actually serve
to manage earnings per share, stock market optics or compensation structures rather than to create
value. Not all buybacks are disciplined: some are opportunistic, or financed with debt at the top of
the cycle, and buybacks can also serve to smooth earnings per share or to mask a shortage of growth
opportunities. The effect also persists because management is presumed to be better informed than
the market about fundamental value and about the alternative uses of capital, and because of
frictions: uncertainty about whether the programme will actually be executed, the difficulty for
investors of telling opportunistic repurchases from purely distributive ones, and the slow
adjustment of expectations about capital discipline. The exact share of the effect that comes from
the signal rather than from the supply structure is debated.

## Prediction

Stocks of companies that announce open-market repurchases earn positive abnormal returns after the
announcement, over several months to several years depending on the study. The drift has been
documented on US stocks and then internationally. Buybacks carried out below intrinsic value add
value for the remaining shareholders, and those carried out above it destroy value. The effect depends
on the type of buyback, the country, the actual execution after the announcement and the profile of
the company.

Some market segments respond recurrently to the announcement or to the execution of repurchase
programmes. In several studies, the abnormal returns after repurchases suggest an underreaction to
the information contained in the potential reduction of supply and in the choice of financial
policy; the natural horizon can run from the event itself to several quarters, and the setting
studied most is the North American stock markets.

## What would refute it

- No abnormal returns after repurchase announcements beyond the announcement reaction itself.
- Buybacks carried out at high valuations, or to manage earnings per share, followed by the same
  returns as buybacks carried out below intrinsic value.
- Repurchasing companies whose earlier bad news proves permanent rather than temporary, with no
  correction of the market's earlier reaction.
- Firms that shrink their float aggressively performing no differently from fundamentally comparable
  firms whose share count does not change; programmes announced but not executed followed by the
  same returns as programmes carried out in full, leaving no role for the reduction of supply.
- Repurchases followed on average by underperformance, as when they mainly smooth earnings per share
  or mask a shortage of growth opportunities.
