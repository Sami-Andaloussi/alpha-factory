## Mechanism

Information diffuses slowly between national markets, and between the industries of different
countries. "Central" or better-informed markets, often the United States, can react first; foreign or
less liquid markets incorporate the implications later. The US market often acts as the leading
market for other countries, not because a rise in the United States mechanically lifts everything,
but because global macroeconomic information, financial conditions and the pricing of world risk are
incorporated there faster; other national markets then absorb part of that information with a delay,
which creates cross-predictability between countries.

The structural mechanism combines the weight of the United States in the world economy and in world
markets, the depth of its information, the role of the dollar, the centrality of its large listed
sectors and its leading role in forming expectations about growth, profits and financial conditions,
with time zones, the hierarchy of global information, regulatory fragmentation, unequal access to
information, the specialisation of investors, the cost of monitoring foreign markets, and the partial
segmentation of capital. Macroeconomic, sector or technological news revealed in a major market can
be informative about firms in other countries that share the same economic exposure without being
priced there at once.

This is the extension of industry information diffusion to the international scale. It is related to
industry lead-lag, to the international diffusion of macroeconomic information and to global swings
between risk-on and risk-off. The theory concerns in particular the relations between transnational
assets, ADRs, exporting firms and global sector clusters.

## Prediction

Lagged US market returns predict the subsequent returns of other national stock markets, the
information propagating from the dominant pricing centre to slower markets, across countries and across
sectors. Several studies show lead-lags between US industries and foreign industries, and between
markets that are open at different hours. The effect tends to be stronger when macroeconomic
uncertainty rises and during downturns, when investors put more weight on aggregate signals and the
constraints on processing information become more visible.

## What would refute it

- Lagged US returns do not predict the returns of other national markets or of their industries.
- Other markets lead the United States as often as the United States leads them, with no relation to
  liquidity, access to information or investor specialisation.
- The lead of US industries over foreign ones is fully explained by time-zone effects, that is by
  non-overlapping trading hours.
- The apparent lead is a reaction to a global factor that was already observed, rather than diffusion
  from one market to another.
- The predictability is no stronger in periods of high macroeconomic uncertainty or in downturns than
  in calm expansions.
