## Mechanism

The theory extends the accrual anomaly: the market errs not only about the average persistence of
accruals, but about the very quality of reported earnings. When a rise in earnings is driven by
discretionary accruals, by beating earnings thresholds, by opportunistic adjustments or by aggressive
accounting policies, future profits are more fragile than the price reflects. Low future returns then
come from the gradual revelation of the poor quality of current earnings. The price can reflect
an accounting figure too literally, without taking in its economic reliability.

## Prediction

Firms whose earnings rest on discretionary accruals or aggressive accounting earn lower subsequent
returns, as cash flows, forecast revisions or earnings disappointments reveal the gap between reported
earnings and real earning power. The natural horizon is often intermediate: several quarters to a few
years. The empirical settings include firms close to earnings thresholds, periods around external
financing, opaque firms, and sectors where accounting judgement weighs heavily.

The effect can overlap with fraud, governance, weak management or the external financing
environment.

## What would refute it

- Earnings driven by discretionary accruals that prove as persistent as other earnings.
- No lower subsequent returns for firms with poor earnings quality, whether near earnings thresholds,
  around external financing or in opaque sectors.
- An effect entirely accounted for by fraud, governance or the financing environment, with no role
  left for the quality of ordinary reported earnings.
