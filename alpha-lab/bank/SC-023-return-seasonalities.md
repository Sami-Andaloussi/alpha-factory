## Mechanism

The theory of return seasonalities, due to Keloharju, Linnainmaa and Nyberg, generalises cross-sectional seasonality beyond the calendar month. It shows seasonalities appearing "almost everywhere": in the same calendar month, but also on the same weekdays, in other asset classes and in international indices. The idea is no longer only that a stock outperforms in a given month, but that it can carry a recurring calendar signature at several frequencies.

This suggests a more general mechanism tied to recurring cycles of demand, of risk, of investor clienteles, of sentiment or of constraints on attention. Seasonalities are not simply a residue of momentum or of other known factors: they are a temporal structure of relative returns in their own right. Authors also show that these seasonalities are often offset by seasonal reversals outside the matching period, which raises the central question of whether they reflect a seasonal risk premium or a transitory mispricing; risk-based, mispricing-based and flow-based readings compete to explain them.

## Prediction

An asset can carry a recurring calendar signature at several frequencies: its relative performance recurs in the same calendar month and on the same weekdays. Outside the matching period, these seasonalities are often offset by seasonal reversals. The pattern appears in individual stocks, in other asset classes and in international indices, and it is not simply a residue of momentum or of other known factors.

Where it has been observed: across stocks, other asset classes and international indices. In crypto markets, a study of 151 cryptocurrencies finds that past returns on the same weekday predict future relative performance in the cross-section, which carries the logic over to a market that trades around the clock.

## What would refute it

- No predictive power of past same-season returns for relative returns in the same season, whether at the monthly or at the weekday frequency.
- Seasonalities fully explained by momentum or by other known factors.
- For the risk-premium reading, seasonal gains that are entirely offset by seasonal reversals elsewhere in the year, leaving no net premium; for the mispricing reading, seasonal returns that match seasonal variations in exposure to risk.
