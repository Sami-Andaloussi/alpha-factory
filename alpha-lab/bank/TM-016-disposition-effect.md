## Mechanism

The disposition effect is the tendency to sell winners too early, to "take the profit", and to hold
losers too long, to avoid realising a loss (Shefrin and Statman, 1985). It is consistent with
prospect theory and mental accounting, and close to loss aversion. For momentum the bias matters
because it changes the supply and demand pressure around information. Investors hesitate to
acknowledge a mistake on their losers and do not rebalance fully rationally; conversely they cash in
their winners too quickly, though not enough to offset fully the continued revaluation when good news
keeps diffusing. Losers stay held and the selling pressure on them accumulates late; adjustments take
place slowly around reference points.

Grinblatt and Han model a market where rational investors coexist with "disposition investors". In
equilibrium the bias creates a stable spread between the market price and the fundamental value.
Even when the fundamental value follows a random walk, the market price incorporates news slowly,
because part of the holders remain anchored on their purchase price and on their mental frame of
gains and losses. On stocks where most holders have large unrealised gains, the price shows excess
volatility and underreacts to negative announcements, adjusting slowly downward. The gradual
convergence of the spread between fundamental value and equilibrium price, driven by the gradual
updating of the holders' reference points and by the random evolution of fundamentals, produces a
predictability that looks like momentum: stocks with a large accumulated unrealised gain keep
outperforming stocks with accumulated losses. Frazzini links the same bias to the underreaction of
prices to news, and the effect is tied explicitly to the post-earnings-announcement drift and to
stock price momentum.

The behavioural pressure is aggregated in the market and does not disappear at once. The effect
persists because it rests on deep-rooted behaviour, and because it is hard to arbitrage without
knowing the actual distribution of the holders' cost bases; arbitrage is capital-intensive and
exposed to timing risk. The bias is carried mainly by individual, non-institutional investors. It is
documented mostly in equities, where the investors' entry price is salient and where year-end
taxation modulates the behaviour; its scope is any listed asset held by investors blinded by their
gains, retail crypto included.

## Prediction

Stocks with a large capital gains overhang, those on which most holders have unrealised gains,
outperform over the following weeks and months stocks on which holders have unrealised losses, even
when fundamentals follow a random walk; after strong rises, prices keep rising, and these stocks
keep outperforming as long as the holders of gains remain passive sellers, which pushes back the
correction. Grinblatt and Han find that the aggregate unrealised gain captures most of the momentum
effect: once it is controlled for, the classic momentum effect weakens or disappears. The effect
plays out over short to medium horizons, around earnings announcements.

## What would refute it

Investors who realise gains and losses at the same rate. Stocks with a large capital gains overhang
earning no more than stocks with large unrealised losses once past returns are controlled for.
Classic momentum that survives intact once the overhang is controlled for. Prices that adjust fully
to news regardless of whether holders sit on gains or losses. No year-end modulation of selling by
investors holding losses.
