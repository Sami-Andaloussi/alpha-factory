## Mechanism

Value and momentum premia exist across many asset classes, which suggests that the mechanisms
behind them (underreaction, overreaction, funding constraints, risk aversion and segmentation)
belong not to one asset class but to the way investors trade relative relations. The central finding
is not only that value and momentum appear almost everywhere, but that they share a common structure
across markets: individual stocks, country equity indices, interest rates, currencies and
commodities. This suggests drivers of behaviour and risk that cut across markets, and a possible
propagation of regimes from one asset class to another.

Momentum in particular is not specific to US equities: it is documented in almost every asset class
and in every developed market. The momentum returns of different asset classes are positively and
significantly correlated with each other, which suggests common risk factors or common behavioural
mechanisms behind them. This correlation across asset classes also creates a crowding risk: in a
drawdown of global momentum, all momentum strategies suffer at the same time. Japan is the main
exception: momentum in Japanese equities is generally weak or insignificant, an absence often
attributed to cross-shareholdings between firms (keiretsu), to the large presence of retail traders
and to the specific macroeconomic conditions of Japan.

The theory is a framework rather than a specific channel. It does not replace finer mechanisms such
as production chains, the business cycle, carry, the diffusion of information or cointegration, but
it treats relative-value effects as objects that span several asset classes, with their own
covariances. Their persistence comes from funding risk, episodes of crowding, heterogeneous
arbitrage horizons and the difficulty of bearing long divergences.

## Prediction

Within each asset class (individual stocks, country equity indices, interest rates, currencies and
commodities), cheap assets outperform expensive ones and recent winners outperform recent losers, and
the returns of these value and momentum strategies share a common structure across asset classes,
suggesting common drivers of behaviour and risk. Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen (2013) document momentum
simultaneously in equities (United States, United Kingdom, continental Europe, Japan), commodity
futures, currency forwards and government bond futures, with positive and significant correlations
between the momentum returns of the different asset classes. Rouwenhorst (1998) found momentum in 12
European markets with returns comparable to those of the US market, 1.16% a month against 1.31%. All
momentum strategies lose together in global momentum drawdowns, as in the great reversal of 2009.
Momentum in Japanese equities is weak or insignificant.

## What would refute it

- Value and momentum premia are absent from some of these asset classes, or momentum is absent from
  most asset classes and developed markets outside US equities.
- The value and momentum returns of different asset classes show no common structure and are
  unrelated across markets; momentum returns uncorrelated across asset classes.
- Momentum strategies in different asset classes that do not lose together in global momentum
  drawdowns.
- The premia are specific to the investor base, the rules and the constraints of one market, and
  disappear where these differ.
- Momentum in Japanese equities as strong as elsewhere.
