## Mechanism

Reputational herding explains imitation among investment managers by career incentives (Scharfstein
and Stein, 1990). A manager may prefer to copy the decisions of other managers rather than act on
private information, because failing while being "different" damages a reputation more than failing
"with the group", which is more easily excused.

This individually rational behaviour produces correlated flows and a persistent demand in the same
direction, herding, which can turn into price trends at intermediate horizons if managers adjust
their positions with inertia. The persistence comes from the structure of incentives: herding is
rational for each manager given how managers are judged, so it is not a simple irrationality that
disappears as individuals learn.

## Prediction

Managers subject to career concerns trade in the same direction as their peers, their correlated
demand persisting over successive periods, and where positions are adjusted with inertia, the
securities they herd into can trend in the direction of that demand over intermediate horizons.

## What would refute it

Managers whose trades are no more correlated with those of their peers than their private information
would imply, including managers with strong career concerns. Herding that disappears as managers gain
experience, when the incentive structure has not changed. Correlated institutional demand that is not
followed by price trends in the securities concerned, even where positions are adjusted with inertia.
