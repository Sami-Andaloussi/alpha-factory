## Mechanism

Many studies have found lead-lag effects between asset classes. A classic example is the delayed
reallocation between equities and bonds. The general mechanism is that some markets, equities for
example, have better-informed investors who absorb macroeconomic information first, while others, such
as bonds and currencies, process the information more slowly, possibly because of regulatory
constraints, the complexity of the products, or "buy-and-hold" institutions that are slow to react. The
lag is tied to a gap in efficiency between markets, for instance the greater liquidity and transparency
of the equity market compared with corporate bonds.

These effects are conceptually close to traditional momentum over time, but they extend across assets:
the past returns of one asset class inform the future returns of another.

## Prediction

Strong stock-market performance over one year often predicts strong corporate bond performance the next
year, which is attributed to a delayed reaction of the bond market (Agarwal et al., 2019). Lags have
also been found between currencies and bonds, and between the equity indices of different countries.
Studies of cross-asset momentum show that these dynamic spillovers predict the returns of the lagging
assets. The lag is persistent. The spillovers play out over one to twelve months.
They can change as market structure evolves (rising rates, for example, stabilised bonds) and as
international markets become more connected.

## What would refute it

- Past equity returns do not predict later corporate bond returns, and lagged returns of currencies,
  bonds or foreign equity indices do not predict the returns of the other markets.
- Bonds and currencies react to macroeconomic information as fast as equities.
- The lag bears no relation to the gap in liquidity and transparency between the leading and the lagging
  market.
- The lag does not persist: spillovers found in one period are absent in the next, as with a statistical
  coincidence.
