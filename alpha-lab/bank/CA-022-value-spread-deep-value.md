## Mechanism

The theory holds that what matters is not only how cheap an asset or a factor is in absolute terms,
but the valuation gap between the "cheap" leg and the "expensive" leg of a relative relation. When
that gap becomes exceptionally wide, the expected relative return rises, because a growing share of
the divergence reflects very high risk premia, constraints on arbitrage, asymmetric flows or errors
of extrapolation.

Economically, the "value of value" varies over time. Gaps widen particularly when arbitrageurs are
constrained, when growth narratives attract the flows, or when investors extrapolate recent winners
too far. The persistence is consistent with limits to arbitrage: financing the cheap leg through a
further phase of widening can be very costly in tracking error, in patience and in capital. The
relation applies to sectors, countries, factors and asset classes, and at times to groups of
crypto-assets.

## Prediction

When the valuation spread between the cheap and the expensive legs of a relation becomes
exceptionally wide, the subsequent return of the cheap leg relative to the expensive one rises. The
effect is clearest at medium to long horizons and in deep-value episodes.

## What would refute it

- The valuation spread between the cheap and expensive legs does not predict their subsequent
  relative return, including at medium and long horizons and after exceptionally wide spreads.
- Exceptionally wide spreads are followed by further widening as often as by convergence, the
  divergence reflecting neither very high risk premia, nor constraints on arbitrage, nor asymmetric
  flows, nor errors of extrapolation.
