## Mechanism

The procyclicality of leverage explains a directional amplification (Adrian and Shin, 2014). When
measured volatility falls, constraints of the value-at-risk type allow more leverage and therefore
more risk; when volatility rises, often after an adverse move, institutions must shrink their balance
sheets and exposures, which can mean selling into a fall, or buying into a rise, and so reinforces the
direction of the move.

Trend and momentum can then be seen as an emergent property of risk-management policies that are
widely shared, such as volatility targets and value-at-risk limits, and of their synchronisation
across the financial system. The persistence comes from the fact that these constraints are hard,
written into contracts, risk committees, regulation and financing, and are applied with some delay,
which creates inertia in deleveraging and releveraging.

## Prediction

Intermediaries' leverage rises when volatility is low and falls when volatility rises, and their
deleveraging after adverse moves can reinforce the direction of prices, with an inertia that comes
from constraints applied with some delay.

## What would refute it

Leverage of intermediaries that does not vary with measured volatility, or that rises when
volatility rises. Deleveraging after adverse moves that leaves no trace in subsequent price moves.
Changes in value-at-risk or volatility-target constraints that do not affect institutional exposures.
