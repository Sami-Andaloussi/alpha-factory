## Mechanism

Information about a firm is transmitted economically to its customers and suppliers, with a delay.
When an important firm receives good or bad news, the fundamental value of its trading partners can
change, but the market does not immediately revalue the whole economic chain: when a major customer
surprises positively, part of that news should mechanically raise the fundamental value of its
suppliers; conversely, weakness at the customer destroys future demand for the firms upstream. The
relationship itself then carries a relative-value effect that rests neither on pure statistical
similarity nor on mere correlation, but on a concrete causal channel.

Customer-supplier relations involve revenue dependence, visibility of the order book, pressure on
margins, bargaining power, concentration risk, working-capital needs and sometimes the spread of
financial constraints. News about a large customer can carry information about the supplier's future
sales; news about a critical supplier can bear on the customer's production capacity.

The core of the mechanism is limited attention. Investors process direct news about a company
efficiently, but its second-round implications for linked firms much less well, because these
require mapping economic networks and assessing the elasticity of each link. The neglect is favoured by
the facts that the map of commercial links, though public, is costly to track systematically, that the
exact exposures change over time, and that fund managers are often organised in coverage silos. This
lets the predictability persist even though the information is public.

## Prediction

The literature documents that returns cross-predict between customers and suppliers linked by declared
commercial relationships: news reflected in one firm's price is later reflected in the prices of its
trading partners. The returns of a firm's major customers, and news about them, predict the firm's
subsequent returns in the same direction: good news at the customer is followed by gains at its
suppliers, bad news by losses. The natural horizon is broadly daily to monthly, with a persistence that
fits gradual diffusion rather than a simple microstructure bounce. The links vary in intensity and the
transmissions are non-linear. The effect is documented first on US stocks. The logic rests on cash flows
linked through networks of production, distribution or technological dependence.

## What would refute it

- Returns of linked firms no longer predict each other once common sector or macroeconomic shocks are
  accounted for: part of the relation can be a shared shock rather than a transmission along the
  link. Or the cross-predictability is no stronger between economically linked firms than between
  firms that are merely correlated.
- The lagged returns of major customers, and news about them, do not predict the returns of their
  suppliers; news about a firm is reflected in its customers' and suppliers' prices on the same day
  it is reflected in its own.
- The predictability reverses, as a microstructure bounce would, instead of persisting.
- The predictability is as strong for links that sit within one manager's coverage as for links that
  cross coverage silos.
