## Mechanism

Economic information that matters for an industry is not incorporated into all the stocks of the
sector at once. Some firms, often the most visible, the most liquid, the largest or the most
"representative" of the sector, the most followed and those at the centre of the information chain,
react first; the others, small, neglected firms and those in less competitive industries, follow with
a delay, which creates a lead-lag within the industry: news travels sequentially between the firms or
segments of one industry. The same mechanism is also described as industry momentum; the emphasis
here, however, is on the speed at which information is absorbed, an explicitly directional
propagation of information from leaders to followers within the same economic complex, not only on
the persistence or comovement of the industry's return as a whole.

A large share of financial information is sectoral: final demand, input prices, regulation, the
investment cycle, innovation, supply-chain risks, and revisions of comparable multiples. A profit
warning, a cost shock, a demand surprise or a macroeconomic indicator concentrated on one sector
affects the whole industry, but investors do not revalue every name at once. Investors learn first
through the firms that serve as sector "bellwethers"; an announcement or a price move by a sector
leader is then read as a signal for its peers. A sector shock is known first to the large firms that
many analysts follow, and then spreads gradually to the small firms with less coverage. The diffusion
is slow because of limited attention and the specialisation of analysts, and because a common shock
has to be "translated" stock by stock: inference from one firm to another requires information
processing and knowledge of the business, and sometimes time to tell an idiosyncratic shock from a
sector shock. Several economic layers can produce the delay: similar cash flows, the sector reading of
investors, the propagation of analyst revisions, and factor rebalancing and benchmark-driven flows
that hit the central names of the sector first. The mechanism is close to customer-supplier links,
to the lead of large stocks over small stocks, and to production networks.

Limited media and analyst coverage and investor inattention to less-followed stocks prevent immediate
arbitrage. The gap is not fully arbitraged away because translating a leader's move into its
fundamental impact on a peer is noisy, since not all firms in an industry have the same exposure, and
because arbitrage across stocks requires capital, diagnosis and execution on several names.

## Prediction

Past returns of industry leaders, the large, closely followed and central firms, predict the future
returns of the small and neglected firms in the same sector, in the same direction: once a leader has
revealed the information content of a shock, the lagging assets of the same economic complex move
towards it. The pattern has been observed at daily, weekly and monthly horizons, over several days to
several months, mainly in equities. It is strongest when sector information is complex or hard to
aggregate, when the followers are little followed, and in industries with thin analyst coverage and
weaker competition.

Small firms in an industry react more slowly to news coming from the large firms of the same industry
(Hou, 2007). This underreaction, or slow information diffusion, creates the intra-industry lead-lag and
a post-announcement drift in small firms after announcements by large ones. In that study the effect is
driven mostly by underreaction to bad news, and it is stronger in small, less competitive or neglected
industries. Large and small capitalisations of the same sector are positively correlated, and
cointegration models indicate that sector information released by large firms is incorporated later by
small ones. The effect is documented on US stocks (over 1992-2000), with a natural horizon of a few days
to a week of post-announcement persistence, and it disappears as analyst coverage increases. At high
frequency, the books also recall that lead-lags may exist between assets of the same sector, alongside
microstructure effects.

## What would refute it

- Within industries, the lagged returns of large and closely followed firms do not predict those of
  small and neglected firms; or their predictive power disappears once common exposure to sector
  factors is controlled for: part of the signal can reflect a shared factor rather than slow
  diffusion.
- The apparent lead of the leaders over the followers reflects only the persistence of the industry's
  return as a whole, with no sequential propagation from firm to firm inside the industry.
- The lag of followers is no longer when they have less analyst or media coverage, when sector
  information is more complex or harder to aggregate, or when competition is weak, and it does not
  shrink as their analyst coverage increases.
- Followers react as fast to bad news from the leaders as to good news, contrary to the asymmetry
  reported by Hou (2007).
- At high frequency, the lead-lag between stocks of the same sector is entirely accounted for by
  microstructure effects.
