## Mechanism

Investors do not simply extrapolate mechanically: they overreact especially to news they judge "representative". A run of good news makes a company look more and more like a "type" of high-growth firm; investors revise their expectations too aggressively, then correct them as growth returns to normal levels. The price follows the same path: a surge, then a cooling.

The central point is that fundamentals do mean-revert, but the diagnostic error amplifies the movement. Expectations of long-term growth form too high after positive news and too low after negative news. As analysts' forecasts and realised outcomes return to normal, valuations normalise too. The theory links value, long-term reversals and the dynamics of expectations: an asset can become expensive or cheap relative to its peers without any immediate liquidity shock, because the structure of beliefs itself departs from a sustainable regime.

The effect persists because representative narratives are powerful, socially shared, and reinforced by the media, analysts and delegation. Even sophisticated investors can be slow to fight a dominant story when the career cost of being contrarian is high.

## Prediction

Firms whose recent news has made analysts' long-term growth expectations most optimistic subsequently earn lower returns, as growth and forecasts revert towards normal levels; firms with the most pessimistic expectations earn higher returns.

## What would refute it

- Revisions of long-term growth expectations that do not overshoot: forecast errors unrelated to the direction of past news.
- Stocks with the most optimistic growth expectations that do not underperform once expectations and outcomes normalise.
- Realised growth that does not revert towards normal levels after runs of good or bad news.
