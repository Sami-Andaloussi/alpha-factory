## Mechanism

Investor flows into funds respond convexly to past performance. Funds that have done well attract
far more capital than median funds lose, and poorly performing funds do not necessarily suffer
symmetric, immediate outflows. Capital goes to the funds and sectors that have recently performed
well and leaves the funds that disappoint. The literature and the observations reported in Chains of
Finance show that retail investors are seen as "flighty", that they buy past performance, and that a
"winner takes all" pattern prevails, in which a small handful of funds captures most of the net
subscriptions.

The relation has market consequences because it forces the winning managers to buy more of the
assets that have already produced their performance, which can prolong some trends. Past performance
attracts subscriptions; subscriptions force purchases; the purchases can further support the
securities already held by the winning portfolios. Losing funds, for their part, must sell. These
purchases and sales happen not because managers change their minds but because subscribers move
their capital. The chain links the relative performance of managers to the future demand for
securities: some flows thus persist, and delegated management can amplify investment styles. The chain is
often combined with flow-driven momentum and institutional herding.

The relation persists because end investors allocate capital on simple signals of performance, even
though this behaviour is sometimes costly for them. Asset gathering depends on observed performance,
rankings, platforms and intermediaries, and end clients often misunderstand what the manager
actually does. Arbitrageurs cannot easily neutralise a process fed by recurring new subscriptions.
Because outflows can come at any time, managers must keep liquid assets and avoid positions that
would take too long to unwind. This narrows the universe in which they really invest and accentuates
crowding, especially when many funds own the same liquid names. This is not a pure microstructure
effect but a deep and recurring source of buying and selling pressure running through the asset
management industry.

## Prediction

Funds with high recent returns receive disproportionately large inflows, while poorly performing
funds do not suffer symmetric, immediate outflows, and a few funds capture most net subscriptions.
The securities held by winning funds receive additional buying, which can support them further and
prolong some trends, while the holdings of losing funds face forced selling. The natural horizon is
weeks to months, with accelerations during drawdowns and changes in sector leadership. The mechanism
operates in equities; in crypto it runs through managed vehicles, platforms, and rotations between
assets or themes.

## What would refute it

- A flow-performance relation that is linear or symmetric, with winners gaining no more than losers
  lose.
- Net subscriptions spread evenly across funds rather than concentrated on the recent top
  performers.
- No additional price pressure on the securities held by funds with large inflows, and no
  prolongation of the trends in those securities.
