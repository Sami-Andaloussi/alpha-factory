## Mechanism

Geographic proximity favours the local diffusion of information and creates relations of precedence
between firms. Firms located close to one another can share the same demand base, labour markets, banks,
local investors and suppliers, or the same regional regulatory constraints, so information revealed by
one can be informative about the other.

The mechanism is not reduced to physical distance. It rests on spatial frictions in information: local
attention, social and professional networks, regional investors and analysts, local knowledge of
economic conditions, industrial clustering, and learning from geographically correlated shocks.
Information that appears first in a "local leader" can take time to be extrapolated to the other firms
exposed to the same geography. Geography thus acts as a latent graph of information diffusion, even in a
digital world.

Parsons, Sabbatucci and Titman (2020) document that the lead-lag can also run between firms that share a
headquarters city or region but belong to different industries. It is tied to the way financial analysts
are organised: by sector rather than by geographic area. Two firms located in the same city (Seattle,
for example) can share analysts and local information flows even when they are in different industries,
and one of them reacts earlier than the other to common news. This differs from the classic industry
lead-lag, which is strong among small firms: here the lag is not associated with the number of analysts
or with liquidity, but with the overlap of analysts between the two firms. Local, "spatial" information
thus diffuses differently from sector information. The effect assumes the absence of certain
arbitrageurs, those able to follow both the geography and the sector.

## Prediction

Studies show lead-lag effects between co-located or geographically linked firms: the returns of a firm
lead those of other firms located in the same area, including firms in other industries, typically over
short to intermediate horizons. The results of Parsons, Sabbatucci and Titman (2020) show an excess
return of 5-6% a year from these relations between firms sharing a headquarters location. The effect has
been observed mainly on contemporary US stocks; it is weaker than the industry lead-lag but
statistically significant even for large capitalisations. It disappears for firms that many analysts
cover in common, whose reactions are more simultaneous, and it is weaker when the firms have no analyst
in common.

## What would refute it

- The lead-lag between co-located firms disappears once industry, supply-chain, local-finance and local
  political links are accounted for: geography aggregates several channels.
- Co-located firms in different industries show no lead-lag, or one that is fully explained by the
  number of analysts or by liquidity rather than by the overlap of analysts.
- The lead-lag persists between co-located firms that many analysts cover in common.
- Information revealed by a local leader reaches the other firms exposed to the same geography at the
  same time as the leader's own price.
