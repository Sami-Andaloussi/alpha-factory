## Mechanism

A shock to a commodity, above all to energy, does not hit all stocks in the same way. It creates
relative winners and losers according to the exposure to input costs, terms of trade, revenues from
production, global demand and the investment cycle. The effect thus concerns equity sectors, through
their economic relation to the commodity market.

The literature has shown that the effect depends on the type of shock: a rise in the oil price driven by
strong global demand does not carry the same implications as an adverse supply shock or a demand shock
specific to oil. Energy, materials, transport, consumer discretionary and industrial stocks, and some
national markets, do not absorb this news at the same speed or in the same direction. The relation is
therefore structural but conditional. The theory relates to production networks, to sector rotation and
to the hierarchy of sensitivities to macroeconomic shocks. By analogy, in spot equities and
crypto-assets, any asset upstream or downstream of a shock to a real or digital asset can be thought of
in the same way.

## Prediction

After a commodity price shock, above all an oil price shock, sectors and national markets diverge
according to their exposure to input costs, terms of trade, revenues from production, global demand
and the investment cycle. The implications differ according to whether the shock comes from strong
global demand, from adverse supply or from demand specific to oil, and sectors and national markets
do not absorb the news at the same speed or in the same direction.

## What would refute it

- Commodity price shocks move all equity sectors alike, with no difference between the sectors that
  produce the commodity and those that use it.
- The sector response is the same whatever the origin of the shock: global demand, supply, or demand
  specific to oil.
- Sectors and national markets absorb the shock immediately and at the same speed, leaving no lagged
  relative move.
