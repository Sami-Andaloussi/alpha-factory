## Mechanism

The pre-holiday effect is the abnormally high return of equities just before a holiday on which the market is closed, often on the session or half-session immediately preceding it, sometimes with a more complex pattern on the adjacent days. Measured as an average daily return, it has historically been one of the most spectacular calendar effects: in some US samples, a highly disproportionate share of the annual return was concentrated on a few holiday eves.

The proposed mechanisms are mainly behavioural and microstructural, and the explanations the books put forward are mainly behavioural and institutional. Before the holiday, mood is more favourable and optimism rises temporarily; risk aversion falls and risk-taking rises ahead of a period of rest. Institutional trading desks are less active. Short sellers close their positions before the break, the supply of shares for sale falls relative to demand, residual demand and the supply of liquidity are out of balance, and there can simply be buying pressure at the end of the session before the prolonged closure. Individual investors' orders build up, sessions are short, and market makers adjust their books and inventories before the closure. Attention is asymmetric. A common global factor comes into play when several markets share holidays or similar closing rhythms. A purely mechanical explanation based on the close is generally insufficient, because the positive returns often build up over a wider part of the day. The effect thus links the calendar, mood, liquidity and the segmentation of market participants. It is close to the Santa Claus rally, but shorter, more mechanical, and anchored on the eves of official closures.

Its persistence probably comes from its link to real interruptions of trading and to recurrent portfolio behaviour. The relative persistence of the effect despite its notoriety suggests that microstructure frictions, the short duration of the effect and the intermittent nature of the calendar limit its full arbitrage.

## Prediction

Returns on the trading session, or half-session, before a market-closing holiday are abnormally high compared with ordinary days. The authors also show that the effect can shift in time, for example to the third day before the holiday rather than the day before.

Where and when it has been observed: the books document it over a very long history, as very international and wider than a US artefact, though it is not uniformly international: it is observed mainly in equities, in markets that really close for national holidays, and it seems to depend on local practices; it has also been studied in the Italian stock market. It has been documented in cash equities and in S&P 500 and Russell 2000 index futures. Its strength varies by period and market. The literature indicates that it has declined or shifted, weakening in some recent periods, and that in some recent samples it has become more of a small-cap and microcap effect, which is consistent with the idea that a well-known anomaly is arbitraged first in the liquid segments.

## What would refute it

- Returns on pre-holiday sessions no higher than on ordinary trading days.
- An effect as strong before holidays on which the exchange stays open as before real closures.
- Excess returns that come only from the closing print, with nothing earlier in the session, which would reduce the effect to a mechanical closing artefact.
- After the effect became widely known, an excess return that persists as strongly in large, liquid capitalisations as in small ones.
- No short covering, no build-up of individual orders and no adjustment of market makers' inventories before closures.
