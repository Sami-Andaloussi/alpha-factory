## Mechanism

Industry momentum is the continuation of performance at the level of industries or sectors rather
than of individual securities. It sits between pure macro trends and stock-by-stock momentum. The
industry can be the relevant economic unit of momentum when information, flows and investors'
perceptions move by theme rather than security by security. Economic shocks and demand cycles are
sectoral: economically relevant news is not incorporated first at the purely idiosyncratic level of
each stock, but at the level of industry groups whose members share the same shocks to demand, costs,
regulation, capacity and investment. When a sector benefits from a structural trend or a shock, such
as a rise in the oil price for energy, the adoption of a technology for the technology sector, a rise
in final demand, improving margins, a revision of capacity, easing input costs or a regulatory boost,
all its members benefit from it simultaneously, and the underreaction to these sector shocks shows up
as industry momentum.

The causal mechanism put forward often centres on the diffusion of information along economic chains:
peers, supply chains and comparable firms. A common shock hits the leaders of a sector first, then
spreads to the other members of the industry: investors often extrapolate first from the most visible
leaders, and the other names adjust only later, so information about one firm or activity is
incorporated only gradually into the valuation of the whole sector, which creates delays,
heterogeneous across its securities, and therefore persistence. The effect rests less on a
continuation specific to each stock than on an economic relation between distinct instruments that
belong to the same block of cash flows, a bridge between sector rotation and structural comovement. A
second reading, which does not exclude the first, runs through flows and benchmarking: managers'
sector allocations, their rotations and delegated management can create an inertia of demand at the
sector level, and institutional reallocations are often made by sector pockets. The persistence comes
from the segmentation of the sell-side, the slow diffusion between comparable firms, sector
benchmarks and the constraints on managers' rotations. Full arbitrage is prevented by transaction
costs, capital constraints, the specialised mandates of fund managers, attention frictions,
heterogeneity among investors, and the difficulty of telling a temporary shock from a genuine change
in the fundamental regime.

A part of individual stock momentum is therefore a structural economic effect (sector rotation,
supply and demand shocks, the progressive revaluation of an industry theme, reallocations by
sector-benchmarked managers) rather than a firm-specific one, which separates an idiosyncratic
momentum from a thematic, sectoral one. Not all price continuation is sectoral, but a non-trivial
block of momentum comes from the slowness with which information common to an industry is
incorporated and arbitraged. The theory is related to the gradual diffusion of information, to
lead-lag between industries and to sector rotation. By analogy, for equity index futures the theory
suggests that directional persistence can be read not only at the level of the broad indices but at
the level of economic sub-blocks (energy, materials, financials, growth, cyclicals, defensives), and
by sector or region; for commodity futures, groups of commodities (by product class, complex or
sub-sector: energy, metals, grains or agricultural products) take the place of industries, and
momentum between groups is analogous to industry momentum in stocks. Assets that share the same
fundamental exposure can go through a temporary hierarchy between leaders and laggards.

## Prediction

Industries that have performed best over the past 6 to 12 months keep outperforming over the
following 6 to 12 months, a relative persistence of performance at the level of the industry or
sector; within an industry hit by a shock, the less visible names follow the move of its visible
leaders with a delay. This industry continuation is partly, but not entirely, explained by the
momentum of the individual stocks that compose the industries. Conversely, it would explain a
substantial part of individual stock momentum. Moskowitz and Grinblatt (1999) find that industry
momentum accounts for between 0.47% and 0.78% of monthly return in momentum strategies on stocks,
and suggest that individual stock momentum is partly "industrial": winning stocks often belong to
winning industries. The books stress that industry momentum can survive even when some
decompositions by market factor fail to explain overall momentum. It has been documented mainly on
US stocks, at intermediate horizons of about 3 to 12 months.

## What would refute it

- Past winning industries over 3 to 12 months not outperforming past losing industries over the
  following months.
- Stock-level momentum that is unchanged once industry momentum is removed, meaning that winners are
  no more likely to belong to winning industries; or a persistence of relative performance entirely
  explained by the continuation of each stock's own returns, with nothing left at the level of the
  industry.
- No delay in the response of a sector's less visible members to shocks that have already moved its
  most visible leaders: all members reprice at the same time.
- Sector momentum that appears only as a short-lived artefact.
- A persistence as strong where trading costs, capital constraints and attention frictions are low as
  where they are high.
