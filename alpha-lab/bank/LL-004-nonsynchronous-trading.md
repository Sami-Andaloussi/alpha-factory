## Mechanism

At least part of the lead-lag observed between assets comes from a lag in observation or execution
rather than from information spreading between them. If two assets are sampled at the same calendar time
while their last actual trades did not take place at the same moment, the more active asset will appear
to "predict" the other; the books show that this generates spurious autocorrelation and
cross-autocorrelation. Assets and venues do not update their prices at the same instant: last trades are
not synchronous, some quotes are stale, and market clocks are not perfectly aligned. Lo and MacKinlay
show that part of the cross-sectional lead-lag can be generated by nontrading and nonsynchronous
sampling.

Between large and small capitalisations the effect arises naturally: the large stock reflects the news
because it is trading now, while the small stock still shows an old price. What looks like information
travelling between assets is therefore not always that: sometimes it is just the delayed display of the
same information, already known to the market. Hasbrouck shows that a secondary price can simply be a
midpoint delayed by transmission and processing lags. The cause is therefore not a pure informational
advantage but a friction of trading and measurement: a thinly traded stock or a slow venue incorporates
information after another.

The effect persists in data because of illiquidity, imperfect timestamps, closed markets, sampling
frequencies that are too coarse, uneven trading activity, fragmented markets, reporting latencies, and
time aggregation, which degrades causal identification. Within a measured lead-lag, one part can be
genuine diffusion and another part a microstructure artefact.

## Prediction

Returns sampled in calendar time show cross-autocorrelation in which the frequently traded asset leads
the rarely traded one, and spurious autocorrelation, even where no information passes between them. It
largely disappears once the data are correctly synchronised. It does not account for the whole lead-lag
between large and small capitalisations: nontrading explains only part of it (Lo and MacKinlay).

## What would refute it

- The apparent lead-lag between active and inactive assets survives intact once prices are sampled
  synchronously. This is observed for part of the lead of large over small stocks, which nontrading does
  not explain.
- The cross-autocorrelation does not depend on differences in trading frequency or on the precision and
  alignment of timestamps.
- A lagging price keeps adjusting after its own next trades instead of catching up when it trades, which
  would indicate slow diffusion rather than a stale price.
