## Mechanism

Some flows are generated not by clients directly but by the internal rules of vehicles with a
target allocation. Balanced funds, pension funds, target-date funds and multi-asset strategies
rebalance their weights to return to a set allocation. After equities rise, they must sell equities
to return to the target; after equities fall, they must buy them, or the reverse depending on their
specific rules. These orders can be mechanical, tied to the calendar, and aggregated on a large
scale.

The mechanism is structural and largely insensitive to short-term fundamental information: the flow
is created by mandates, management charters, risk constraints and operational processes. It explains
why some month-ends, quarter-ends and periods of wide dispersion in performance can come with
predictable buying or selling pressure. A growing share of savings is managed by such standardised
vehicles.

It persists because the aim of these vehicles is not to maximise the value of each trade but to keep
a risk, age or allocation profile. Arbitrageurs can anticipate the flows, but their ability to
neutralise them depends on the size of the rebalancing, on how predictable the exact rules are, and
on the cost of carrying a position until the flows are executed.

## Prediction

After equities rise, these vehicles sell equities to return to their target allocation, and after
equities fall they buy them, or the reverse depending on their specific rules. Some month-ends,
quarter-ends and periods of wide dispersion in performance can therefore come with predictable
buying or selling pressure.

## What would refute it

- No predictable buying or selling pressure around month-ends and quarter-ends linked to the
  preceding moves of equities.
- Rebalancing pressure unrelated to the size of the assets held by balanced, pension and
  target-date funds.
- Rebalancing flows absorbed without any price effect even when the required trades are large.
