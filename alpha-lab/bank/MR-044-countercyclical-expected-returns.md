## Mechanism

Expected excess returns on stocks and bonds vary countercyclically with economic activity: they are high in recessions, when risk premia are high, and low in expansions, when premia are compressed (Fama and French 1989). This variation is a form of reversion: after a period of low returns (an expansion), expected future returns are higher, and the reverse after a period of high returns. Ilmanen (2011) presents the mechanism through the decomposition of expected returns into "building blocks", modulated by the business cycle.

The same countercyclical variation of the risk premium appears, to some degree, in equity index, interest-rate and commodity futures. Expected returns can be tracked with macroeconomic and valuation variables: the earnings yield, the dividend yield, the term premium and backwardation.

Valuation ratios (the dividend yield, the price-to-earnings ratio, the price-to-book ratio and the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio, CAPE) predict returns over several years: periods of low valuations, when markets are "cheap", tend to be followed by persistently high returns, and periods of high valuations by low returns. Shiller (1981) shows that stock prices are too volatile relative to dividends for returns to be unpredictable, which implies that returns must be predictable. This persistence of the regime of returns shares properties with trends without being momentum in the strict sense: it is closer to long-term mean reversion, but it generates slow trends at macroeconomic horizons, since a market whose valuations rise gradually over several years is in a slow uptrend fed by the compression of risk premia. Ilmanen (2011) describes this slow-moving variation of expected returns as interacting with momentum strategies at different horizons.

## Prediction

Expected excess returns are higher in recessions and lower in expansions, and they can be tracked with macroeconomic and valuation variables: the earnings yield, the dividend yield, the term premium and backwardation. The dividend yield predicts future returns over horizons of 2 to 5 years (Fama and French 1988; Campbell and Shiller 1988): periods of low valuations tend to be followed by persistently high returns, and the reverse. The horizon is macroeconomic, from quarters to years; the variation does not play out at high frequency.

## What would refute it

- Expected excess returns that are not higher in recessions, or not higher after periods of low returns.
- Dividend yields, earnings yields, the term premium and backwardation that do not predict later returns.
- Risk premia that move procyclically, rising in expansions and falling in recessions.
- Stock price volatility consistent with the volatility of dividends under unpredictable returns.
- Gradual rises in valuations over several years that are not accompanied by falling expected returns.
