## Mechanism

Window dressing is the adjustment of a portfolio by its manager just before a reporting date, mostly
the end of a month, a quarter or the year, so that the list of positions shown to clients,
consultants, committees, boards or regulators looks more acceptable. Managers may sell embarrassing
or visible losers, buy visible winners, or cut positions whose mere presence in the portfolio would
be read negatively, not because the fundamental view has changed but because clients will judge the
manager's stock-picking from the published holdings; at the end of the financial year they also seek
to show positions that look more liquid, more defensive or more presentable. Leaning for the tape,
also called portfolio pumping, is a related, more aggressive and more microstructural practice:
rather than swapping stocks to dress up the snapshot of the portfolio, the manager pushes up the
price of securities the fund already holds, often through last-minute purchases just before the
performance cut-off, to lift the published net asset value or the closing performance, even
temporarily. In both cases an identifiable participant buys or sells not to express a conviction
about value, but to shape the perception of its performance or its process. The flow is induced by
the governance of delegated management, not by fundamental information. Window dressing can work
through a gradual cosmetic reallocation over several days or weeks; pumping works through the last
prices of the period.

It is a classic agency friction. Future asset gathering and career risk depend on the image of
competence projected by the disclosed portfolio, not only on future returns. Clients, consultants
and platforms mostly see recent performance and snapshots of the holdings, not how the positions
actually came about; when the two signals conflict, the manager has an incentive to manipulate the
second. For pumping the agency logic is even sharper: fund rankings, bonuses, future inflows and
reputation depend on figures measured exactly at the cut-off date, and the convexity of flow and
compensation incentives combines with illiquid holdings in which an order at the end of the trading
day or of the quarter can move the price. Pumping is most likely in small capitalisations, in
concentrated portfolios, among poorly ranked managers and among "star" funds that have much to gain
or lose from a single rank.

Window dressing produces price pressure tied to the calendar and to visibility: it can create
temporary pressure as reporting dates approach, above all in small stocks, fashionable stocks,
stocks with high mutual-fund ownership and stocks that are already the period's winners. The effect
can be stronger at the end of the year than at other quarter-ends, because clients' evaluation is
most salient then. Window dressing is often invoked to explain part of the turn-of-the-year effect
and some quarter-end price moves. Dimson (1988), Keim and Ziemba (2000), Singal (2003), Shefrin
(2008), Ilmanen (2011) and Carver (2015) present window dressing and tax-loss selling as major
explanations of the price distortions around the end of the year, above all in small stocks,
illiquid securities and recent losers; they also stress that neither can be said to suffice
everywhere: in some samples institutional constraints and year-end communication play the key role,
while in others individual investors appear to matter most. Window dressing does not naturally
explain every aggregate calendar effect.

The size of the effect depends on how often holdings are reported, on how much attention end
investors pay to them, on the reputational cost borne by the manager, and on whether the position
can be rebuilt after the reporting date. The practice persists as long as holdings are disclosed
with a delay, investors reward the appearance of the portfolio and monitoring is imperfect; it is
tied to real reporting, accounting and governance constraints. Arbitrageurs have to cope with
pressure that can be concentrated in short windows and in specific segments, where arbitrage is
costly, and, for pumping, with uncertainty beforehand about which stocks will be targeted, the
brevity of the window and execution costs. Its size is heterogeneous: it is not a universal and
continuous law of the market, but a recurring friction of governance, concentrated in the segments
where delegated management and the publication of holdings matter. It combines with the flows tied
to fund rankings, with benchmarking, and with the concentration of flows on winning funds. By
analogy, in crypto, the mechanism can exist for funds, treasuries or managers who publish snapshots
of their reserves.

## Prediction

Before month-ends and quarter-ends, and possibly more strongly before the year-end, managers can buy
recent winners and sell recent losers or stigmatised holdings, which puts temporary pressure on
prices, especially among small stocks, fashionable stocks, stocks with high mutual-fund ownership
and the period's winners; the pressure can build over several days or weeks before the reporting
date, and it fades after it. At quarter-ends, stocks already held by equity funds rise at the close
of the last trading day of the period, often followed by rapid reversals after the reporting date;
the rise is stronger in the market segments where the price impact of orders is high. The effects
are generally short-term but recurrent, and concentrated in the segments where delegated management
and holdings disclosure matter.

Studies of equity mutual funds document price increases at quarter-ends in stocks the funds already
hold, reversals afterwards, and a greater intensity in the market segments where the price impact of
orders is high. Other work suggests forms of pumping at the level of fund families, after regulators
tightened the rules on the practices of individual funds.

## What would refute it

- No abnormal buying of recent winners, or selling of recent losers, by funds in the days and weeks
  before month-ends, quarter-ends and the year-end, in holdings or trading data.
- No temporary price pressure before reporting dates on stocks with high mutual-fund ownership,
  small stocks or recent winners, or pressure that does not fade after them.
- No abnormal rise in the closing prices of fund-held stocks on the last trading day of the quarter,
  or quarter-end rises that persist, as a fundamental revaluation would, instead of reversing after
  the reporting date.
- Price pressure no more concentrated at month-ends, quarter-ends and year-ends than on other dates.
- Price moves before reporting dates as large in stocks with little delegated ownership as in stocks
  widely held by funds; quarter-end rises no larger in illiquid, high-impact stocks than in liquid
  ones.
- An effect whose size bears no relation to how often holdings are reported or to how much attention
  end investors pay to them; no link between the size of the quarter-end move and the incentives of
  the funds holding the stock, such as their ranking or the concentration of their portfolio.
- An effect that continues unchanged when holdings are disclosed without delay.
