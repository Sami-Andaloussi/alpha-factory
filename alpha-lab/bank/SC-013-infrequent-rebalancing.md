## Mechanism

The infrequent rebalancing hypothesis holds that investors who do not reallocate continuously, but at specific calendar horizons, generate predictable patterns of autocorrelation and seasonality in returns. If different investors rebalance their portfolios monthly, quarterly or at other frequencies, demand premia and price pressures are not uniform over time; they come back at regular intervals.

The mechanism is structural and close to models of slow-moving capital. Investors do not correct the deviations of their portfolios at once, because of monitoring costs, operational frictions, governance, inertia or internal rules. When the rebalancing date arrives, a batch of orders is released, which can produce return autocorrelation of variable sign and a rise in the cross-sectional dispersion of expected returns during the rebalancing period.

The hypothesis links several families of anomalies: seasonality, autocorrelation, calendar patterns in turnover, and institutional reallocation. It can explain why some effects recur even in sophisticated markets: not because they are unknown, but because persistent portfolio routines generate them. It is also conceptually close to month-end, quarter-end and benchmark-rebalancing phenomena.

## Prediction

Returns show autocorrelation and seasonality at the frequencies at which investors rebalance, monthly, quarterly or otherwise: demand premia and price pressures come back at these regular intervals, with autocorrelation whose sign can vary, and the cross-sectional dispersion of expected returns can rise during rebalancing periods. These effects can recur even once they are known, as long as the portfolio routines that generate them persist.

## What would refute it

- No recurring price pressure, autocorrelation or rise in cross-sectional dispersion at the calendar frequencies at which investors rebalance.
- Holdings and trading data showing no batching of orders at rebalancing dates, or return patterns unrelated to that batching.
- Seasonal patterns that disappear once known although the rebalancing routines behind them continue.
