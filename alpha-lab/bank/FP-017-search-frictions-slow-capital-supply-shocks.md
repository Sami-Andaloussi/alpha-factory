## Mechanism

In a market with search frictions, a counterparty is not found instantly. A seller under pressure
must sometimes accept a degraded price because no better buyer is available in the short run. Duffie
shows that a supply shock can make the price jump down and then recover gradually, at the pace at
which counterparties are found and capital is reallocated. An excess of supply today can therefore
create an observable discount even though fundamentals have not changed, and the discount takes
time to disappear. The same framework explains why "vulture" buyers demand a large discount to
provide liquidity during a shock.

The mechanism persists because arbitrage takes time, balance sheet, the sourcing of counterparties,
and sometimes an over-the-counter or bilateral infrastructure. It is therefore not confined to pure
over-the-counter markets; by analogy, it applies to small capitalisations, blocks that are hard to
cross, and fragmented crypto markets in which the true depth varies from one venue to another. The
size of the effect depends on the degree of fragmentation, on transparency, and on the cost of
searching for counterparties. It is a structural cousin of slow-moving capital and of fire sales,
with the emphasis on matching and negotiation rather than on leverage alone.

## Prediction

After a supply shock, prices can jump down below their fundamental value, even though fundamentals
have not changed, and then recover gradually, as counterparties are found and capital is
reallocated. The size of the effect depends on the fragmentation of the market, its transparency and
the cost of searching for counterparties: over-the-counter markets and, by analogy, small
capitalisations, blocks that are hard to cross, and fragmented crypto markets. Buyers who provide
liquidity during the shock demand large discounts.

## What would refute it

- Supply shocks followed by no price discount, or by an immediate and complete recovery, in
  fragmented and opaque markets.
- A speed of recovery unrelated to the cost of searching for counterparties or to the degree of
  fragmentation.
- Discounts after supply shocks as large in transparent, centralised markets as in over-the-counter
  or fragmented ones.
