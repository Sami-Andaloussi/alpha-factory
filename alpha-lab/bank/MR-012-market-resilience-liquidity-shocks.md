## Mechanism

Resilience is the capacity of a market to recover its normal spread and depth after a liquidity shock. After an event that consumes liquidity massively (a supply or demand shock, a very large execution), the spread widens and depth falls for a while. Value traders and opportunistic liquidity providers then step in, expecting a return to equilibrium, and their entry is a force of reversion: it brings the price, the spread and the depth back towards their normal levels. Harris (2002) describes value traders as the fundamental arbitrageurs who stabilise markets by buying when prices are too low and selling when they are too high; their activity is the concrete form that resilience takes. Market makers recovering from an inventory shock and high-frequency market makers restoring their quotes are also involved.

Resilience is weaker when (1) the market is less competitive, (2) fundamental uncertainty is high, so that value traders hesitate, and (3) market makers' balance-sheet constraints bind, as in liquidity crises. In systemic crises resilience can collapse into a liquidity spiral. The theory is related to inventory shocks, liquidity spirals, transitory price impact and order imbalances.

## Prediction

After a shock that consumes liquidity, spreads widen and depth drops; then price, spread and depth return towards their normal levels as value traders and liquidity providers enter. The speed of this recovery varies enormously with market conditions and the regime: it is faster in competitive markets and slower when fundamental uncertainty is high or dealers' balance sheets are constrained. In the 2007–2009 crisis resilience was very weak; in liquid futures contracts it is generally strong.

## What would refute it

- Price, spread and depth that do not recover after liquidity shocks.
- A recovery speed unrelated to competition among liquidity providers, to fundamental uncertainty or to dealers' balance-sheet constraints.
- Recovery as fast in crises as in calm periods.
- Price reversals after liquidity shocks that occur without any entry of value traders or liquidity providers.
