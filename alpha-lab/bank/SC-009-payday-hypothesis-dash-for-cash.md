## Mechanism

The payday hypothesis, or monthly payment cycle, explains the effects seen at the end and at the start of the month by the periodicity of liquidity needs and transfers, both in the real economy and in institutional asset management. The initial idea is that standardised payments (salaries, pensions, dividends, coupons, retirement contributions, insurance payouts and social benefits) are concentrated around the turn of the month. Depending on the agent, this creates either a cash surplus to invest or, on the contrary, a temporary cash need to finance.

There are two variants. Ogden's payday hypothesis stresses the injection of liquidity on the side of investors. The "Dash for Cash" version stresses rather the system-wide cash needs of institutions at the end of the month, which move relative prices and the cost of capital before they are absorbed. The two do not necessarily contradict each other; they describe different sides of a recurring cash cycle.

The mechanism is structural and potentially lasting, because it follows from the operating calendar of payments rather than from a passing belief. It can show up in equities, in bonds, in funding and in style or risk spreads, and it is all the more likely where markets are dominated by investors bound by accounting and liability calendars. Arbitrage is limited because prices have to adjust to liquidity needs that cannot be cancelled.

## Prediction

The end- and start-of-month effects follow the monthly payment cycle. Around the turn of the month, standardised payments inject liquidity on the side of investors, creating a cash surplus to invest for some agents and a temporary cash need to finance for others; at the end of the month, institutions' system-wide cash needs move relative prices and the cost of capital, before the move is absorbed. The pattern can show up in equities, in bonds, in funding costs and in style or risk spreads, and it is more likely where markets are dominated by investors bound by accounting and liability calendars.

## What would refute it

- No clustering of payments and cash transfers around the turn of the month, or no link between their timing and turn-of-the-month returns.
- No move in relative prices or in the cost of capital at the end of the month, when institutions need cash, or a move that is not absorbed afterwards.
- Turn-of-the-month returns as large in markets or periods where payment calendars are spread across the month as where they are concentrated.
- An effect no more present in markets dominated by investors bound by accounting and liability calendars than in other markets.
